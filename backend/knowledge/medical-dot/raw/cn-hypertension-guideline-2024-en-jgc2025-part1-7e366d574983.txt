In 1999, China released its first edition of the “Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of Hypertension”, which was subsequently updated in 2005, 2010, and 2018, establishing a diagnostic, assessment, grading, stratification, prevention, intervention, and management framework for hypertension tailored to the unique characteristics of the Chinese population. Over the past two decades, these guidelines have been widely implemented in clinical practice across China and have played a significant role in the management of hypertension and chronic diseases, the development of grassroots guidelines, the formulation of clinical pathways, and the implementation of health insurance policies.

The “Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of Hypertension in China (2024 Revision)” were developed based on the 2018 version, incorporating the latest research findings in the field of hypertension and related diseases both domestically and internationally. The committee responsible for this revision comprises multiple academic organizations and experts in the field of hypertension in China. The guidelines adhere to the principles of education and practicality, thoroughly analyzing recent clinical research evidence, and integrating pathophysiological insights with expert judgment to form this guiding document.

Hypertension is the most common cardiovascular disease, with multifactorial etiologies and multi-dimensional prevention and treatment strategies. These guidelines emphasize that the prevention and treatment of hypertension should be based on the principles of classification, staging, and typing.

In recent years, the prevalence of hypertension in China has continued to rise. In addition to traditional risk factors such as increased life expectancy and high salt intake, metabolic-related risk factors (e.g., overweight and obesity), and psychological factors have become increasingly important in the onset and progression of hypertension. With the development of society, technological advancements, and improved health insurance coverage, more appropriate technologies are now available for widespread use in medical institutions. The level of grassroots prevention and management has gradually improved, making it possible to conduct broader etiological screening, cardiovascular risk assessment, and detection of hypertension-mediated target organ damage. The diagnostic criteria for hypertension remain consistent with the 2018 version, particularly the retention of Grade 3 hypertension. This is due to the fact that, compared to regions with higher hypertension control rates, over 8% of hypertensive patients in China are classified as Grade 3, and the diagnostic and intervention strategies for this population differ significantly from those for Grade 1 and 2 hypertension. Several recent studies on intensive blood pressure reduction have further validated the recommendations on blood pressure control targets made in the 2018 version. Evidence for the use of new antihypertensive drugs continues to accumulate, and this guideline provides recommendations for the application of newly approved medications for hypertension treatment. Additionally, evidence supporting device-based treatments for hypertension is growing, and research on enhancing hypertension management through “Internet+” technologies is being widely conducted. Therefore, the guidelines have been updated to reflect these advancements.

1 CURRENT STATUS OF HYPERTENSION PREVALENCE AND CONTROL IN THE CHINESE POPULATION

Key Points

• The prevalence of hypertension in the Chinese population continues to rise. In recent years, the trend is particularly evident among young and middle-age individuals, as well as in rural areas.

• Awareness, treatment, and control rates of hypertension among Chinese patients have shown significant improvement, but overall remain at relatively low levels, with rates of 51.6%, 45.8%, and 16.8%, respectively. These rates are higher in women than in men, higher in urban residents compared to rural residents, and lower in young and middle-aged individuals.

• High sodium and low potassium intake, overweight and obesity, smoking, excessive alcohol consumption, and psychosocial factors are significant risk factors for hypertension in the Chinese population.

• The Chinese government and professional organizations have implemented a series of hypertension prevention and control programs aimed at further improving the management of hypertension.

Compared to the five previous nationwide hypertension surveys conducted in 1958-1959, 1979-1980, 1991, 2002, and 2012-2015,[2] although the total number of participants, age distribution, and diagnostic criteria were not entirely consistent across these surveys, the overall trend shows an increase in prevalence (Table 3).

Research on hypertension incidence is relatively limited. However, follow-up data from the China Health and Nutrition Survey indicate that the age-standardized incidence of hypertension among Chinese adults increased from 40.8 per 1,000 person-years in 1993-1997 to 48.6 per 1,000 person-years in 2011-2015, with incidence rates being higher in men than in women, and higher in the eastern, central, and northeastern regions compared to the western regions.[6]

The awareness, treatment, and control rates of hypertension are crucial indicators reflecting the status of hypertension prevention and management. The 2012-2015 survey indicated that among individuals aged 18 years and above, the awareness, treatment, and control rates of hypertension were 51.6%, 45.8%, and 16.8%, respectively, showing a significant increase compared to the rates in 1991 and 2002 (Table 4). Multiple nationwide surveys consistently demonstrated that these three rates are higher in women than in men, higher in urban residents compared to rural residents, and higher in Han Chinese than in ethnic minorities.[2] Compared to elderly hypertensive patients, young and middle-aged individuals exhibited significantly lower rates in all three categories.

1.3 Major Risk Factors for Hypertension in the Chinese Population

Hypertension risk factors in the Chinese population encompass genetic predispositions, age, body weight, gender, and various unhealthy lifestyle behaviors.[3] The presence of these risk factors is widespread among the population, and as the number and severity of these factors accumulate, there is a corresponding increase in blood pressure levels and the risk of developing hypertension.

1.3.1 High sodium and low potassium diets

High sodium and low potassium intake are significant contributors to the development of hypertension. There is a positive correlation between sodium intake and blood pressure levels.[7] Specifically, for every 1 g increase in 24-h urinary sodium excretion, systolic blood pressure increases by 2.11 mmHg, and diastolic blood pressure by 0.78 mmHg. Additionally, approximately one-third of Chinese adults are sensitive to sodium.[8] The INTERSALT study (International Study of Salt and Blood Pressure) confirmed that potassium intake also plays a crucial role in influencing blood pressure across different populations. Research conducted among hypertensive individuals in China indicated that each unit increase in the 24-h urinary sodium-to-potassium ratio results in a rise in systolic/diastolic blood pressure by 0.46/0.24 mmHg.[9]

Data from 2015–2017 show that the average daily salt intake among Chinese adults was 9.3 g, but only 23.3% of the population met the recommended intake of less than 5 g per day.[10]

1.3.2 Overweight and obesity

Overweight and obesity are strongly associated with higher hypertension prevalence.[11] An increase of 5 kg/m² in body mass index (BMI) is associated with a 49% increase in the risk of developing hypertension.[12] Moreover, weight changes in middle-aged and elderly individuals with baseline overweight or obesity have a more pronounced impact on blood pressure, with a 10% change in body weight leading to an increase of 4.94 mmHg in systolic blood pressure and 2.50 mmHg in diastolic blood pressure.[13]

In recent years, the issue of overweight and obesity among Chinese residents has become increasingly prominent, with a prevalence of abdominal obesity reaching 29.1%, and significant variations observed across different provinces.[14] According to the China Health and Nutrition Survey, the overweight and obesity rates among urban and rural residents of all age groups have been steadily increasing. In 2018, the average BMI of Chinese adults was 24.5 kg/m², an increase of 3 kg/m² compared to 1991.

1.3.3 Aging

Aging is a critical factor contributing to the development of hypertension. The fifth national hypertension survey in China revealed that hypertension prevalence increases significantly with age, with more than 55% of individuals aged 65 and older suffering from hypertension, characterized mainly by elevated systolic blood pressure and decreased diastolic blood pressure. Controlling blood pressure in the elderly not only reduces the risk of major cardiovascular events but also helps protect cognitive function and prevent Alzheimer’s disease. China currently has the largest elderly population globally and is one of the fastest-aging countries. By the end of 2019, approximately 176 million people in China were aged 65 and above, and it is projected that by 2050, the elderly will account for 30% of the total population.[15]

1.3.4 Smoking

Smoking is a well-established independent risk factor for cardiovascular diseases and mortality. It is closely linked to an increased risk of hypertension, coronary heart disease, stroke, and other cardiovascular conditions, as well as higher mortality rates from these diseases. The risk of cardiovascular disease and mortality increases with the amount and duration of smoking.[16-18] Exposure to secondhand smoke also increases the risk of coronary heart disease and stroke.[19] According to the “China Smoking and Health Report 2020”, tobacco use results in over one million deaths annually in China, a number expected to rise to two million by 2030.

1.3.5 Excessive Alcohol Consumption

Long-term or occasional heavy drinking can severely impact health. A five-year follow-up study of 12,497 adults found that,[20] after adjusting for other risk factors, the risk of developing hypertension was 1.24 times higher in male drinkers and 1.41 times higher in female drinkers compared to non-drinkers. Additionally, as the frequency of drinking increases, so does the risk of hypertension.[21] Men who drink ≤ 2 times per week and > 2 times per week have a sequentially higher risk of hypertension, at 1.51 and 2.13 times greater, respectively, compared to non-drinkers.

1.3.6 Psychosocial factors

Rapid socioeconomic development, faster paces of life, and increased social competition have led to rising psychosocial stress. Hypertensive patients are more likely to experience mental and psychological issues, with a prevalence 2.69 times that of non-hypertensive individuals.[22] A meta-analysis including 59 cross-sectional and prospective studies involving over 4 million participants found that patients with anxiety disorders had a higher risk of developing hypertension, 1.37 times in cross-sectional studies and 1.40 times in prospective studies, compared to those without anxiety disorders.[23] A study from China indicated that there is a close relationship between stress and hypertension.[24]

1.3.7 Air pollution

The World Health Organization has identified air pollution as one of the largest environmental health threats globally. Studies across 28 provinces in China from 2011 to 2012,[25] as well as the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study,[26] suggest that air pollution significantly increases the risk of hypertension. Both indoor and outdoor air pollution are major concerns, with household use of solid fuels and smoking contributing to indoor pollution and increased hypertension risk.[27]

1.3.8 Cancer treatment

Cardiovascular diseases resulting from cancer treatment have become the second leading cause of death among cancer survivors. Compared to the general population, cancer patients and survivors have a higher prevalence of hypertension.[28] Chemotherapy is an independent risk factor for hypertension. A meta-analysis has shown that tyrosine kinase inhibitor treatment significantly increases the burden of hypertension compared to standard chemotherapy (with a relative risk of 3.78).[29] It has been reported that over half of the patients receiving anti-angiogenic therapy targeting vascular endothelial growth factor have hypertension.

1.3.9 High altitude

More than 290 million people in China live at altitudes exceeding 500 m, with about 1.25 million residing above 2500 m.[31] Low atmospheric pressure (hypoxia), cold temperatures, and significant temperature variations are typical climatic features of high-altitude areas that affect blood pressure. Acute exposure to high altitude typically results in increased blood pressure.[32] Long-term residents of high-altitude areas, especially those living above 750 m, show progressively higher average blood pressures as altitude increases.[33] Research from the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau indicates that residents in high-altitude areas above 2000 m have a hypertension prevalence of 36.3%, compared to 19.6% in lower-altitude areas below 1000 m.[34] In Tibetan residents living above 3000 m, every 100 m increase in altitude raises the hypertension prevalence by 2%.[35]

1.3.10 Other Risk Factors

In addition to the afore-mentioned risk factors, other contributors to hypertension include a family history of hypertension, lack of physical activity, and low educational levels. Rapid urbanization and expansion of urban areas have also had adverse effects on public health levels. Urban fragmentation, lack of access to healthy food, high population density, environments unsuitable for walking, and insufficient park or green space areas all increase the risk of developing hypertension.[36,37]

1.4 Hypertension Prevention and Control Programs and Projects in China

In recent years, China has implemented several national initiatives such as the “National Basic Public Health Services”, “Healthy China Action”, “Medium and Long-term Plan for Chronic Disease Prevention and Control” and “National Health and Lifestyle Action” to actively promote hypertension prevention and control at the national level.

National Basic Public Health Services incorporates hypertension into chronic disease patient health management. The services are targeted at primary hypertension patients aged 35 and above within the jurisdiction. The services include hypertension screening, follow-up assessments, categorized interventions, and health check-ups.

The “Healthy China 2030” Planning Outline sets specific targets for hypertension management: by 2030, to achieve an awareness rate of hypertension among residents aged ≥ 30 years of not less than 65%, a standardized management rate of hypertension patients of not less than 70%, and continuous improvements in the treatment and control rates of hypertension.

The “Medium and Long-term Plan for Chronic Disease Prevention and Control in China (2017-2025)” sets major targets to manage 110 million hypertension patients by 2025, aiming to achieve a standardized management rate of 70%.

The “National Health and Lifestyle Action Plan (2017-2025)” implements initiatives like “Three Reductions, Three Healths” (reduction of salt, oil, sugar intake, and promotion of healthy oral hygiene, healthy weight, and healthy bones), encourages moderate physical activity, controls smoking and alcohol consumption, and promotes mental health. Efforts are also made to explore new models of action tailored to local conditions,[38] enhancing the role of risk factor control in hypertension management.

Comprehensive implementation of initial blood pressure testing for individuals aged 35 and above helps in early identification of hypertension patients and high-risk groups, providing timely intervention guidance to reduce the risk of disease onset in high-risk groups. Standards for hypertension health management have been established to explore pilot demonstrations of chronic disease health management institutions using hypertension as an entry point,[39] and to build comprehensive chronic disease prevention and control models suited to different regional characteristics.

In 2019, China initiated pilot work on centralized drug procurement and use. The implementation of centralized drug tendering and procurement policies has led to a general decrease in the price and daily costs of antihypertensive drugs,[40] substantially reducing the burden of medication costs on patients and helping to improve control rates.

The WHO-HEARTS initiative integrates core elements of chronic disease control into the primary healthcare system,[41] offering models that can be adapted even in resource-poor areas for the management of hypertension. The HEARTS in China project was launched as a pilot in Henan Province in July 2018. As of June 2022, the project has screened over 700,000 adults aged 18 and older in routine outpatient clinics, registering more than 320,000 hypertensive patients, achieving a treatment rate of 71.9% and a control rate of 39.7%. The capacity of primary care physicians to implement standardized treatment pathways has continually improved, demonstrating that the HEARTS toolkit is a scalable, sustainable, and effective strategy for hypertension prevention and control.

May Measurement Month (MMM) is an initiative started by the International Society of Hypertension and the World Hypertension League. Since 2017, the Chinese Hypertension League has conducted the MMM project nationwide for six consecutive years, completing blood pressure measurements for nearly 1.6 million adults. Between 2017 and 2018, the awareness, treatment, and control rates of hypertension increased from 60.1%, 42.5%, and 25.4%[42] respectively to 62.3%, 57.3%, and 35.9%.[43] Data analysis from the MMM project has identified modifiable factors affecting blood pressure or hypertension control, such as obesity, unhealthy lifestyle habits, and a low proportion of combination medication use, providing evidence to support hypertension prevention and control measures.

2 HYPERTENSION AND CARDIOVASCULAR RISK

Key Points

• There is a continuous, independent, and directpositive correlation between office blood pressurelevels and cardiovascular risk.

• The association between 24-h ambulatory bloodpressure and nighttime blood pressure with cardiovascular risk is even stronger. Home blood pressure, particularly morning blood pressure, is closelyrelated to patient prognosis.

• Stroke remains the most common complicationamong hypertensive individuals in China, andthere is a noticeable increase in coronary heart disease events.

• Other complications caused by hypertension include atrial fibrillation, heart failure, end-stage renal disease (ESRD), and dementia.

Elevated blood pressure can cause structural and functional changes in the arterial vascular system and the organs it supplies, leading to target organ damage and eventually cardiovascular events, resulting in organ failure in the heart, kidneys, and other organs.

2.1 The Relationship Between Blood Pressure and Cardiovascular Risk
2.1.1 Office blood pressure and cardiovascular risk

There is a close causal relationship between office blood pressure levels and the risk of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality. In a prospective observational study involving 61 populations worldwide (approximately 1 million individuals aged 40-89 years) with an average follow-up of 12 years,[44] it was found that baseline office blood pressure ranging from 115/75 mmHg to 185/115 mmHg showed a continuous, independent, and direct positive correlation with the risks of stroke, coronary heart disease events, and cardiovascular mortality. For every 20 mmHg increase in systolic blood pressure or 10 mmHg increase in diastolic blood pressure, the risk of cardiovascular events doubled.

In the Asia-Pacific cohort study, which included 13 populations from China,[45] office blood pressure levels were strongly associated with stroke and coronary heart disease events. The relationship between elevated blood pressure and stroke and coronary heart disease events was found to be stronger in Asian populations compared to populations in Australia and New Zealand. For every 10 mmHg increase in systolic blood pressure, the risk of stroke and fatal myocardial infarction increased by 53% and 31% respectively in the Asian population, while the increases were 24% and 21% respectively in the Australian and New Zealand populations.

In recent years, there has been a surge in the number of young and middle-aged individuals with hypertension, often characterized by elevated diastolic blood pressure.[46] The Kailuan study in China, which analyzed 87,346 participants with an average age of 50.9 years, found that isolated diastolic hypertension (IDH) was associated with an increased risk of hemorrhagic stroke and overall cardiovascular risk compared to normal blood pressure.[47] A meta-analysis that included 15 cohort studies with 489,814 participants found that IDH significantly increased the risks of stroke, cardiovascular mortality, and other adverse outcomes compared to normal blood pressure.[48]

Additionally, international multicenter study[49] and cohort studies in our country[50-51] had shown that pulse pressure was closely associated with various adverse outcomes such as cardiovascular disease mortality, myocardial infarction, and stroke, and was independent of mean arterial pressure. The studies also found that there was a J-shaped curve relationship between pulse pressure and the risk of cardiovascular disease mortality and event occurrence; both excessively high and low pulse pressures increase the risk of adverse outcomes.[49-50]

There is a causal relationship between blood pressure levels and the occurrence of heart failure. Clinical follow-up data indicate that as blood pressure levels increase, the incidence of heart failure also increases,[52] with heart failure and stroke being the two complications most closely associated with blood pressure levels. Long-term hypertension-left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH)-heart failure constitutes an important chain of events. Hypertension mainly leads to heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF); if combined with coronary heart disease and myocardial infarction, heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) can also occur.

Hypertension is an important cause of atrial fibrillation.[53] Hypertension-atrial fibrillation-cerebral embolism constitutes an important yet often overlooked chain of events. As blood pressure load (based on blood pressure levels and the exposure to elevated blood pressure during the follow-up period) increases, the incidence of atrial fibrillation gradually rises.[54]

In addition to cardiovascular risks, hypertension is also a significant risk factor for chronic kidney disease (CKD). Long-term clinical cohort follow-up had found that with the increase in office blood pressure levels,[55] the incidence of end-stage renal disease (ESRD) also significantly increases. The incidence of ESRD in patients with severe hypertension is more than 11 times that of those with normal blood pressure, and even at the high-normal blood pressure level, it is 1.9 times higher.

Elevated blood pressure is also closely related to dementia. A meta-analysis shows that individuals with systolic blood pressure of 140 mmHg or higher or diastolic blood pressure of 80 mmHg or higher in middle age have a 37% increased risk of dementia later in life; moreover, those with systolic blood pressure above 130 mmHg in middle age also have a 34% increased risk of cognitive impairment compared to those with systolic blood pressure of 130 mmHg or lower.[56] Analysis of pooled cohort data at the individual level also found that higher cumulative systolic blood pressure levels in middle and early adulthood were associated with later cognitive decline,[57] suggesting that early blood pressure control in middle-aged and younger populations is particularly important for preventing later disease risks.

2.1.2 Out-of-office blood pressure and cardiovascular risk

Twenty-four-hour ambulatory blood pressure and nighttime blood pressure are even more closely associated with cardiovascular disease risk than office blood pressure. For every 20 mmHg increase in office systolic blood pressure, 24-h systolic blood pressure, and nighttime systolic blood pressure, the risk of cardiovascular events increases by 20%, 45%, and 36%, respectively. When 24-h blood pressure is added to the office blood pressure measurement, the predictive ability for cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality improves significantly.[58] Elevated 24-h systolic blood pressure[59] and increased pulse pressure[60] are associated with adverse outcomes in individuals aged 50 to 60 and older. In contrast, increased 24-h diastolic blood pressure and isolated diastolic hypertension (IDH)[59,61] are related to an increased risk of cardiovascular events in middle-aged and younger individuals under the age of 50.[62]

Home blood pressure monitoring, especially home morning blood pressure, is closely related to patient prognosis. Among untreated patients with normal office blood pressure, every 10 mmHg increase in home systolic blood pressure raises the risk of composite cardiovascular events by 22% to 28%.[59] In patients with controlled office systolic blood pressure below 130 mmHg after antihypertensive treatment, those with morning home systolic blood pressure ≥ 145 mmHg have a 1.5 times higher risk of cardiovascular events than those with morning home systolic blood pressure < 125 mmHg.[60]

2.1.3 Central arterial pressure and cardiovascular risk

Central arterial pressure is as strongly associated with cardiovascular risk as brachial arterial pressure.[63] In patients with central arterial hypertension, the risk of cardiovascular events is approximately double that of patients with normal peripheral and central arterial pressures, regardless of whether brachial arterial pressure is elevated.[64] Currently, interventional studies are underway to explore whether antihypertensive treatment can reduce the risk of cardiovascular events in patients with isolated central arterial hypertension who have controlled brachial arterial pressure.

2.1.4 Blood pressure variability and cardiovascular risk

Blood pressure variability (BPV), which reflects the degree of fluctuation in blood pressure levels, is associated with cardiovascular risk. Short-term and 24-h blood pressure variability have an inverse relationship with cardiovascular risk, independent of average 24-h blood pressure levels.[65] The Kailuan study in China showed that participants in the highest quintile of systolic BP variability had a 37% higher risk of all-cause mortality and an 18% higher risk of cardiovascular events compared to those in the lowest quintile.[61]

2.2 Clustering of Multiple Risk Factors in Hypertension and Cardiovascular Risk

Hypertensive patients often have multiple coexisting risk factors, which significantly increase cardiovascular risk. Cohort studies in China have shown that when hypertension is combined with cardiovascular metabolic risk factors such as abnormal glucose metabolism, abdominal obesity, and dyslipidemia, the risk of cardiovascular disease is 2.2 times higher than in patients with hypertension alone.[66] On the other hand, hypertensive patients who maintain a non-smoking status, regular physical activity, a healthy diet, and normal levels of body mass index, total cholesterol, and fasting blood glucose have a lower cardiovascular risk. Patients with four or more of these indicators in the normal range have a 35% lower risk of developing cardiovascular disease compared to those with one or fewer normal indicators.[67]

Hypertension is closely associated with chronic kidney disease (CKD), with the two conditions mutually exacerbating each other and further increasing cardiovascular risk. Research in China has shown that in hypertensive patients with CKD, uncontrolled hypertension significantly increases the risk of left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) and adverse renal outcomes.[68] Furthermore, a meta-analysis of 24 cohorts demonstrated that estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) can enhance the prediction of cardiovascular disease beyond traditional risk factors, with this predictive power being more pronounced in hypertensive patients.[69] These findings highlight the importance of comprehensive management of cardiovascular risk factors to reduce cardiovascular risk in hypertensive patients.

2.3 Characteristics of Cardiovascular Risk in Hypertensive Populations in China

Cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases are the leading causes of death in China, accounting for more than 45% of total mortality.[70] In the Chinese population, the association between hypertension and stroke is significantly stronger than that between hypertension and coronary heart disease. Cohort studies have shown that individuals with systolic/diastolic blood pressure levels of ≥ 160/100 mmHg have a 4.77 times higher risk of stroke and a 2.27 times higher risk of coronary heart disease compared to those with blood pressure levels of < 120/80 mmHg.[71]

Over the past 30 years, the incidence and mortality rates of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), such as coronary heart disease and ischemic stroke, have continued to rise in the Chinese population. In contrast, the mortality rate from hemorrhagic stroke has shown a significant decline since 2000, reflecting initial success in hypertension prevention and control in China.[72] However, stroke remains the most prevalent type of cardiovascular disease and the leading cause of death in the Chinese population, making stroke prevention a primary goal in hypertension control.[73] Additionally, the rising prevalence of atrial fibrillation and heart failure in China is also closely linked to hypertension.[68] Controlling blood pressure is crucial for reducing overall cardiovascular risk.

3 BLOOD PRESSURE MEASUREMENT

Key Points

• Resting Blood Pressure Measurement Steps:

° Measure blood pressure in the seated positionafter at least 5 min of rest, with the armpositionedat heart level.

° It is recommended to use an upper-armelectronic blood pressure monitor that has been validatedfor accuracy (Class I, Level C evidence). The use ofmercury sphygmomanometers is not recommended (Class III, Level C evidence).

° Use a standard cuff size; for individuals with anarm circumference greater than 32 cm, use a largercuff, and for those with an arm circumference lessthan 24 cm, use a smaller cuff (Class I, Level C evidence).

° At the first visit, measure blood pressure in botharms and use the arm with the higher reading forsubsequent measurements (Class I, Level B evidence).

° When measuring blood pressure, repeat themeasurement at 30-60 s intervals and record theaverage of the two readings. If the systolicor diastolic blood pressure readingsdiffer by morethan 10 mmHg, take a third measurement and record the average of the three readings (Class I,Level C evidence).[74]

° Elderly individuals, diabetic patients, or thosewith orthostatic hypotension should have theirstanding blood pressure measured additionally.The standing blood pressure should be measured 1min and 3 min after changing from a supine or sitting position to a standing position

° Concurrentlywith bloodpressure measurement,pulse rate should be determined (Class I, Level Bevidence).

• Various Blood Pressure Measurement Methods Evaluation:

° Office blood pressure measurements are commonly used for diagnosing hypertension, gradingblood pressure levels, and observing the effects ofantihypertensive therapy (Class I, Level A evidence).

° Out-of-office blood pressure measurementsshould be conducted whenever possible to confirmhypertension, identify white-coat hypertension andmasked hypertension, assess the effectiveness of antihypertensive therapy, and diagnose resistant hypertension (Class I, Level C evidence).

° Ambulatory blood pressure monitoring can alsoassess the circadian rhythm of blood pressure, determine nocturnal hypertension, and identify clinical phenotypes such as morning hypertension.

° Internet-based remote real-time home bloodpressure monitoring is a new model for blood pressure management.

Blood pressure measurement is a fundamental method for evaluating blood pressure levels, diagnosing hypertension, and monitoring the effectiveness of antihypertensive treatment.[75] In clinical practice and public health efforts, office blood pressure measurement and out-of-office blood pressure measurement are primarily used. The former includes routine office blood pressure measurements taken by healthcare professionals and automated office blood pressure measurements (AOBP) performed by patients themselves, both of which must be conducted under standardized conditions according to uniform protocols. The latter includes ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM) and home blood pressure monitoring (HBPM), which provide extensive blood pressure data outside the medical setting. These out-of-office measurements are more strongly associated with target organ damage and offer better cardiovascular risk prediction than office blood pressure measurements.

Whether using office or out-of-office blood pressure measurements, it is recommended to use upper-arm electronic blood pressure monitors that have been validated through international standard protocols (ISO 81060, European Society of Hypertension, British Hypertension Society, or the Association for the Advancement of Medical Instrumentation). The list of validated blood pressure monitors can be found on the website http://www.stridebp.org.

3.1 Office Blood Pressure Measurement
3.1.1 Office Blood Pressure Measurement

Office blood pressure measurement is conducted by healthcare professionals under standardized conditions and following uniform protocols. It is the most commonly used method for diagnosing hypertension, grading blood pressure levels, and assessing the efficacy of antihypertensive treatment.

The use of mercury sphygmomanometers is not recommended. If a mercury sphygmomanometer or a similar device (e.g., an aneroid sphygmomanometer or a simulated mercury column sphygmomanometer) is still being used, and auscultation of Korotkoff sounds with a stethoscope is required, the specific measurement method should follow the steps outlined in Key Point 3A. The cuff should be rapidly inflated to a pressure 30 mmHg higher than the point at which the radial pulse disappears, then deflated slowly at a constant rate of 2 mmHg per second. For individuals with a slow heart rate, the deflation rate should be even slower. After recording the diastolic blood pressure reading, quickly release the remaining pressure to zero. During deflation, listen carefully to the Korotkoff sounds and observe the vertical height of the mercury column at the first phase (appearance of the sound) and the fifth phase (disappearance of the sound) of the Korotkoff sounds. The systolic blood pressure is recorded at the first phase of the Korotkoff sound, and the diastolic blood pressure is recorded at the fifth phase. For children under 12 years of age, pregnant women, patients with severe anemia, hyperthyroidism, aortic regurgitation, or where the Korotkoff sound does not disappear, the fourth phase of the Korotkoff sound (muffling) should be used as the diastolic blood pressure reading. When reading blood pressure values, the last digit should only be 0, 2, 4, 6, or 8; digits like 1, 3, 5, 7, or 9 should not be used, and care should be taken to avoid end-digit preference. When measuring blood pressure in patients with atrial fibrillation, a longer interval between Korotkoff sounds may occur, requiring multiple measurements to obtain an average value.

3.1.2 Automated office blood pressure measurement (AOBP)

Office blood pressure measurement is a primary method in the diagnosis and management of hypertension. However, standard office blood pressure measurement can be difficult to standardize and is often subject to a significant white-coat effect. To address this, countries such as Canada have developed the AOBP method,[76] where the entire blood pressure measurement process is carried out by the patient using an upper-arm electronic blood pressure monitor in a relatively independent space. This process includes resting in a seated position for 5 min, followed by two measurements with an interval of 30-60 s between them. If the two measurements differ significantly, a third measurement is taken, and the average of the two or three measurements is recorded. Since no healthcare personnel are present during the measurement, the white-coat effect is significantly reduced. This method has been widely adopted in clinical practice in North America and was used to assess patients’ blood pressure in the Systolic Blood Pressure Intervention Trial (SPRINT).[77] Studies have shown that automated office blood pressure measurements are significantly lower than conventional office blood pressure measurements.[78] Consequently, some scholars believe that these measurements may not align with the diagnostic assessment standards currently used for office blood pressure, and further clinical research is warranted.

China has a large number of hypertensive patients, and the conditions for diagnosis and treatment vary widely. Many patients have very short consultation times, making it difficult to perform standardized office blood pressure measurements, which severely affects the quality of hypertension diagnosis and assessment. Establishing an AOBP (Automated Office Blood Pressure) system can effectively solve the issue of standardizing office blood pressure measurements, and it is necessary to promote its application nationwide.[79]

3.2 Out-of-Office Blood Pressure Measurement
3.2.1 Ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM)

ABPM can assess an individual’s blood pressure levels during daily life activities, including periods of light physical activity during the day and sleep at night.[80] ABPM is performed using an automatic electronic blood pressure monitor, which can effectively reduce measurement bias and the white-coat effect. ABPM significantly enhances the accuracy of blood pressure measurement, helping to identify white-coat hypertension (elevated office blood pressure with normal ambulatory blood pressure) and masked hypertension (normal office blood pressure with elevated ambulatory blood pressure). ABPM includes blood pressure measurements during both daytime and nighttime, allowing for the calculation of average blood pressure for these periods. This enables the diagnosis of hypertension during specific time periods, such as isolated nighttime hypertension.[81-84] It also allows for the evaluation of circadian blood pressure rhythms, including the drop in blood pressure from day to night and the rise in blood pressure from night to morning upon waking. ABPM can also assess blood pressure variability between readings, providing a more accurate and comprehensive assessment of blood pressure risk. Additionally, ABPM can be used to evaluate arterial elasticity and salt sensitivity, among other factors.[85,86]

The specific methods for ABPM can be found in the “Chinese Guidelines for Ambulatory Blood Pressure Monitoring”.[80] In recent years, the use of remote dynamic data analysis and reporting platforms has standardized ABPM analysis and reporting, further promoting its clinical application, including in community health service centers.[87,88]

3.2.2 Home Blood Pressure Monitoring (HBPM)

HBPM is conducted by patients themselves in a relatively familiar home environment, which can also reduce the white-coat effect to some extent and is useful for detecting white-coat hypertension or masked hypertension. Compared to ABPM, the significant advantage of HBPM is that it can be performed over the long term. The vast majority of hypertensive patients can manage and control their hypertension through HBPM. HBPM can significantly improve hypertension control rates and patient outcomes.[89] For individuals with normal blood pressure, HBPM can help detect hypertension early, thereby increasing awareness of hypertension.[90,91]

In recent years, with the widespread use of electronic blood pressure monitors, HBPM has played an increasingly important role in hypertension management and control in China. In more developed eastern regions, most hypertensive patients use HBPM; however, its use needs to be further improved in central and western regions. HBPM also requires standardization in data analysis and reporting, necessitating the reliance on remote platforms for data analysis and reporting. Based on the lifestyle habits of Chinese residents, it is recommended that HBPM be conducted for 5 to 7 days, with 2 to 3 readings taken each morning and evening. Morning blood pressure should be measured in a seated position after emptying the bladder, before taking medication, and before breakfast. Evening blood pressure should be measured after dinner, after washing up, and before going to bed. The average of no fewer than 12 readings over at least 3 days should be used for diagnosing hypertension or evaluating treatment efficacy.[92,93]

3.3 Blood Pressure Kiosk Measurement

Blood pressure kiosks provide a unique and independent mode of blood pressure measurement, distinct from traditional office or home blood pressure measurements.[94] These kiosks are primarily used for screening hypertension and identifying cases of uncontrolled hypertension. Individuals with elevated blood pressure detected through kiosk measurements should undergo standardized office blood pressure measurement, ABPM, or HBPM for further evaluation to confirm the diagnosis of hypertension or to assess the efficacy of antihypertensive treatment. With the widespread use of electronic blood pressure monitors, many countries and regions have installed blood pressure kiosks in public spaces, offering convenient access to blood pressure monitoring. In China, significant progress has been made in the construction of such facilities, particularly in the more developed eastern regions, where many community health service centers have established blood pressure kiosks.

With the rapid advancement of wireless communication and big data technologies, blood pressure kiosk measurements not only allow residents to monitor their blood pressure but also provide professional interpretation of the results and corresponding health advice.[88] For individuals with longer-term blood pressure measurement data, it is possible to assess blood pressure control over a specific period, including blood pressure variability, thereby further improving hypertension control rates and reducing the risk of cardiovascular events.

3.4 Wearable Blood Pressure Measurement

In recent years, the industry has developed wearable wrist-type electronic blood pressure monitors by incorporating micro-pump and micro-cuff technologies into traditional wrist-type electronic blood pressure monitors.[95] Although these devices still use oscillometric measurement technology that requires inflation and deflation, the noise level during inflation has been significantly reduced, and the comfort of inflation and deflation has been notably improved due to the narrower cuff. Wearable blood pressure measurement somewhat overcomes the environmental limitations of traditional blood pressure measurements, significantly increasing the frequency of blood pressure measurements. Unlike ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM), these devices cannot yet accurately measure blood pressure during sleep. Moreover, many measurements may be conducted outside the home environment, differing from traditional home blood pressure monitoring (HBPM). Therefore, wearable blood pressure measurement represents a novel approach, and its use for hypertension diagnosis and treatment evaluation still requires extensive applied research to establish normal values and grading criteria for blood pressure.

Wearable electronic blood pressure monitors must also undergo accuracy validation according to standardized protocols. Only those devices that have passed accuracy validation should be used for blood pressure measurement. As of now, several wrist-worn electronic blood pressure monitors using oscillometric measurement technology have passed accuracy validation.[96,97] One domestic wristwatch has been certified to meet the standards of the Association for the Advancement of Medical Instrumentation, the European Society of Hypertension, and the International Organization for Standardization,[97] making it suitable for daily blood pressure monitoring. Blood pressure measurement using cuffless technologies such as pulse wave transit time still requires further scientific research.[98,99]

3.5 Central Arterial Blood Pressure Measurement

As blood flows from the elastic, large-diameter aorta to the smaller-diameter muscular arteries in the periphery, systolic pressure increases significantly, resulting in typical pulse pressure amplification, which forms the theoretical basis for central arterial pressure detection.[100] Central arterial pressure represents the perfusion pressure of major organs and is therefore likely to be more closely associated with target organ damage.[101] As the elastic function of the aorta declines, the central arterial systolic pressure increases, which can manifest as central arterial hypertension. Recent studies have shown that isolated central arterial hypertension carries a cardiovascular event risk similar to that of combined central and peripheral arterial hypertension.[102,103]

The technology for detecting central arterial blood pressure has matured and become automated. In addition to the commonly used pressure waveform analysis technology, recent advances in oscillometric blood pressure measurement technology have enabled dynamic blood pressure monitors to simultaneously assess central arterial blood pressure, paving the way for the clinical application of central arterial blood pressure monitoring.[104]

3.6 Four-Limb Blood Pressure Measurement

Simultaneous four-limb blood pressure measurement greatly improves the accuracy of comparing blood pressure between different limbs, allowing for the calculation of the ankle-brachial index (ABI) and the differences in blood pressure between both upper or both lower limbs. This technique can assess occlusive diseases of the aorta, lower limb arteries, and subclavian arteries. Typically, lower limb blood pressure is higher than upper limb blood pressure. If the ABI is ≤ 0.9 or the blood pressure difference between both upper limbs or both lower limbs exceeds 15 mmHg, peripheral vascular disease should be considered.[105]

Simultaneous four-limb blood pressure measurement has been practiced in China for some time and has played an important role in evaluating peripheral vascular disease, though it still needs to be more widely adopted. Among older adults over 65 years with a high risk of peripheral vascular disease, regular four-limb blood pressure measurement is recommended to promptly detect and diagnose peripheral vascular disease and avoid blood pressure assessment errors due to significant differences in blood pressure between both upper limbs.[106]

3.7 Blood Pressure Measurement in Hypertension Screening

To increase awareness of hypertension, various forms of hypertension screening activities have been conducted globally in recent years.[107] Blood pressure measurements during these screenings are not office measurements but rather a unique form of measurement. Although the measurement techniques are not particularly special, the measurement environments can vary greatly. Screening blood pressure measurements should be conducted in a manner similar to office blood pressure measurements, with a 5-min rest before measurement and 2 to 3 measurements taken at 30 to 60 s intervals. Due to the specific conditions of the screening environment, these measurements are generally only suitable for preliminary hypertension screening. Standardized office blood pressure measurement, ABPM, or HBPM is needed for the confirmation of hypertension.

4 DIAGNOSTIC EVALUATION

The purpose of diagnostic evaluation is to differentiate the causes of hypertension, assess the patient’s cardiovascular and cerebrovascular risk, and guide diagnosis and treatment. The content of the diagnostic evaluation includes the following three aspects: (1) establishing the diagnosis of hypertension and determining the grade of blood pressure level; (2) identifying the causes of hypertension and distinguishing between primary and secondary hypertension; (3) identifying other cardiovascular risk factors, assessing target organ damage, and determining the clinical conditions related to cardiovascular diseases, cerebrovascular diseases, or kidney diseases. 4.1 Medical History

A comprehensive and detailed understanding of the patient’s medical history should be obtained, including the following: (1) duration of illness: record the time, place, and highest level of blood pressure first discovered or diagnosed. If the patient has received antihypertensive treatment, specify the types, doses, efficacy, and any adverse reactions of the antihypertensive drugs used in the past and present.

(2) Symptoms: inquire about any current or past symptoms related to hypertension, such as chest pain, shortness of breath, palpitations, claudication, peripheral edema, headache, blurred vision, nocturia, hematuria, dizziness, etc.

(3) Past medical history: ask whether the patient currently has or previously had a stroke, transient ischemic attack (TIA), myocardial infarction, angina, heart failure, arrhythmia, peripheral arterial disease, chronic kidney disease (CKD), diabetes, dyslipidemia, sleep apnea, and any history of tumors or cancer treatment.

(4) Family history: inquire whether the patient has a family history of hypertension, stroke, diabetes, dyslipidemia, coronary heart disease, or kidney disease, including the age at which cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events occurred in first-degree relatives.

(5) Clues for secondary hypertension: Look for clues such as a history of nephritis or anemia, symptoms like muscle weakness, episodic paralysis, paroxysmal headaches, palpitations, excessive sweating, or the presence of snoring accompanied by sleep apnea. Also, inquire about the long-term use of medications that may raise blood pressure.

(6) Lifestyle: ask about the intake of salt, alcohol, and fat, smoking habits, physical activity level, weight changes, and sleep habits.

(7) Psychosocial factors: understand the current mental and psychological state, including whether the patient experiences anxiety, depression, or insomnia. Also, inquire about the family situation, work environment, education level, economic status, stressful events, personality traits, and coping strategies.

4.2 Physical Examination

A thorough physical examination helps to identify clues for secondary hypertension and assess the condition of target organ damage. The physical examination includes:

(1) Measurement of Blood Pressure: (See the section 3 “Blood Pressure Measurement”), pulse rate, body mass index (BMI), waist circumference, and hip circumference.

(2) Observation: Look for signs such as Cushingoid appearance, anemic appearance, exophthalmos associated with hyperthyroidism, or lower limb edema.

(3) Auscultation: Perform auscultation of the heart, carotid arteries, thoracic aorta, abdominal arteries, and femoral arteries.

(4) Palpation: Palpate the thyroid gland, check the pulses of the extremities, and assess neurological signs.

4.3 Laboratory Tests

Basic tests: Blood biochemistry (serum potassium, sodium, fasting blood glucose, lipids, uric acid, creatinine), complete blood count, urinalysis (urine protein, glucose, and sediment), electrocardiogram.

Recommended tests: Echocardiogram, carotid ultrasound, oral glucose tolerance test, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), homocysteine, urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR), fundus examination, chest X-ray, pulse wave velocity (PWV), and ankle-brachial index (ABI).

Optional Tests: For suspected cases of secondary hypertension, the following tests may be selected as needed: plasma renin activity (PRA) or renin concentration, serum and 24-hour urinary aldosterone, serum and urinary cortisol, plasma free metanephrines and normetanephrines, blood or urinary catecholamines, 24-h urine protein quantification, 24-h urinary electrolytes, spot urine sodium and creatinine, renal artery ultrasound and angiography, renal and adrenal ultrasound, adrenal computed tomography (CT), adrenal vein sampling (AVS), and sleep monitoring. For hypertensive patients with comorbidities, corresponding tests may include assessments of cardiac function, renal function, lower extremity arterial ultrasound, and cognitive function.

4.4 Assessment of Target Organ Damage

Hypertensive target organ damage refers to structural or functional changes in large arteries, small arteries, or end organs (heart, kidneys, brain, and eyes) caused by elevated blood pressure. It is an indicator of subclinical cardiovascular or renal disease.[108] The presence of target organ damage increases cardiovascular risk. Assessing whether target organ damage is present in hypertensive patients is an essential part of diagnostic evaluation, particularly in detecting asymptomatic subclinical target organ damage. Early detection and timely treatment can reverse subclinical target organ damage. It is recommended to adopt relatively simple, cost-effective, and easily promotable methods for screening and preventing subclinical target organ damage, tailored to local conditions and individual patient needs.

4.4.1 Heart

Left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) is a typical form of target organ damage and an independent risk factor for cardiovascular events. Common examination methods include ECG and echocardiography. ECG is simple and feasible and can be used as a screening method for LVH, with commonly used indicators such as the Sokolow-Lyon voltage (SV1 + RV5) and Cornell voltage-duration product. Echocardiography is more sensitive than ECG in diagnosing LVH. The left ventricular mass index (LVMI) can be used to detect and diagnose LVH and is a strong predictor of cardiovascular events. Other methods to evaluate hypertensive heart damage include 24-h Holter monitoring, chest X-ray, exercise testing, cardiac radionuclide imaging, and coronary CT angiography.

Antihypertensive therapy may reverse left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) and suggest a better prognosis.[109] It is recommended that patients with LVH undergo follow-up, preferably through echocardiography to monitor the progression of LVH and other structural and functional changes in the heart.

4.4.2 Kidneys

Kidney damage is primarily characterized by increased serum creatinine, reduced estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), or increased urinary albumin excretion. A reduced eGFR[110] and increased urinary albumin[111-112] are independent predictors of elevated cardiovascular risk and the progression of kidney disease. Serum creatinine alone is not sensitive enough to assess renal insufficiency, as kidney function may significantly decline before serum creatinine levels rise. The eGFR, calculated based on serum creatinine, is a sensitive indicator of kidney function and can be assessed using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) formula, the modification of diet in renal disease (MDRD) formula, or the MDRD modification formula proposed by Chinese scholars.[113] Hypertensive patients, especially those with diabetes, should have their 24-hour urinary albumin excretion or urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) regularly monitored. UACR, measured from a spot urine sample (preferably morning urine), is the preferred method for quantifying urinary albumin excretion. Elevated serum uric acid levels may also have some predictive value for cardiovascular risk.[114]

4.4.3 Brain

Hypertension is the most critical risk factor for stroke, including ischemic stroke and hemorrhagic stroke. It is also a major risk factor for chronic, asymptomatic brain damage, which can ultimately lead to dementia. Long-term hypertension has a cumulative effect on brain vascular damage, including atherosclerosis, white matter lesions, silent cerebral infarctions, microinfarctions, microbleeds, and brain atrophy.[115]

Cranial magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) or computed tomography angiography (CTA) can help detect lacunar infarctions, asymptomatic cerebrovascular lesions (such as intracranial artery stenosis, calcification, and plaque lesions, aneurysms), and white matter damage, though they are not recommended for clinical screening of target organ damage. Transcranial Doppler ultrasound is helpful in diagnosing cerebral vasospasm, stenosis, or occlusion. The Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) is commonly used for cognitive function screening.

4.4.4 Fundus

Fundoscopy can detect hypertensive retinopathy, such as hemorrhages, microaneurysms, hard exudates, cotton wool spots, papilledema, and/or macular edema. According to the Keith-Wagener and Barker classification, grade 3 or 4 hypertensive retinopathy is valuable in prognosis assessment. Fundoscopy can also observe and analyze retinal microvascular remodeling. Specific hypertensive patients should undergo fundoscopy, particularly those with hypertensive emergencies, suspected malignant hypertension, or concomitant diabetes. New visualization technologies for fundoscopy may assist in screening and evaluating retinopathy in large numbers of hypertensive patients.[116,117]

4.4.5 Large Arteries

Carotid intima-media thickness (IMT) can predict cardiovascular events, with atherosclerotic plaques being a stronger predictor than carotid IMT.[118] There is growing evidence that increased large artery stiffness predicts cardiovascular risk. Accelerated PWV is a strong predictor of cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality. Carotid-femoral pulse wave velocity (cfPWV) is the gold standard for measuring large artery stiffness.[119] Brachial-ankle pulse wave velocity (baPWV) is also strongly correlated with cardiovascular risk and can be used as a key indicator for assessing large artery stiffness.[120,121] The ABI is effective in screening and diagnosing peripheral arterial disease (PAD) and predicting cardiovascular risk.[122]

4.5 Blood Pressure Classification and Cardiovascular Risk Stratification

Key Points

• Definition of Hypertension: In the absence of antihypertensive medication, office blood pressure ≥140/90 mmHg, home blood pressure ≥ 135/85 mmHg, or 24-hour ambulatory blood pressure ≥ 130/80mmHg, daytime blood pressure ≥ 135/85 mmHg,nighttime blood pressure ≥ 120/70 mmHg.

• Based on the elevated level of office blood pressure, hypertension is classified into Grade 1, Grade2, and Grade 3 (I, C).

• Cardiovascular risk stratification is conductedbased on blood pressure levels, cardiovascular riskfactors, target organ damage, clinical complications,and comorbidities such as diabetes and CKD, stratifying into four levels: low risk, moderate risk, highrisk, and very high risk (I, C).

4.5.1 Classification and Grading by Blood Pressure Level

Currently, China classifies blood pressure levels into normal blood pressure, normal-high blood pressure, and hypertension. Hypertension is further classified into Grade 1, Grade 2, and Grade 3 based on office blood pressure levels (Table 6). The retention of Grade 3 hypertension is primarily due to the fact that, compared to countries and regions with higher hypertension control levels, more than 8% of hypertensive patients in China fall under Grade 3 hypertension.[3] It is estimated that there are over 20 million Grade 3 hypertensive patients in China, and the diagnostic and intervention strategies for these patients differ significantly from those for Grade 1 and Grade 2 hypertension, warranting special attention, active monitoring, and treatment to prevent target organ damage and clinical complications. The classification of blood pressure levels 120-139/80-89 mmHg as normal-high is intended to emphasize the need for lifestyle interventions in this population. These classifications and gradings apply to adults aged 18 years and older.

4.5.2 Cardiovascular Risk Stratification

Hypertension is the most significant independent risk factor influencing the occurrence and prognosis of cardiovascular events. However, when combined with other risk factors beyond elevated blood pressure, cardiovascular risk further increases. Therefore, in addition to blood pressure levels, a comprehensive consideration of other cardiovascular risk factors is required to evaluate and stratify cardiovascular risk. The presence of target organ damage, clinical complications, or comorbidities such as diabetes and chronic kidney disease (CKD) further elevates cardiovascular risk. Stratifying cardiovascular risk in hypertensive patients helps to more accurately determine the timing and targets for initiating antihypertensive treatment, optimize treatment strategies, and implement comprehensive management.

When conducting cardiovascular risk stratification, both cardiovascular risk and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) risk can be evaluated. ASCVD risk assessment is mainly used to guide lipid-lowering and glucose-lowering therapies.[125] However, when determining antihypertensive treatment strategies, both ASCVD and hemorrhagic stroke should be considered, so cardiovascular risk assessment is recommended.

The 1999 edition of the Chinese Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of Hypertension adopted the cardiovascular risk assessment and stratification method recommended by the World Health Organization/International Society of Hypertension in 1999, which stratifies hypertensive patients according to their absolute risk of cardiovascular disease over the next 10 years into low risk (< 15%), moderate risk (15%-20%), high risk (20%-30%), and very high risk (> 30%).[126,127] This method has been consistently used in the 2005, 2010, and 2018 editions of the Chinese Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of Hypertension and has been widely applied in clinical practice, the formulation of clinical pathways, and medical insurance payments in China.

Based on the implementation of previous Chinese Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of Hypertension and related research progress, the cardiovascular risk stratification content has been partially modified (Tables 8 and 9). Among the risk factors influencing the cardiovascular prognosis of hypertensive patients, increased heart rate and hyperuricemia have been added.[128,129] In the assessment of target organ damage, brachial-ankle pulse wave velocity (baPWV) has been included,[120,121] and the threshold for carotid-femoral pulse wave velocity (cfPWV) has been adjusted from ≥ 12 m/s to ≥ 10 m/s.[130] According to research results from the Chinese population,[131,132] the left ventricular mass index (LVMI) thresholds for evaluating left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) via echocardiography have been changed to ≥ 109 g/m² for men and ≥ 105 g/m² for women.

5 TREATMENT OF HYPERTENSION

Hypertension is fundamentally a cardiovascular syndrome caused by a variety of etiologies, including genetic and environmental factors. The damage caused by hypertension is determined by the elevated blood pressure itself, as well as by other concomitant cardiovascular risk factors, target organ damage, and/or complications affecting the heart, brain, kidneys, and blood vessels. Therefore, the treatment of hypertension should encompass the following three aspects: (1) antihypertensive treatment targeting elevated blood pressure itself (staging); (2) treatment addressing concomitant risk factors, target organ damage, and clinical complications (grading); and (3) correction and treatment of the underlying causes of hypertension (classification).

5.1 Strategies for Antihypertensive Treatment

The goal of antihypertensive treatment is to effectively reduce blood pressure, control the progression of hypertension, and prevent or delay complications such as stroke, myocardial infarction, heart failure, and renal insufficiency.

Antihypertensive treatment requires balancing benefits and risks. For patients with low cardiovascular risk, the absolute benefits of drug treatment are smaller compared to those with high risk, though the costs and potential risks of drug therapy may not differ significantly.

5.1.1 Populations benefiting from antihypertensive treatment

Key Point 5A: Populations Benefiting from Antihypertensive Treatment

• The benefits of antihypertensive drug treatmentare mainly observed in hypertensive patients withmoderate to high cardiovascular risk and individuals with high-normal blood pressure at high cardiovascular risk.

• In antihypertensive therapy, it is necessary toweigh the benefits and potential risks, especially forhigh-risk and very high-risk patients, those withmultiple comorbidities, and elderly patients.

A meta-analysis including 16 clinical trials comparing different intensities of blood pressure-lowering treatment showed that a reduction of 10/5 mmHg in systolic/diastolic blood pressure can reduce the relative risk of stroke by 29%, coronary heart disease by 20%, major cardiovascular events by 25%, and cardiovascular disease mortality by 21%.[133] The Blood Pressure Lowering Treatment Triallists’ Collaboration (BPLTTC) meta-analysis results, which included 48 clinical trials using antihypertensive drugs, showed that in patients with and without previous cardiovascular disease, a reduction of 5 mmHg in systolic blood pressure reduced the relative risk of major cardiovascular events by 9% and 11%, respectively. Considering most of the patients with a baseline systolic blood pressure < 140 mmHg included in the BPLTTC had already taken antihypertensive drugs, the blood pressure grading of these patients is not clear, and the cardiovascular protective effect of renin-angiotensin system (RAS) inhibitor class antihypertensive drugs cannot be ruled out.

The main benefit of antihypertensive drug treatment has been confirmed in hypertensive patients at moderate or higher cardiovascular risk and in those with high-normal blood pressure who are at high cardiovascular risk. Large-scale randomized clinical trials and subsequent meta-analyses have demonstrated the benefits of antihypertensive drug treatment in hypertensive patients.[135] In individuals with high-normal blood pressure who are at high cardiovascular risk, meta-analyses have shown that antihypertensive treatment can significantly reduce cardiovascular events.[133,136] However, subgroup analyses from the Heart Outcomes Prevention Evaluation (HOPE)-3 study indicated that antihypertensive treatment did not reduce cardiovascular event risk in individuals with normal or high-normal blood pressure at moderate cardiovascular risk.[137]

Currently, there is still a lack of evidence from intensive antihypertensive intervention studies in populations with multiple comorbidities, frail elderly individuals (living in nursing homes or requiring assistance with daily living), and very elderly individuals.

Given that hypertension control rates in China remain low and that hypertension continues to be the leading risk factor for cardiovascular disease morbidity and mortality, an intensive antihypertensive treatment strategy should be adopted where treatment conditions allow, to achieve maximum cardiovascular benefits.

5.1.3.1 Office blood pressure targets

Based on previous research evidence, the target office blood pressure for the general hypertensive population should be controlled below 140/90 mmHg.

In recent years, several clinical studies have explored the impact of lower office blood pressure targets (systolic blood pressure < 130 mmHg) on cardiovascular outcomes in hypertensive patients.[139-142] The results have shown that in patients at high cardiovascular risk, those with diabetes, post-stroke patients, and elderly patients, intensive blood pressure control (target systolic blood pressure < 130 mmHg) is more beneficial for cardiovascular outcomes. A meta-analysis of clinical trials with varying intensities of antihypertensive treatment has shown that lowering office systolic blood pressure to < 130 mmHg,[133] compared to ≥ 130 mmHg, significantly reduces the risks of stroke, coronary heart disease, cardiovascular mortality, and all-cause mortality. A network meta-analysis based on randomized trials suggests that a target office systolic blood pressure of < 130 mmHg achieves the best balance between efficacy and safety in hypertensive patients.[143]

Historically, studies have suggested that the blood pressure target for elderly hypertensive patients should be higher than that for the general hypertensive population. However, a clinical study and subgroup analysis published in 2021 indicated that a lower office blood pressure target (systolic blood pressure < 130 mmHg) may be beneficial for the elderly population.[77,142] It is important to note, however, that advancing age alone is not a sufficient reason to set a higher blood pressure target. For elderly patients, the blood pressure target should be determined based on a comprehensive assessment of the severity of comorbidities, treatment tolerability, and factors influencing treatment adherence.

In summary, the recommended office blood pressure targets are as follows (Figure 4): (1) for hypertensive patients at high or very high cardiovascular risk, and those with comorbidities, the recommended office blood pressure target is < 130/80 mmHg, provided the patient can tolerate it.


Figure 4Office blood pressure targets.

(2) For the general hypertensive population without comorbidities, the target should be < 140/90 mmHg, and if tolerated, further reduced to < 130/80 mmHg.

(3) For elderly hypertensive patients, those aged 65–79 years should aim for < 140/90 mmHg, and if tolerated, < 130/80 mmHg; for those aged 80 years and older, the target should be < 150/90 mmHg, and if tolerated, < 140/90 mmHg.

The choice and intensity of treatment strategies should balance long-term benefits with patient tolerability, considering the potential adverse events associated with intensive antihypertensive treatment, such as hypotension, electrolyte imbalances, acute kidney injury or failure, and syncope.[77] It is important to avoid or minimize treatment discontinuation due to patient intolerance. Intensive interventions are recommended for high-risk and very high-risk patients, and active interventions to reverse target organ damage are reasonable for patients without severe comorbidities but with subclinical target organ damage. There is no clinical evidence supporting the benefit of antihypertensive drug treatment for low- to moderate-risk individuals with high-normal blood pressure.

While some evidence suggests different blood pressure targets for specific populations, this largely depends on the patient’s tolerance to treatment and the complexity of the treatment regimen. If blood pressure can be safely reduced to lower levels without the need for a complex treatment plan, there is no need to adjust the treatment to allow for a higher blood pressure.

5.1.3.2 Blood pressure targets beyond office blood pressure

Defining blood pressure targets is crucial for effective hypertension management. Historically, most clinical studies on blood pressure targets have used office blood pressure measurements. However, the role of Home Blood Pressure Monitoring (HBPM) and Ambulatory Blood Pressure Monitoring (ABPM) in hypertension management has become increasingly important. Therefore, in addition to achieving the target office blood pressure, it is also essential to monitor and achieve target levels for home blood pressure and ambulatory blood pressure. Based on the corresponding risk of events in the population, which aligns with an office blood pressure of 140/90 mmHg (Table 10),[144] the following targets can be considered: a home blood pressure target of < 135/85 mmHg and a 24-h ambulatory blood pressure target of < 130/80 mmHg.

Achieving full 24-h blood pressure control should be a key goal of antihypertensive treatment.[145] Increasing evidence indicates that nighttime hypertension, non-dipper blood pressure patterns, and morning blood pressure surges are associated with adverse cardiovascular outcomes. Antihypertensive treatment should focus on controlling 24-h blood pressure, including morning and nighttime levels. Prospective observational studies have shown that morning hypertension detected through home blood pressure monitoring is associated with higher cardiovascular risk, regardless of office blood pressure. Even when office and/or morning blood pressure are well controlled, isolated nighttime hypertension remains a risk factor for target organ damage and cardiovascular disease.[146] On top of achieving target office blood pressure, it is recommended to aim for a morning blood pressure target of < 135/85 mmHg and a nighttime blood pressure target of < 120/70 mmHg.

Blood pressure variability (BPV) is an important risk factor for cardiovascular outcomes, independent of average blood pressure levels. Evidence from clinical trials, large registries, and meta-analyses has shown that increased BPV can predict cardiovascular outcomes independently of mean blood pressure values.[147] Some patients have high cardiovascular risk despite controlled office blood pressure, largely due to elevated BPV.[148] However, the clinical application of BPV is limited by the complexity of its indicators and the lack of standardization in assessment methods. Currently, no interventional studies have examined whether reducing BPV translates into clinical benefits, and no specific treatment targets for BPV have been proposed.

The concept of “Time in Target Range” (TTR), which estimates the proportion of time a patient’s blood pressure remains within a specified target range, has emerged as a predictor of cardiovascular risk independent of blood pressure levels. Studies suggest that higher TTR is consistently associated with lower cardiovascular risk and all-cause mortality.[149-150] TTR reflects the long-term consistency and persistence of blood pressure control and represents a new metric for evaluating the effectiveness of antihypertensive therapy. However, further research is needed to explore its clinical application.

5.1.3.3 Time to achieve target blood pressure

Reducing blood pressure to target levels significantly lowers the risk of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular complications. Subgroup analyses from the Felodipine Event Reduction (FEVER) study suggest that patients who achieve target blood pressure within one month may have a further reduced risk of cardiovascular events compared to those who achieve target levels later.[151] A retrospective analysis showed that patients who reached target blood pressure within 30 days of hypertension diagnosis had a lower all-cause mortality risk compared to those who achieved it after more than 90 days, with no significant difference between those who achieved it in 30-90 days.[152]

For most hypertensive patients, blood pressure should be gradually reduced to the target level within 4 to 12 weeks, depending on the patient’s condition. For younger patients with a shorter duration of hypertension, the target can be reached more quickly. In elderly patients, especially those with a longer disease course, comorbidities, and poorer tolerance, the reduction rate can be slower.

5.2 Methods of Antihypertensive Treatment

Antihypertensive treatment methods include lifestyle modification, pharmacological therapy, and device-based therapies. Lifestyle modification is an indispensable component of hypertension treatment. For the majority of hypertensive patients, antihypertensive medications are often required. Over the past decade, device-based therapies have accumulated significant evidence in a select group of hypertensive patients.

5.2.1.1 Reducing sodium intake and increasing potassium intake

Excessive sodium intake and/or insufficient potassium intake, as well as a low potassium-to-sodium intake ratio, are significant risk factors for the prevalence of hypertension in China.[8,153] Moderately reducing sodium intake and increasing dietary potassium can help lower blood pressure.[154-157] A low-salt diet is recommended for the general population, and sodium intake should be restricted in hypertensive patients.[158]

Several studies, including the Salt Substitute and Stroke Study (SSaSS) and the Diet, Exercise, and Cardiovascular Health (DECIDE-salt) study,[159,160] conducted in stroke patients and high-risk stroke populations in China, have shown that low-sodium, potassium-enriched salt substitutes can significantly reduce blood pressure, lower the risk of stroke, and decrease cardiovascular events and mortality. For example, in Shandong Province, a government-led salt reduction program was implemented, which included the development of local food standards and regulations, promotion of low-sodium products, distribution of salt measuring spoons, media campaigns, and public education. Five years after the program began, the population’s 24-h urinary sodium excretion decreased by 25%, potassium excretion increased by 15%, the adjusted mean systolic blood pressure dropped from 131.8 mmHg to 130.0 mmHg, and the mean diastolic blood pressure decreased from 83.9 mmHg to 80.8 mmHg.[161]

In the Chinese diet, approximately 75% of sodium comes from salt used in home cooking, followed by high-sodium condiments and pickled products. With changes in dietary patterns, sodium from processed foods has also become an important source of sodium intake.[162] Therefore, it is essential to strengthen sodium content labeling and increase public awareness of sodium intake.

Key recommendations and measures for reducing sodium and increasing potassium intake include: (1) all hypertensive patients should take measures to limit sodium intake; (2) it is recommended that sodium intake first be reduced by 30% and further reduced to less than 2 g/day (5 g/day of sodium chloride); (3) for individuals with good renal function, low-sodium, potassium-enriched salt substitutes are recommended; (4) reduce the use of salt in cooking and high-sodium condiments (including monosodium glutamate and soy sauce), and use other seasonings (such as chili, garlic, vinegar, and pepper) to enhance flavor; (5) avoid or reduce the intake of high-sodium processed foods, such as pickles, ham, and various types of salted and preserved foods; (6) it is recommended to use a salt measuring spoon during cooking as a reminder to limit salt usage; (7) increase the intake of potassium-rich foods, such as fresh vegetables, fruits, and legumes; and (8) potassium supplements (including medications) are not recommended for lowering blood pressure.

Hypertensive patients are advised to assess their salt intake using simple methods. Semi-quantitative evaluations can be conducted using simplified food questionnaires and salt threshold tests, applicable to about 50% of the population.[163,164] Chinese researchers have proposed a formula to estimate sodium intake using spot urine samples, which is suitable for sodium chloride intake ranging from 6 to 12 g/day (urinary sodium excretion of 106 to 212 mmol/day). The estimated value within this range has an average deviation of 1.1 mmol/day from the actual measured value, making it useful for quantitative assessments of sodium intake, although there may still be tendencies to underestimate high values and overestimate low values.[165]

5.2.1.2 Balanced diet

A healthy dietary pattern can reduce the risk of hypertension and cardiovascular diseases. A balanced diet is a crucial means of preventing and treating hypertension.[166,167] Individuals with high-normal blood pressure and hypertensive patients should understand the principles and methods of dietary management and consistently apply them in daily life and self-management.

The Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) diet, developed in 1997 by the National Institutes of Health in the United States, was specifically designed to control hypertension according to the principles of a diet rich in fruits, vegetables, and protein, while being low in fat, sugar, and salt. The DASH diet is high in fresh vegetables, fruits, low-fat (or fat-free) dairy products, poultry, fish, soy, and nuts, while being low in sugar-sweetened beverages and red meat. It is low in saturated fat and cholesterol, and rich in potassium, magnesium, calcium, high-quality protein, and fiber. The standard DASH diet can reduce blood pressure within 14 days,[168] and adherence to the DASH diet effectively lowers the risk of cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality.[167]

The Chinese Heart-Healthy (CHH) diet is a healthy dietary pattern that aligns with Chinese dietary cultural characteristics. Based on the nutrient intake standards of a healthy diet for the Chinese population, it consists of a main and side dish meal plan for breakfast, lunch, and dinner over a two-week cycle without repetition. This diet reduces daily sodium intake from 6 g to 3 g, decreases saturated fat intake, and increases intake of protein, high-quality carbohydrates, potassium, and dietary fiber. A clinical study published in 2022 on the CHH diet showed that it significantly reduced blood pressure in hypertensive patients.[169]

Data analysis from the China Kadoorie Biobank study found that frequent consumption of spicy foods can prevent hypertension.[170,171] Research conducted by Chinese scholars indicates that the primary nutrient in spicy diets, such as chili peppers, is capsaicin, which promotes the production of nitric oxide in the vascular endothelium by acting on its specific target, the capsaicin receptor. This leads to vasodilation and blood pressure reduction, providing important scientific evidence for the prevention of cardiovascular metabolic diseases through spicy diets.[172,173] A meta-analysis published in 2022 found that a preference for spicy food reduces the risk of death from cardiovascular diseases and cancer.[174]

5.2.1.3 Weight Management

Obesity is both an independent disease and a risk factor for various conditions, including type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, hypertension, and stroke. It is listed by the WHO as one of the top ten risk factors contributing to disease burden. The diagnostic criteria for overweight and obesity in Chinese adults are as follows: a body mass index (BMI) of 24 to < 28 kg/m² is classified as overweight, and a BMI ≥ 28 kg/m² is classified as obesity. Central obesity is diagnosed if the waist circumference is ≥ 90 cm for men or ≥ 85 cm for women.[176-177]

Analysis of data from the 2014-2015 China Stroke Screening and Prevention Project involving 62,880 subjects aged 40 years and older revealed a positive correlation between body mass index (BMI) and the risk of hypertension: for every standard deviation increase in BMI, the risk of developing hypertension increases by 53.9%.[178] An observational study conducted among hypertensive patients in Chinese communities showed that, over a follow-up period of 2.9 years, the risk of developing left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) was 2.51 times higher in the obese group compared to the normal weight group.[179] Research on cardiovascular disease monitoring, prevention, early warning, and treatment technology has found that, compared to middle-aged individuals with normal BMI and blood pressure, the risk of cardiovascular events increases by 1.4 times in overweight individuals and 2.3 times in obese individuals.[180] Studies have confirmed that weight loss can effectively lower blood pressure levels and reduce the risk of cardiovascular events.[181,182]

Individuals with high-normal blood pressure and all hypertensive patients should actively control their weight. The main recommendations and measures include: (1) all overweight and obese patients should lose weight, while individuals with normal weight should maintain their weight within a healthy range (BMI: 18.5-23.9 kg/m²; waist circumference < 90 cm for men and < 85 cm for women).[183] (2) The goal of weight management should be to reduce weight by 5% to 15% or more,[184] with a target of losing 5% to 10% of the initial body weight within one year.[185] (3) Weight control should primarily be achieved through comprehensive lifestyle interventions, including self-monitoring of weight, adopting a balanced diet, increasing physical activity and exercise, and behavioral interventions (Table 13). (4) For those who do not achieve satisfactory weight loss results through comprehensive lifestyle interventions, drug therapy or surgical treatment is recommended. (5) Special populations, such as breastfeeding women and the elderly, should adopt individualized weight loss measures according to their specific circumstances.

5.2.1.4 Smoking cessation

Early data analysis from the Systolic Hypertension in China (Syst-China) clinical trial indicated that smoking is a risk factor for all-cause mortality, non-cardiovascular disease mortality, cancer mortality, as well as fatal and non-fatal stroke. The potential benefits of smoking cessation for hypertensive patients are roughly comparable to the benefits of antihypertensive treatment.[186]

Recent studies suggest that smoking is associated with an increased incidence of masked hypertension,[187,188] particularly in heavy smokers or elderly males. Quitting smoking can significantly reduce the risk of cardiovascular disease and all-cause mortality.[189] Therefore, assisting smokers in quitting is crucial for the prevention and control of cardiovascular diseases.

Key recommendations and measures for smoking cessation include: (1) physicians should strongly advise and urge hypertensive smokers to quit smoking. (2) If necessary, smoking cessation medications should be used to combat withdrawal symptoms. (3) The use of electronic cigarettes as a substitute therapy should be avoided as much as possible. (4) Smoking cessation should be supplemented with physical exercise. (5) Combined smoking cessation interventions, including psychological and behavioral interventions, smoking cessation medications, and various cessation media, should be employed. (6) Personalized smoking cessation interventions should be implemented.

5.2.1.5 Limiting alcohol consumption

Alcohol consumption increases the risk of hypertension, and blood pressure levels are positively correlated with alcohol intake.[21] Different types of alcoholic beverages have varying associations with hypertension; beer (odds ratio = 1.51), wine (odds ratio = 1.71), and spirits (odds ratio = 2.01) show progressively stronger associations with hypertension.[190]

Consuming 280 g of alcohol per week increases systolic blood pressure by 4.8 mmHg.[191] Long-term alcohol consumption is an independent risk factor for the development of hypertension, even at low levels of consumption.[21,192] An analysis of 83 prospective studies involving over 500,000 drinkers in high-income countries revealed that the lowest mortality risk is associated with alcohol intake below 100 g per week. Above this threshold, the incidence of fatal hypertensive diseases gradually increases with higher alcohol consumption.[193]

Limiting alcohol intake can lower blood pressure and alleviate the dual disease burden caused by alcohol consumption and hypertension, leading to synergistic health benefits. A meta-analysis indicates a clear dose-response relationship between reduced alcohol intake and blood pressure reduction for individuals consuming more than 24 g of alcohol daily.[194]

Key recommendations and measures for limiting alcohol consumption include: (1) no type of alcohol is beneficial to the human body, and the alcohol consumption level that minimizes health loss is zero. (2) Hypertensive patients are advised not to drink alcohol. If drinking is unavoidable, the daily alcohol intake should not exceed 25 g for men and 15 g for women.[195] The calculation method for alcohol intake is 0.8 × volume consumed (mL) × alcohol content (% v/v) as indicated on the bottle divided by 100.

5.2.1.6 Exercise intervention

Exercise can improve blood pressure levels. Studies have shown that regular exercise is beneficial for both the prevention and treatment of hypertension.[196-198] For hypertensive patients, exercise for therapeutic purposes involves not only increasing daily physical activity but also implementing active exercise interventions.

Specific forms of exercise interventions include: (1) aerobic exercise: strong evidence indicates that aerobic exercise can reduce blood pressure by 5 to 7 mmHg in adult hypertensive patients.[199,200] Among low, medium, and high-intensity aerobic exercises, moderate-intensity aerobic exercise has the best antihypertensive effect.[197,201] (2) Resistance exercise: the blood pressure-lowering effect of resistance exercise may be comparable to or even greater than that of aerobic exercise.[200] The purpose of resistance exercise for hypertensive patients is not to increase muscle strength but to achieve therapeutic effects through light strength training. (3) Meditation and breathing exercises: these can alleviate or even eliminate various causes of hypertension, such as psychological stress, cervicogenic cardiovascular diseases, and poor posture. (4) Flexibility and stretching exercises: comprehensive training of joint mobility and muscle strength, such as flexibility and stretching exercises, is a simple and safe method to eliminate fatigue, improve daily activity capacity, and delay aging.

For hypertensive patients with well-controlled blood pressure, it is recommended to adopt a mixed training regimen primarily consisting of aerobic exercise (moderate intensity, 30 minutes per day, 5-7 days per week) and supplemented by resistance exercise (2-3 times per week). It is also advisable to combine breathing exercises with flexibility and stretching exercises. For patients whose blood pressure is not controlled (systolic pressure > 160 mmHg), high-intensity exercise is not recommended until blood pressure is under control. - Static Resistance Exercise: Seated or standing lateral arm raises; weighted lateral arm raises; wall-sit at a high position.
Exercise Intensity: For dynamic resistance exercises, use 50%-70% of 1RM*; for static or isometric resistance training, typically use low intensity (< 40% of 1RM) with intermittent grip strength exercises.
Exercise Duration and Frequency: For dynamic resistance exercises, repeat 8-12 times per set, progressively increasing to 2-3 sets per session, 2-3 times per week; for static or isometric resistance exercises, each session should last 2 minutes, with a total exercise duration of 12-15 minutes.
Breathing and Meditation	Target Muscles: Active expiratory and inspiratory muscles.
Exercise Forms: Awareness of cervical breathing, chest breathing, abdominal breathing, and the full process of exhalation and inhalation. Rhythmic breathing during aerobic exercise, with patterns such as one inhale followed by one exhale, or one inhale followed by three exhales. During resistance training, avoid holding your breath; synchronize breathing with slow movements, inhaling when exerting force and exhaling slowly during relaxation.
Exercise Intensity: Start breathing practice at 30%-50% of maximum inspiratory and expiratory pressures.
Exercise Duration and Frequency: Relax and focus on breathing anytime and anywhere, consciously slow down or mentally count breaths, take a few deep breaths, with slow inhalations and even slower deep exhalations. Avoid hyperventilation caused by excessively rapid breathing.
Flexibility and Stretching	Exercise Forms: Awareness of the neutral spine position, and gentle stretching that matches individual spinal joint mobility, including flexion, extension, lateral bending, and rotation of the cervical and thoracic spine. Gradual active stretching within the pain-free range of motion for the limbs; avoid forceful passive stretching with assistance. Synchronize breathing with specific stretching movements and avoid holding your breath during stretches.

When recommending exercise interventions to hypertensive patients, clinicians should conduct a comprehensive evaluation. The assessment should include the patient’s blood pressure and cardiovascular risk levels, evaluation of cardiopulmonary endurance through a cardiopulmonary exercise test, measurement of maximal oxygen uptake, assessment of peak blood pressure during exercise and the time taken for blood pressure to recover post-exercise, as well as body composition analysis to determine muscle mass and visceral fat. This assessment will help identify contraindications to exercise and enhance the safety of exercise interventions for hypertension.

Clinicians should also emphasize exercise safety to hypertensive patients. Patients need to monitor their blood pressure values at the beginning, during, and at the end of exercise. It is crucial to be aware of symptoms such as chest pain, unusual shortness of breath, or dizziness, and if such symptoms occur, exercise should be stopped immediately. Patients should avoid exercises that involve significant static muscle contractions, especially those that require breath-holding.

5.2.1.7 Reducing mental stress

Meta-analyses have shown that increased mental stress raises the risk of developing hypertension.[22,23] Large cohort studies also indicate that persistent mental tension, negative life events, and emotions such as anger significantly increase the risk of ischemic cardiovascular diseases.[202,203]

Mental stress can be alleviated through various methods, such as cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT), mindfulness and meditation, yoga, deep breathing exercises, and progressive muscle relaxation.[198,204,205] A meta-analysis of 15 randomized controlled trials showed that CBT can reduce symptoms of depression and anxiety, leading to decreases in systolic and diastolic blood pressure by 8.7 mmHg and 5.8 mmHg, respectively.[206] Another recent meta-analysis reported that mindfulness-based stress reduction practices, including eight weekly mindfulness sessions and more than 30 min of daily meditation, can improve stress and emotional fluctuations in hypertensive patients, reducing systolic and diastolic blood pressure by 6.6 mmHg and 2.5 mmHg, respectively.[207] Additionally, a systematic review found that practicing yoga for 45 minutes daily over 12 weeks can lower systolic blood pressure by 6.5 mmHg and diastolic blood pressure by 2.8 mmHg.[208]

Combining different methods, such as mindfulness and meditation with exercise and yoga, may be most effective for hypertensive patients. The primary recommendations and measures for reducing mental stress include:[198] (1) engage in at least three hours of stress-reduction exercises weekly to alleviate mental stress and lower blood pressure; (2) alternatively, practice yoga, meditation, or Tai Chi for at least 45 min daily; (3) listen to music for at least 25 min each day or three times a week.

5.2.1.8 Maintaining healthy sleep

Healthy sleep includes adequate sleep duration and good sleep quality. Healthy sleep is associated with a lower risk of hypertension,[209] and among hypertensive patients, it is related to a lower incidence of coronary heart disease and stroke.[210] Short sleep duration increases the risk of developing hypertension.[211,212] and insomnia is associated with cardiovascular disease mortality and all-cause mortality.[213] Cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) is beneficial for hypertensive patients as it can improve sleep quality and lower blood pressure.[206]

Specific recommendations for maintaining healthy sleep include:[198] (1) it is recommended that adults aim for 7-9 h of sleep per night, ensure good sleep quality, and maintain a regular sleep schedule.

(2) Patients with resistant hypertension, nocturnal hypertension, and/or abnormal blood pressure dipping patterns should be screened for sleep disorders.

(3) Individuals with sleep disorders (such as snoring, sleep apnea, or insomnia) should regularly monitor their blood pressure and consider ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM).

(4) Those who work night shifts or have rotating work schedules should consider using ABPM for diagnosis and follow-up assessment.

(5) Hypertensive patients should avoid taking diuretics at night to prevent excessive nighttime urination, which can disrupt sleep.

Measures to improve sleep disorders include: (1) sleep assessment: conduct a sleep assessment using patient history, physical examination, sleep diaries, the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), and polysomnography technology.

(2) Sleep cognitive behavioral therapy: (a) educate patients on sleep hygiene, such as going to bed at the same time each night, avoiding caffeine and nicotine before bed, taking a warm foot bath or shower before bed, using comfortable bedding, maintaining a comfortable room temperature, and ensuring a quiet environment or playing soft, soothing music; (b) Implement stimulus control therapy by avoiding stimulating activities unrelated to sleep (such as watching TV, using mobile phones, reading, or planning/thinking) while in bed; (c) Apply sleep restriction therapy by controlling the duration of daytime naps. Hypnotherapy, physical therapy, and traditional Chinese medicine treatments can also help improve sleep disorders.

(3) Medication: if necessary, use medications while paying attention to interactions between antihypertensive drugs and sleep medications:[198] (a) anti-anxiety/depression medications can increase the concentration of nifedipine, verapamil, and metoprolol, so drugs with minimal interactions, such as sertraline and citalopram, should be chosen; (b) trazodone, mirtazapine, and venlafaxine can cause orthostatic hypotension, so dosages should be carefully monitored, and these drugs should be taken before bedtime.

Lifestyle interventions can undoubtedly lower blood pressure and, in some cases, reduce cardiovascular risk. However, most hypertensive patients and some individuals with high cardiovascular risk at normal high blood pressure levels still require pharmacological treatment. A series of outcome-based randomized controlled trials provides reliable evidence for the pharmacological treatment of hypertension.

5.2.2.1 Clinical trial evidence for antihypertensive treatment

Since the 1950s, randomized controlled trials (RCTs) focusing on cardiovascular and cerebrovascular complications have been conducted worldwide, establishing the theoretical foundation for the treatment and management of hypertension. These clinical trials can be categorized into four main types: (1) placebo-controlled trials: Early hypertension treatment trials primarily compared antihypertensive therapy with placebo or no treatment. The results demonstrated that antihypertensive treatment significantly reduces the risk of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular complications in various types of hypertension. These studies form the most important theoretical basis for the treatment and management of different types of hypertension.[214,215]

(2) Comparative Trials Between Different Drug Classes: These trials mainly compared newer antihypertensive drugs, such as calcium channel blockers (CCBs), angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs), and angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs), with traditional antihypertensive drugs like thiazide diuretics and β-blockers. The findings indicated that blood pressure reduction is the primary reason these antihypertensive drugs decrease cardiovascular and cerebrovascular complications.[216] Although differences among the drugs are generally small, specific differences exist in particular populations or complications, and different combination therapies also show variability.[217]

(3) Trials Involving Hypertensive Patients: These studies compared intensive versus non-intensive blood pressure management to determine the optimal target blood pressure for treatment. For example, in the United States, the Systolic Blood Pressure Intervention Trial (SPRINT) enrolled hypertensive patients and conducted intensive antihypertensive treatment trials using various drugs to lower the average systolic blood pressure to 121 mmHg. Compared to a target of 133 mmHg, this more intensive approach significantly reduced the incidence of composite cardiovascular endpoint events, mainly heart failure.[77,141] It is worth noting that the blood pressure measurements in SPRINT were taken using automated devices, which generally produce readings 5-15 mmHg lower than traditional clinical methods. The study on the Strategy of Blood Pressure Intervention in the Elderly Hypertensive Patients (STEP) conducted in our country, using a similar open trial design and selecting a population of hypertensive patients aged 60 to 79, further confirmed that significant differences in blood pressure can lead to differences in cardiovascular events, primarily stroke.[142]

(4) Trials Involving High or Intermediate Cardiovascular Risk Patients: these studies included some patients with normal blood pressure or those whose blood pressure was already controlled. The results suggest that after achieving a target blood pressure below 140/90 mmHg, further reductions should follow individualized principles, fully considering the patient's specific characteristics, the composition of the antihypertensive regimen, and the implementation methods.[137]

Chinese researchers have independently completed a series of antihypertensive clinical trials and contributed to several international multicenter studies. Early studies such as the Syst-China trial.[218,219] the Shanghai Elderly Hypertension Study,[220] and the Chengdu Hypertension Intervention Trial[221] confirmed that aggressive antihypertensive therapy based on CCBs, such as nitrendipine and nifedipine, can significantly reduce the incidence and mortality of stroke among Chinese hypertensive patients. Building on these findings, the FEVER study demonstrated that adding felodipine to hydrochlorothiazide, compared to hydrochlorothiazide alone, further lowered systolic/diastolic blood pressure by 4/2 mmHg and reduced the incidence of fatal and non-fatal strokes by 27%.[222] Subsequent post-hoc analyses of the FEVER study revealed that the lowest risks of stroke, cardiac events, and overall mortality were observed when the average treated blood pressure was below 120/70 mmHg. Additionally, elderly patients with a systolic blood pressure < 140 mmHg experienced more significant benefits than those with higher blood pressure.[223,224] The Post-Stroke Antihypertensive Treatment Study (PATS), the first large-scale placebo-controlled trial in the world for secondary prevention of stroke, showed that treatment with indapamide (2.5 mg/day) reduced blood pressure by 5/2 mmHg and decreased the incidence of stroke by 29% compared to placebo.[225,226] Intervention studies aimed at preventing hypertension in individuals with high-normal blood pressure (systolic/diastolic blood pressure of 130-139/80-89 mmHg) did not observe clinical benefits from blood pressure reduction.

Participation of Chinese Researchers in International Multicenter Antihypertensive Trials: Chinese researchers have participated in several international multicenter clinical trials on antihypertensive treatment, such as the Perindopril Protection Against Recurrent Stroke Study (PROGRESS),[227] the Hypertension in the Very Elderly Trial (HYVET),[228] the Action in Diabetes and Vascular Disease: Preterax and Diamicron Controlled Evaluation (ADVANCE),[229] and HOPE-3.[137] The results of the PROGRESS trial indicated that treatment with perindopril combined with indapamide, or perindopril alone, reduced the risk of recurrent stroke by 28%. Subgroup analysis showed that Asian participants, particularly those from China and Japan, experienced a greater reduction in stroke risk. Post-hoc analysis revealed that lowering the average post-treatment blood pressure to as low as 112/72 mmHg did not exhibit a J-shaped curve. Further follow-up of the 1520 Chinese participants over an average of six years confirmed that antihypertensive treatment significantly reduced the risk of recurrent stroke, as well as the risks of total mortality and myocardial infarction.[230]

The HYVET trial demonstrated that, in very elderly hypertensive patients (aged ≥ 80 years) with systolic blood pressure > 160 mmHg, lowering systolic blood pressure to 150 mmHg using sustained-release indapamide significantly reduced the risks of stroke and death compared to placebo.[228] The ADVANCE trial results indicated that, in diabetic patients, lowering blood pressure by 5.6/2.2 mmHg to an average of 135/75 mmHg with low-dose perindopril/indapamide combination therapy reduced the risk of combined macrovascular and microvascular events by 9% compared to standard antihypertensive treatment.[229]

In the HOPE-3 trial, the candesartan/hydrochlorothiazide combination reduced systolic/diastolic blood pressure by an additional 6/3 mmHg compared to placebo, but overall, it did not show clinical benefit. Further predefined subgroup analysis revealed that in patients with systolic blood pressure > 143.5 mmHg, the cardiovascular risk in the antihypertensive treatment group was significantly lower than in the placebo group. However, in patients with systolic blood pressure < 131.5 mmHg, aggressive blood pressure reduction did not result in a decrease in cardiovascular risk.[137]

5.2.2.2 Basic Principles for the Use of Antihypertensive Drugs

The goal of pharmacological treatment is to achieve and maintain target blood pressure levels over the long term, ultimately reducing the overall risk of cardiovascular, cerebrovascular, renal, and vascular complications and mortality. Based on this goal, the following basic principles for the use of antihypertensive drugs are recommended: (1) risk reduction: It is recommended to choose antihypertensive drugs that have been proven to reduce the risk of cardiovascular disease and mortality.

(2) Long-Acting Antihypertensive Drugs: drugs that effectively control blood pressure for 24 h with once-daily dosing should be the first choice. These long-acting drugs have the advantage of reducing blood pressure variability, maintaining circadian rhythm, and are more effective in preventing cardiovascular and cerebrovascular complications.[129,145,231]

(3) Combination Therapy: for patients with blood pressure ≥ 160/100 mmHg, those whose blood pressure is 20/10 mmHg above the target, high-risk/very high-risk cardiovascular patients, or hypertensive patients who do not achieve target blood pressure with monotherapy, combination antihypertensive therapy is recommended.[232,233] For patients with stage 1 hypertension, starting with low-dose combination therapy may also be considered.[234] Combination therapy includes free combination or single-pill combinations (SPCs). SPC is beneficial for improving adherence and can be recommended preferentially.[235-236]

(4) Initial Dosage: standard doses are generally used for most patients; for frail and elderly patients or those with very high-risk conditions involving the heart, brain, or kidneys, it is typically recommended to start treatment with a lower effective dose. If necessary, the dose can be gradually increased to the full dose.[238]

(5) Timing of medication: Antihypertensive drugs are usually taken in the morning. Recent studies, such as the TIME study,[237] have shown that taking antihypertensive drugs in the evening does not provide additional cardiovascular benefits compared to morning administration. Unless there is a clear need to control nighttime blood pressure elevation, taking antihypertensive medication before bedtime is not routinely recommended.[145]

(6) Individualized treatment: The choice of antihypertensive drug should be tailored to the patient's comorbidities, drug efficacy, and tolerance, as well as the patient's personal preferences and long-term compliance.

5.2.2.3 Classification of Antihypertensive Drugs

The 2018 edition of the Chinese Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of Hypertension recommends commonly used antihypertensive drugs, including CCBs, ACEIs, ARBs, thiazide diuretics, and β-blockers, as well as single-pill combinations (SPCs) composed of these drugs. This guideline introduces angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitors (ARNIs) as a new category of commonly used antihypertensive drugs. The guideline suggests that all these antihypertensive drugs and SPCs can be used for both initial and maintenance therapy.

Different patients present with varying risk factors, target organ damage, and concomitant clinical conditions, which can be termed as strong indications for the selection of specific antihypertensive drugs. Prioritizing a particular class of antihypertensive drug based on these clinical situations is necessary for stratified and precise blood pressure management.[238,239] Different types of antihypertensive drugs, even those within the same class, may vary in the duration of their antihypertensive effect and the discontinuation rate of medication.[133]

The benefits of antihypertensive therapy mainly derive from the reduction in blood pressure. Some meta-analyses have reported differences among drugs in the prevention of specific cardiovascular events.[133,135] However, overall, the benefits provided by the commonly used antihypertensive drugs for initial treatment are similar. Although certain β-blockers lack some randomized controlled trial evidence, the entire progression of hypertension is accompanied by overactivation of the sympathetic nervous system, and hypertensive patients with multiple comorbidities can benefit from β-blocker therapy.[240]

(1) CCBs: CCBs primarily work by blocking calcium ion channels in vascular smooth muscle cells, leading to vasodilation and blood pressure reduction. They are generally divided into two categories: dihydropyridine CCBs and non-dihydropyridine CCBs. In East Asia, dihydropyridine CCBs are the most widely used antihypertensive drugs due to their significant blood pressure-lowering effects, including reducing nighttime systolic blood pressure.[241] Long-acting dihydropyridine CCBs can improve nighttime and early morning blood pressure control and reduce blood pressure variability.[242,243]

Previous large-scale antihypertensive clinical trials conducted in China mainly used dihydropyridine CCBs as the study drugs. These studies have confirmed that antihypertensive regimens based on dihydropyridine CCBs can significantly reduce cardiovascular risk, particularly the risk of stroke, in hypertensive patients.[218,220,222]

Long-acting dihydropyridine CCBs can be combined with other commonly used antihypertensive drugs, making them especially suitable for elderly hypertensive patients, those with isolated systolic hypertension (ISH), stable angina, coronary or carotid atherosclerosis, and peripheral vascular disease.[244-246] Common side effects include reflex sympathetic activation leading to increased heart rate, facial flushing, ankle edema, and gingival hyperplasia. Short-acting nifedipine has more pronounced side effects and is prone to cause blood pressure fluctuations, so it is not recommended as a regular antihypertensive drug, nor should it be used in patients with acute coronary syndrome.[247] Dihydropyridine CCBs have no absolute contraindications, but they should be used with caution in patients with tachycardia and heart failure.

There are fewer randomized controlled trials (RCTs) involving non-dihydropyridine CCBs, and there is no substantial difference in improving cardiovascular outcomes compared to other antihypertensive drugs.[133] Non-dihydropyridine CCBs are suitable for hypertensive patients with comorbid conditions such as coronary artery disease with angina, increased heart rate, supraventricular tachyarrhythmias, chronic kidney disease (CKD), and for screening primary aldosteronism.[248] They can be combined with dihydropyridine CCBs to treat resistant hypertension. Non-dihydropyridine CCBs have stronger negative chronotropic and negative inotropic effects compared to dihydropyridine CCBs, and they may cause second- to third-degree atrioventricular (AV) block. Therefore, they are contraindicated in patients with heart failure and severe AV block. Verapamil and diltiazem, both non-dihydropyridine CCBs, are metabolized by cytochrome P450 3A4 (CYP3A4) and have more drug-drug interactions compared to dihydropyridine CCBs.[248]

(2) ACEIs: the mechanism of action of ACEIs is to inhibit ACE, thereby blocking the formation of angiotensin II (Ang II) and preventing the degradation of bradykinin, which contributes to their antihypertensive effect. ACEIs also promote the activity of ACE2, which blocks the degradation of angiotensin (1-7) [Ang (1-7)], further contributing to vasodilation and anti-proliferative effects.[249] Numerous large-scale clinical randomized controlled trials have shown that ACEIs provide significant protection to target organs and prevent cardiovascular events in hypertensive patients,[250] making them one of the most widely applicable antihypertensive drugs. ACEIs are particularly suitable for patients with left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH), chronic heart failure, chronic coronary artery disease, post-myocardial infarction heart dysfunction, CKD, and proteinuria.[249-251] The combination of diuretics and ACEIs is beneficial for the secondary prevention of ischemic stroke.[252]

The most common side effect of ACEIs is a dry cough, which typically occurs in the early stages of treatment. Patients with mild symptoms can continue taking the medication, but those who cannot tolerate it may switch to an ARB (angiotensin II receptor blocker). Other side effects include hypotension and rash. The incidence of potentially life-threatening angioedema caused by ACEIs is less than 1%, and it is even lower in China.[253] Rarely, taste disturbances may occur. Long-term use of ACEIs or ARBs in CKD patients may lead to elevated blood potassium levels, necessitating regular monitoring of serum potassium and creatinine levels. Contraindications include severe bilateral renal artery stenosis, hyperkalemia, and pregnancy.

(3) ARB: the mechanism of action of ARBs involves blocking the angiotensin type 1 (AT1) receptor to exert antihypertensive effects. The tighter the binding of ARBs to the AT1 receptor, the stronger the blockade of AT1 receptor function.[254] The elevated levels of Ang II in circulation can activate unblocked angiotensin type 2 (AT2) receptors, potentially providing cardiovascular benefits such as vasodilation and anti-proliferative effects. ARBs are widely used due to their good tolerability.

ARBs effectively reduce major cardiovascular events and mortality in patients with hypertension.[251,255] Both ARBs and ACEI can prevent the onset of new diabetes and atrial fibrillation.[255] They are more effective than other antihypertensive drugs in reducing proteinuria and can delay the progression of diabetic nephropathy.[133] ARBs are particularly suitable for patients with LVH, HFpEF, coronary heart disease, post-myocardial infarction, cardiac dysfunction, diabetic nephropathy, proteinuria, metabolic syndrome, and those who cannot tolerate ACEIs.[256]

ARBs should not be used in combination with ACEIs or direct renin inhibitors, as this does not provide additional benefits and may lead to excessive renal events and hyperkalemia.[256,257] Chronic use of ARBs in patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) requires regular monitoring of blood potassium and creatinine levels. ARBs are contraindicated in patients with severe bilateral renal artery stenosis, pregnancy, or hyperkalemia.

(4) Diuretics: diuretics primarily exert their antihypertensive effects by promoting sodium excretion, reducing volume load. Thiazide diuretics are classified into thiazide-type and thiazide-like diuretics. The former includes hydrochlorothiazide and bendroflumethiazide, while the latter includes indapamide and chlorthalidone. Thiazide-like diuretics can reduce intracellular calcium content in small arterial smooth muscle cells through Na+-Ca2+ exchange mechanisms, leading to arteriolar dilation, and they have a longer elimination half-life compared to thiazide-type diuretics.[258,259] In China, commonly used thiazide diuretics for hypertension are hydrochlorothiazide and indapamide.

Low-dose thiazide diuretics in combination with ACEIs or ARBs are preferentially recommended. Thiazide diuretics are especially suitable for elderly patients, those with salt-sensitive hypertension, isolated systolic hypertension (ISH), and heart failure; they are also one of the cornerstone drugs for resistant hypertension. It is generally believed that patients with an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 45 mL/min per 1.73 m² should use loop diuretics for blood pressure control.

The effectiveness of thiazide diuretics in preventing cardiovascular disease and mortality has been confirmed in randomized controlled trials and meta-analyses.[260-262] The PATS study demonstrated that indapamide treatment significantly reduced the risk of recurrent stroke.[255] The Diuretic Comparison Project compared the effects of thiazide-like and thiazide-type diuretics on hypertension outcomes. The results indicated no significant difference between the two types of drugs regarding major adverse cardiovascular outcomes, but in patients with a history of myocardial infarction or stroke, the chlorthalidone group had a lower risk of cardiac events and mortality.[263]

The metabolic adverse reactions of thiazide diuretics are closely related to the dose, so low doses are typically recommended. Long-term users should monitor blood potassium levels, and thiazide diuretics should be used with caution in patients with hyperuricemia and contraindicated in those with gout.

The potassium-sparing diuretic amiloride promotes sodium excretion but has weak antihypertensive activity; it has a synergistic effect when combined with thiazide diuretics (e.g., compound amiloride). It can also be used to treat Liddle syndrome. When used in combination with ACEIs or ARBs, the risk of hyperkalemia should be considered. Triamterene's potassium-sparing effect is weaker than amiloride's, and since low-dose thiazide diuretics rarely cause hypokalemia, it is now rarely used in combination.

(5) Beta-blockers: beta-blockers primarily lower blood pressure by inhibiting excessive sympathetic nervous activity, reducing myocardial contractility, and slowing heart rate. There are three subtypes of beta-blockers: non-selective beta-blockers, highly selective beta-1 receptor blockers, and alpha-1/beta receptor blockers, each with different characteristics. Compared to other antihypertensive drugs, except for the relatively lower effectiveness of atenolol in preventing stroke in the elderly,[264] beta-blockers are generally considered equally effective in preventing major cardiovascular events,[135] and remain one of the commonly used drugs for treating hypertension.[240]

Clinical studies and practice have shown that the antihypertensive efficacy of beta-blockers is relatively better in middle-aged and young populations,[265,266] however, they are not suitable for isolated systolic hypertension (ISH) with significantly increased pulse pressure. Beta-blockers are particularly suitable for patients with coronary heart disease, a history of myocardial infarction, chronic heart failure, aortic dissection, tachyarrhythmias, increased sympathetic activity (e.g., resting heart rate ≥ 80 beats/min,[240] and hyperdynamic states.[267] This class of drugs is also a primary antihypertensive medication for chronic kidney disease (CKD) and the perioperative period.

Common adverse reactions include fatigue, cold extremities, gastrointestinal discomfort, and potential effects on glucose and lipid metabolism. Highly selective beta-1 receptor blockers are safe for patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD).[268] For patients with abnormal glucose and lipid metabolism, alpha-1/beta receptor blockers and highly selective beta-1 receptor blockers are preferred, and they should not be combined with diuretics. Beta-blockers are contraindicated in patients with second or third-degree atrioventricular block or asthma, and should be used with caution in athletes and those with peripheral vascular disease. Abrupt discontinuation of long-term beta-blocker therapy may result in a withdrawal syndrome characterized by rebound effects; therefore, the dose should be gradually reduced before stopping the medication.

(6) ARNI: ARNIs inhibit neprilysin, preventing the degradation of natriuretic peptides, which results in diuresis, natriuresis, vasodilation, and sympathetic inhibition.[269] The angiotensin receptor blockade provided by ARNIs prevents the compensatory activation of the renin-angiotensin system (RAS) that might occur due to neprilysin inhibition, thereby exerting a synergistic antihypertensive effect.[269,270] Sacubitril/valsartan is the first ARNI, approved in China for the treatment of primary hypertension in June 2021. Sacubitril/azilsartan has completed phase II and phase III clinical trials.[271,272] Sacubitril is a prodrug that is metabolized into the active compound LBQ657 after enzymatic cleavage in the body. Sacubitril and valsartan are combined in a 1:1 molar ratio to form a co-crystal structure, which ensures that both drugs are absorbed and eliminated at similar rates, thereby maintaining the synchrony of their pharmacological effects.[273,274]

In multiple studies conducted in Asia and globally, sacubitril/valsartan was found to lower clinic systolic blood pressure by an additional 5-7 mmHg compared to ARBs[275] and further reduce 24-hour ambulatory systolic and diastolic blood pressure by 3.7 and 1.7 mmHg, respectively.[276] For hypertensive patients whose blood pressure remains uncontrolled after CCB treatment, combination therapy with ARNI + CCB further reduces 24-h ambulatory systolic and diastolic blood pressure by 13.1 and 7.7 mmHg, respectively, compared to CCB monotherapy.[277] A multicenter, non-controlled study in China confirmed that replacing RAS inhibitors with sacubitril/valsartan significantly reduced both clinic and 24-h ambulatory blood pressure in patients with resistant hypertension who did not have severe hepatic or renal impairment, thereby increasing the rate of blood pressure control.[278] This drug is also helpful in controlling resistant hypertension in patients with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), who were already on at least four antihypertensive medications, including mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs).[279] Sacubitril/valsartan has shown certain antihypertensive advantages in special populations, including those with hypertension combined with heart failure, LVH, renal disease, elderly patients, and those with resistant hypertension.[280]

The results of the phase III clinical trial of sacubitril/azilsartan indicated that in patients with mild to moderate hypertension, sacubitril/azilsartan 240 mg and 480 mg achieved greater reductions in clinic blood pressure, as well as 24-h, daytime, and nighttime systolic/diastolic blood pressure compared to olmesartan (480 mg, P ≤ 0.001), with a clear dose-dependent effect. Long-term (52 weeks) use data demonstrated good tolerability.[272]

ARNI can be combined with CCBs, beta-blockers, and diuretics in combination therapy, but should not be used with ACEIs, ARBs (except for ARBs with the same active ingredient), or aliskiren.[280] If switching from an ACEI to an ARNI, the ACEI must be discontinued for at least 36 hours (equivalent to 2-3 elimination half-lives for most ACEIs) before starting ARNI to reduce the risk of potential angioedema, while also minimizing the risk of heart failure worsening or significant blood pressure fluctuations.

ARNI may cause adverse reactions such as angioedema, hypotension, renal impairment, and hyperkalemia. ARNI should be used with caution in patients with severe renal impairment [eGFR < 30 mL/min per 1.73 m², renal artery stenosis, and moderate hepatic impairment, and is contraindicated in pregnant women.[280]

(7) Alpha-blockers: although not commonly used as first-line antihypertensive agents, alpha-blockers can play an important therapeutic role in various clinical situations. These drugs are suitable for patients with hypertension combined with benign prostatic hyperplasia, essential tremor, abnormalities in blood glucose or lipid levels, and resistant hypertension. They can also be used in patients with peripheral vascular disease or asthma. Alpha-blockers are particularly valuable in hypertensive emergencies, screening for primary aldosteronism, and preoperative blood pressure control in pheochromocytoma.[281] However, some patients may experience orthostatic hypotension, so it is recommended to use sustained-release formulations. Immediate-release tablets should be taken at bedtime. Alpha-blockers are contraindicated in patients with orthostatic hypotension and should be used with caution in patients with heart failure.

(8) Direct renin inhibitors: the mechanism of action of direct renin inhibitors involves directly inhibiting renin, which subsequently reduces the production of Angiotensin II (Ang II) and significantly lowers blood pressure in hypertensive patients.[282,283] These drugs also reduce plasma renin activity (PRA), block renin/prorenin receptors, and decrease intracellular Ang II production. The only currently available drug in this class is aliskiren, which has been shown to significantly improve organ damage such as hypertension-related proteinuria and left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH).[284,285] While aliskiren combined with ACE inhibitors (ACEI) or ARBs can reduce aldosterone escape and enhance clinical efficacy, it also increases the risk of hyperkalemia, hypotension, and renal impairment.[286,287] Aliskiren can be safely combined with hydrochlorothiazide or amlodipine. The most common adverse reactions are rash and diarrhea.

(9) Mineralocorticoid Receptor Antagonists (MRA): aldosterone, a mineralocorticoid hormone primarily secreted by the zona glomerulosa of the adrenal cortex, plays a significant role in hypertension and related diseases by binding to mineralocorticoid receptors (MR). Aldosterone promotes sodium retention and potassium excretion in the kidneys and may mediate and accelerate the damaging effects of Ang II, promoting vascular inflammation and fibrosis.[288] MRAs block the overactivation of MR, thereby lowering blood pressure and effectively protecting heart and kidney function.

MRAs can be classified into steroidal and non-steroidal types. Steroidal MRAs include spironolactone and eplerenone. These drugs bind to MR proteins less stably and cannot fully block MR overactivation.[288] Spironolactone, as a non-selective steroidal MRA, also inhibits androgen receptors, leading to adverse reactions such as gynecomastia and erectile dysfunction in men and menstrual irregularities in women.[289] Eplerenone, a steroidal MRA optimized for better MR selectivity, has only 1/40th of spironolactone’s affinity for MR. However, a significant proportion of patients with renal impairment still experience hyperkalemia. Finerenone, the first non-steroidal MRA to be marketed, changes the conformation of MR through its side chain, allowing stable binding to MR proteins with high MR selectivity and affinity.[290,291] Finerenone has lower affinity for androgen, glucocorticoid, progesterone, and estrogen receptors, thus reducing the incidence of adverse events.

MRAs are primarily used for treating primary aldosteronism, heart failure, and CKD. Some hypertensive patients may have pathophysiological mechanisms related to relative aldosterone excess, which are clearly associated with the development and progression of resistant hypertension. Spironolactone can be used as a fourth-line treatment in such cases, with efficacy superior to alpha and beta blockers.[292] Eplerenone can be chosen for patients who cannot tolerate spironolactone.[293] Finerenone effectively reduces systolic blood pressure in patients with CKD and resistant hypertension and has a better safety profile regarding hyperkalemia compared to spironolactone.

Both spironolactone and eplerenone reduce cardiovascular mortality and hospitalization rates due to worsening heart failure in patients with HFrEF.[128] Finerenone has a balanced tissue distribution between the heart and kidneys, making it more advantageous than steroidal MRAs in improving CKD outcomes.[294] Multiple studies have confirmed that, when added to standard therapy, finerenone significantly improves cardiorenal outcomes in patients with CKD and diabetes compared to placebo, leading to its approval for heart and kidney protection in patients with type 2 diabetes and CKD.[295]

Eplerenone and finerenone are primarily metabolized by CYP3A4. MRAs should not be used in combination with potassium supplements or potassium-sparing diuretics (amiloride, triamterene).

(10) Endothelin dual receptor antagonists: Endothelin dual receptor antagonists are a novel class of antihypertensive drugs that inhibit the binding of endothelin-1 (ET-1) to ETA receptors, leading to vasodilation and blood pressure reduction. These drugs also inhibit the binding of ET-1 to ETB receptors, reducing vascular permeability and helping to prevent water retention to some extent.[296]

Aprocitentan is currently the only endothelin dual receptor antagonist approved for use worldwide, specifically for adults with hypertension that is difficult to control with existing antihypertensive medications. After oral administration, aprocitentan reaches peak plasma concentrations in 3-4 h, and its plasma half-life is 41.3-46.8 h, allowing for once-daily dosing.[296]

In a phase III clinical trial of aprocitentan,[297] patients with resistant hypertension who continued to have systolic blood pressure of 140 mmHg or higher despite taking three antihypertensive medications, including a diuretic, were enrolled. The results showed that aprocitentan 12.5 mg and 25 mg had superior blood pressure-lowering effects compared to placebo at week 4, and the 25 mg dose continued to be effective in lowering blood pressure through week 40. Aprocitentan was well tolerated, with the most common adverse events being mild to moderate edema or fluid retention.

Aprocitentan may cause serious birth defects and is therefore contraindicated in pregnant women.

(11) Other drugs with antihypertensive effects: research and meta-analyses have shown that antidiabetic drugs such as metformin, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists (GLP-1RA), and sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) also have some weight loss and blood pressure-lowering effects.[128,298] For hypertensive patients who have indications for these drugs, they can be considered for combined use with antihypertensive medications.

5.2.2.4 Combination use of antihypertensive drugs

The combination use of antihypertensive drugs significantly enhances therapeutic efficacy and is an effective method to achieve blood pressure targets.[232] Most hypertensive patients require the use of two or more antihypertensive drugs to reach their target blood pressure.

(1) Indications for combination therapy

Combination therapy is indicated for patients with grade 2 or higher hypertension (≥ 160/100 mmHg) or those at high/very high cardiovascular risk who have a blood pressure 20/10 mmHg above the target level. Initial treatment with a combination of two antihypertensive drugs is recommended for these patients. For patients with grade 1 hypertension, low-dose combination therapy can also be considered. If the target blood pressure is not achieved, the dosage of the initial drugs can be increased, or a third or even a fourth antihypertensive drug can be added.

(2) Method of combination therapy

When combining two drugs, the mechanisms of action should be complementary, providing an additive antihypertensive effect while mitigating or offsetting adverse effects. For example, adding a low-dose thiazide diuretic to an ACEI or ARB can achieve or even surpass the antihypertensive effect that would be achieved by doubling the dose of the ACEI or ARB alone. Similarly, combining an ACEI or ARB with a dihydropyridine CCB yields similar results.

(3) Common Two-Drug Combination Schemes. (a) ACEI or ARB + Thiazide Diuretic: ACEIs and ARBs can slightly increase blood potassium levels, counteracting the hypokalemia commonly induced by long-term use of thiazide diuretics. The combination of an ACEI or ARB with a thiazide diuretic has a synergistic effect, enhancing the overall antihypertensive effect. (b) Dihydropyridine CCB + ACEI or ARB: CCBs primarily dilate arterial blood vessels, while ACEIs or ARBs dilate both small arteries and small veins, resulting in a complementary antihypertensive effect when used together. The common side effect of ankle edema with dihydropyridine CCBs can be alleviated or neutralized by ACEIs or ARBs. The Chinese Hypertension Intervention Efficacy Study (CHIEF) demonstrated that initial treatment with a low-dose, long-acting dihydropyridine CCB + ARB significantly improved blood pressure control rates in hypertensive patients.[234] (c) Dihydropyridine CCB + Thiazide Diuretic: The FEVER study confirmed that the combination of a dihydropyridine CCB and a thiazide diuretic can reduce the risk of stroke in hypertensive patients. (d) Dihydropyridine CCB + β-Blocker: Dihydropyridine CCBs dilate blood vessels and slightly increase heart rate, counteracting the vasoconstrictive and heart rate-slowing effects of β-blockers. Combining these two drugs can reduce the incidence of adverse reactions.

(4) Multiple-Drug Combination Schemes. (a) Three-Drug Combination Scheme: this scheme is created by adding another antihypertensive drug to the two-drug combinations mentioned above, with the most common being a combination of a dihydropyridine CCB + ACEI (or ARB) + thiazide diuretic. (b) Four-Drug Combination Scheme: Mainly used for patients with resistant hypertension, this scheme involves adding a fourth drug, such as a β-blocker, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist, triamterene, clonidine, or α-blocker, to the three-drug combination.

(5) Single-Pill Combination (SPC): SPC is an effective approach to achieving antihypertensive treatment. It typically consists of two or more antihypertensive drugs with different mechanisms of action. Compared to combination therapy with randomly selected medications, the advantages of SPC include ease of use, improved treatment adherence, and enhanced blood pressure-lowering efficacy.[236,299] When using SPC, attention should be paid to the contraindications and potential adverse reactions of its components.

New SPCs: these usually consist of two or three antihypertensive drugs with different mechanisms of action, most of which are taken once daily, making them convenient to use and improving adherence. 5.2.2.5 Adverse reactions of antihypertensive drugs

Most hypertensive patients require lifelong antihypertensive therapy, so it is crucial to minimize the adverse reactions associated with antihypertensive treatment. Some adverse reactions, such as fatigue, falls, and increased fractures, may occur, particularly in the early stages of treatment. Currently, antihypertensive drugs generally have fewer adverse reactions. However, when prescribing these medications, clinicians should consider not only the therapeutic effects but also the potential adverse reactions, especially in relation to the patient’s specific conditions, to avoid contraindications. Different classes of antihypertensive drugs have distinct adverse reactions.[300] The specific adverse reactions of various antihypertensive drugs are detailed in Table 19.[301] Many adverse reactions to antihypertensive drugs are dose-dependent; for example, hypokalemia from thiazide diuretics and ankle edema from calcium channel blockers (CCBs) are dose-related.

5.2.2.6 Health Economics of antihypertensive drug treatment

In the field of hypertension prevention and treatment, one of the fundamental issues that health economics aims to address is how to effectively allocate and utilize limited health resources to maximally meet the demand for hypertension prevention and control services, thereby enhancing the social and economic benefits of these services.

Over the years, China has conducted a series of research studies on the health economics of hypertension prevention and treatment, including: (1) the “Eleventh Five-Year” National Science and Technology Support Program, which involved a survey of the current status of medication costs, hospitalization expenses, and direct medical expenses for the health management of community hypertensive patients.[302]

(2) A comprehensive health economics evaluation of hypertension prevention and control in the Huangpu District of Shanghai and the Fangshan District of Beijing in 2002, involving a total sample size of 240,000 residents.[303]

(3) A multicenter, open, randomized, non-pharmacological hypertension management study conducted over a one-year period in community hospitals across four districts in Shanghai in 2006.[304]

(4) A comparative analysis in 2018 of the economic benefits of office blood pressure measurements and ambulatory blood pressure monitoring in locations such as Lanzhou, Shenzhen, Dongguan, and Zhejiang.[305]

The results of these studies indicate that effective hypertension prevention and treatment can significantly reduce overall medical expenses, conforming to cost-effectiveness principles. The economic outcomes are substantial, demonstrating high health economic benefits.

5.2.3 Device-based treatment for hypertension

Key Points 5F: Device-Based Treatment for Hypertension

• Current research supports the effectiveness andsafety of renal denervation (RDN) for treating hypertension.

• RDN can be considered for hypertensive patientswith drug-resistant hypertension or poor adherence to medication, after excluding secondary hypertension (Ⅱb, B).

• RDN should be performed in departments withextensive experience in the diagnosis and treatment of hypertension, and with the ability to differentiate the causesof secondary hypertension (ⅠC).

Despite advancements in pharmacological treatments for hypertension, poor medication adherence and low rates of blood pressure control remain significant challenges. There is still a strong clinical need for effective, convenient, and long-term non-pharmacological methods to reduce blood pressure. RDN works by disrupting the renal sympathetic nerves, reducing the sympathetic activity of the kidneys and the entire body, thereby lowering blood pressure. Several methods can be employed for RDN, with the main randomized clinical trials utilizing catheter-based radiofrequency energy, ultrasound energy, or local alcohol injections.

Radiofrequency ablation for RDN has demonstrated significant blood pressure reduction[306,309] and good safety[310-315] in randomized sham-controlled trials. Using intraluminal ultrasound energy ablation in sham-controlled trials has shown recent safety and efficacy, though long-term effects are still being monitored.[316-317] Research on the efficacy of alcohol-mediated renal denervation has indicated significant blood pressure reduction, but randomized sham-controlled studies have not confirmed its effectiveness, warranting further investigation.[318,319] Domestic RDN equipment companies in China have also conducted randomized sham-controlled trials with radiofrequency ablation, and their efficacy and safety results have been publicly presented at academic conferences.

Based on the current aggregate of sham-controlled clinical studies, the overall blood pressure reduction from RDN is modest, and it remains unclear whether it will result in the expected decrease in cardiovascular events. Thus, it is still difficult to accurately predict the antihypertensive effects for patients undergoing RDN. It is recommended that RDN be considered as an adjunctive treatment for hypertensive patients who have drug-resistant hypertension or poor medication adherence, after excluding secondary hypertension and with patient consent. RDN should be performed in an orderly manner in departments with extensive experience in hypertension management and capable of identifying the causes of secondary hypertension, along with providing training in hypertension diagnosis and procedural techniques.

Other device-based treatments for hypertension, such as implantable frequency-responsive dual-chamber cardiac pacemakers to modulate atrioventricular intervals,[320] baroreflex activation therapy,[321] iliac arteriovenous anastomosis, carotid body ablation, deep brain stimulation, and slow breathing exercises are still under investigation. Their safety and efficacy remain uncertain, and their clinical application prospects are not yet clear.

5.2.4 Application of traditional chinese medicine in antihypertensive treatment

Key Points 5G: Application of Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) in Antihypertensive Treatment

• For patients with high-normal blood pressurewho require pharmacological treatment and forthose with grade 1 hypertension, the use of TCMpreparations that have liver-calming and yang-suppressing properties, and have evidence-based support, can be considered to improve hypertension-related symptoms and provide some auxiliary antihypertensive effects. These TCM preparations canalso be used in combination with commonly usedantihypertensive drugs (Ⅱb, C).

5.2.4.1 TCM Understanding of Hypertension

According to the characteristics of hypertension and the clinical manifestations of patients, TCM diagnoses hypertension as “dizziness”, “headache”, “wind dizziness”, and “head wind” among other conditions. The earliest descriptions of related conditions are found in the “Huangdi Neijing” (The Yellow Emperor's Inner Canon), which mentions “tight pulse”, marking one of the earliest written records concerning vascular pressure.[322]

In modern TCM, in addition to monitoring blood pressure, greater emphasis is placed on hypertension-related clinical symptoms such as head swelling, headache, irritability, facial flushing, palpitations, insomnia, tongue coating, and pulse characteristics. As image analysis, quantitative vascular pressure collection, and analysis technologies are applied in tongue, eye, and pulse diagnosis, precise classification in TCM may become possible.[323]

TCM attributes the primary causes of hypertension to emotional distress, irregular diet, chronic illness, overexertion, and aging leading to physical weakness. The pathological factors are often wind, fire, phlegm, stasis, and deficiency, with the disease's nature typically being a deficiency in essence and excess in symptoms. The fundamental deficiency is liver and kidney yin deficiency, while the symptomatic excess is hyperactivity of liver yang, qi and blood disharmony, and internal accumulation of phlegm-dampness. Depending on the disease course and the extent of target organ damage, it often presents as hyperactivity of liver yang in the early stages, yin-yang imbalance in the middle stages, and deficiency in both yin and yang in the later stages, with blood stasis and phlegm-dampness possibly complicating the condition throughout the disease process. Overall, the most common patterns are yin deficiency with yang hyperactivity and failure of water to nourish wood, with the principle of calming yang and nourishing yin being widely applied in treatment.

In the 1960s and 1970s, research and development on the blood pressure-lowering effects of traditional Chinese medicines began, with in-depth studies focusing on the active ingredients of herbs such as Rauvolfia (Luo Fu Mu), Kudzu root (Ge Gen), Ligusticum chuanxiong (Chuan Xiong), and Stephania tetrandra (Fang Ji). These studies led to the clinical application of these herbs, playing a positive role in the prevention and treatment of hypertension in China.[324] Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) for treating hypertension still largely relies on syndrome differentiation and treatment. Some classic prescriptions, such as Tianma Gouteng Decoction, Banxia Baizhu Tianma Decoction, Zhenggan Xifeng Decoction, Liuwei Dihuang Pill, and Qiju Dihuang Pill, as well as modern formulations based on empirical evidence, are widely used in clinical hypertension treatment. This approach reflects the advantages of TCM’s holistic perspective and individualized treatment.

5.2.4.2 Recommended Chinese medicines for treating hypertension

For patients with high-normal blood pressure who require medication and for those with grade 1 hypertension, it is recommended to consider using Chinese patent medicines that have liver-calming and yang-suppressing properties and have been supported by evidence. These medicines can help improve hypertension-related symptoms and provide some auxiliary antihypertensive effects. They can also be used in combination with commonly prescribed antihypertensive drugs.

Tianma Gouteng Granules: These granules have the functions of calming the liver, extinguishing wind, clearing heat, and soothing the mind. A randomized placebo-controlled trial conducted on patients with masked hypertension demonstrated that after four weeks of treatment with Tianma Gouteng Granules, the daytime systolic/diastolic blood pressure assessed by ambulatory blood pressure monitoring decreased by 5.44/3.39 mmHg, which was 2.5/1.8 mmHg more than the reduction seen with placebo.[325] Another systematic review that included 15 clinical trials involving 1,508 patients with primary hypertension showed that the combination of Tianma Gouteng Granules with conventional Western medicine had a superior effect in lowering systolic blood pressure compared to Western medicine alone.[326]

Songling Xuemaikang Capsules: These capsules have the functions of calming the liver, suppressing yang, and soothing the heart. A multicenter, randomized, double-blind clinical trial with a positive drug control showed that after eight weeks of intervention, the clinic systolic/diastolic blood pressure decreased by 10.5/7.9 mmHg, which was not inferior to the control group taking 50 mg of losartan.[327] Another systematic review that included 27 clinical trials with a total of 3,100 patients indicated that the combination of Songling Xuemaikang Capsules with conventional Western medicine could further reduce systolic blood pressure by 7.88 mmHg and diastolic blood pressure by 7.85 mmHg.[328]

Yangxue Qingnao Granules: These granules have the functions of nourishing the blood, invigorating blood circulation, and calming the liver.[329] Bai’ai Capsules: these capsules have the functions of nourishing yin, cooling the blood, and calming the liver.[330] Xinmaitong Capsules: these capsules have the functions of invigorating blood circulation, unblocking the meridians, nourishing the heart, lowering blood pressure, and regulating lipids.[331] Clinical studies on the above-mentioned medicines also suggest that they have certain auxiliary antihypertensive effects.

5.2.4.3 Recommendations for appropriate techniques in traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) for treating hypertension

TCM-characteristic topical medications and non-pharmacological treatment methods are also widely applied in the clinical treatment of hypertension. There is a wealth of research related to acupuncture, which can be administered by physicians who have received acupuncture training. Points on the limbs and head and face are considered safer for treatment. A single-blind, randomized, sham acupuncture-controlled trial lasting 6 weeks confirmed that acupuncture intervention could reduce the 24-h average systolic/diastolic blood pressure by 6.4/3.7 mmHg at 3 months and by 5.4/3.0 mmHg at 6 months.[332]

5.2.4.4 Prospects for Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) in the prevention and treatment of hypertension

The TCM strategy of “preventing disease before it arises, preventing deterioration after onset, and preventing decline after transformation” plays a role in different stages of hypertension prevention, treatment, and rehabilitation by regulating the balance of yin and yang. The holistic concept and individualized treatment approach of TCM are particularly beneficial for managing the overall cardiovascular risk in hypertensive patients. The application of TCM in managing blood pressure can, on the one hand, provide auxiliary benefits in lowering blood pressure and, on the other hand, improve the clinical symptoms of hypertensive patients, thereby increasing their adherence to antihypertensive treatment.[333] Moreover, TCM offers some protection against target organ damage, such as improving early kidney damage through methods that nurture yin and calm yang,[334] and improving metabolic disorders in obese hypertensive patients through methods that clear the liver and nourish the kidneys.[335]

To further enhance the role of TCM in hypertension prevention and treatment, it is still necessary to clarify the indications for the independent use of TCM or in combination with commonly used antihypertensive drugs. High-quality evidence-based research focusing on cardiovascular events as endpoints should be conducted. Modern research methods that align with the characteristics of TCM, such as multi-omics technology, artificial intelligence, and big data algorithms, should be employed to elucidate the specific mechanisms of action and explore the establishment of new pathways for integrated TCM and Western medicine in the prevention and treatment of hypertension.

5.3 Management of Related Cardiovascular Risk Factors
5.3.1 Blood glucose control

Key Points 5H: Blood Glucose Control

• Blood Glucose Control Targets: Hemoglobin A1c(HbA1c) < 7%; fasting blood glucose 4.4–7.0mmol/L; 2-hour postprandial blood glucose orpeak glucose < 10.0 mmol/L (Ⅰ, A).

• For elderly patients, those prone to hypoglycemia, those with a long disease course, multiplecomorbidities or complications, shortened life expectancy, limited access to healthcare resources, orthose unable to monitor blood glucose, the bloodglucose control targets can be appropriately relaxed (Ⅱa, B).

• For most patients with type 2 diabetes, metformin is the first-line treatment. Regardless of whetherHbA1c is within the target range, patients with concomitant atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) or at high cardiovascular risk should useSGLT2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) or GLP-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RA); for patients with concomitantchronic kidney disease (CKD) or heart failure,SGLT2i should be used (Ⅰ, A).

Blood glucose control in hypertensive patients should emphasize the comprehensive management of multiple metabolic cardiovascular risk factors through healthy lifestyle practices and pharmacotherapy. The primary goal of blood glucose control is to achieve HbA1c levels below 7%, fasting blood glucose between 4.4 and 7.0 mmol/L, and postprandial blood glucose levels below 10.0 mmol/L. For specific populations, such as the elderly, those at risk for hypoglycemia, or those with advanced disease or complications, these targets may be adjusted to prevent hypoglycemic events and acute hyperglycemia.

Dietary adjustments: The dietary approach should focus on controlling total caloric intake, with carbohydrates accounting for 55%–65% of total calories and protein not exceeding 15%. Maintaining a normal weight is crucial, and smaller, more frequent meals can help control blood glucose levels and prevent hypoglycemia.

Exercise guidelines: Physical activity should be moderate, regular, and individualized. Isotonic exercises involving skeletal muscles, such as walking and swimming, are recommended, while avoiding vigorous activities or excessive exercise. Patients on insulin therapy should adhere to regular meal and exercise schedules.

Pharmacotherapy Principles:[336-338] (1) For most type 2 diabetes patients, metformin is the first-line treatment. If the patient is underweight or if metformin alone does not adequately control blood glucose, alternative or additional antidiabetic medications should be considered.

(2) For patients with HbA1c levels exceeding the treatment target by 1.5%–2.0%, initial combination therapy should be considered.

(3) The selection of antidiabetic medication should be based on the presence of complications and comorbidities. Patients with ASCVD or at high cardiovascular risk should use GLP-1RA or SGLT2i, provided there are no contraindications, regardless of whether their HbA1c targets are met.

(4) For patients with CKD, SGLT2i should be recommended when eGFR is ≥ 30 mL/min per 1.73 m² to reduce the risk of diabetic nephropathy progression and/or cardiovascular events. GLP-1RA can also be considered for patients with eGFR ≥ 30 mL/min per 1.73 m² to reduce the risk of new-onset macroalbuminuria. For patients with impaired renal function, antidiabetic medications with minimal renal excretion should be preferred, and insulin therapy should be used in cases of severe renal impairment.

(5) For patients whose blood glucose remains uncontrolled despite the use of two or more oral antidiabetic agents at moderate to high doses, a combination of oral antidiabetic agents with once-daily basal insulin (intermediate or long-acting insulin or their analogs) should be considered. Alternatively, before starting combination therapy with basal insulin, a combination of oral antidiabetic agents with GLP-1RA could be tried, with weekly GLP-1RA formulations being more patient-friendly than daily injections. If glycemic control is still inadequate, multiple daily insulin injections should be initiated.

(6) For newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes patients with fasting blood glucose > 11 mmol/L or HbA1c ≥ 9% accompanied by significant hyperglycemic symptoms, short-term (2 weeks to 3 months) intensive insulin therapy may be considered to rapidly control hyperglycemia and preserve β-cell function.

(7) Blood glucose control in type 1 diabetes patients relies on a consistent lifestyle and insulin therapy guided by self-monitoring of blood glucose levels.

(8) During antihypertensive treatment, the impact of antihypertensive drugs on blood glucose control should be considered. For example, the prolonged use of high-dose thiazide diuretics may lead to hyperglycemia, while β-blockers can mask symptoms of hypoglycemia such as tachycardia.

5.3.2 Lipid-lowering therapy

Key Points 5I: Lipid-lowering Therapy

• Combination therapy for hypertension and lipidcontrol: Patients with hypertension should receiveaggressive treatment combining antihypertensivetherapy with lipid-lowering therapy. Low-densitylipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) is the primary target of lipid-lowering therapy (Ⅰ, A).

• LDL-C target values based on ASCVD risk stratification: For hypertensive patients, LDL-C targetvalues should be determined according to their atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) riskstratification (Ⅰ, A). Statins are the first-line drugsfor lowering LDL-C (Ⅰ, A). For patients who do notreach target levels, cholesterol absorption inhibitors like ezetimibe or proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9) inhibitors can be added(Ⅰ, A).

• Triglyceride control: For hypertensive patientswith triglyceride levels ≥ 5.7 mmol/L, fibrates orOmega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids should beused to reduce the risk of pancreatitis (Ⅱa, B).

In China, 61.5% of hypertensive patients seen in outpatient settings also have dyslipidemia.[339] According to a 2015 study on disease burden,[340] hypertension and hypercholesterolemia are the two most significant risk factors for ischemic heart disease mortality in the Chinese population.

Controlling blood pressure alone is not sufficient to significantly reduce the ASCVD risk in hypertensive patients. Over the past 30 years, secondary prevention studies involving lipid-lowering drugs have shown that aggressive LDL-C reduction can improve cardiovascular outcomes in hypertensive patients.[341-343] Randomized controlled trials in primary prevention populations, such as the Anglo-Scandinavian Cardiac Outcomes Trial-Lipid-Lowering Arm (ASCOT-LLA),[344] the HOPE-3 trial,[345] and the Polycap Study,[346] have demonstrated that combining antihypertensive therapy with LDL-C reduction can further decrease the incidence of composite cardiovascular events by 20%-30%.[347] Therefore, early and aggressive treatment combining antihypertensive and LDL-C lowering therapies is crucial in reducing the burden of ASCVD.

LDL-C treatment goals: All hypertensive patients should undergo early screening for dyslipidemia and other ASCVD risk factors. The individual ASCVD risk stratification, as per the latest Chinese lipid management guidelines, should guide the determination of LDL-C target levels.[348] For patients with different risk levels, LDL-C treatment targets are provided. LDL-C is the primary target for lipid-lowering therapy, and non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (non-HDL-C) can be considered a secondary target if LDL-C is at goal.

Timing of LDL-C treatment: In hypertensive patients whose LDL-C levels exceed the target level corresponding to their ASCVD risk, lipid-lowering therapy should be initiated. Lifestyle interventions such as smoking cessation, weight control, low-salt and low-fat diets, and increased aerobic exercise should be emphasized. In patients at high ASCVD risk or above, lipid-lowering drug therapy should be combined with antihypertensive therapy to achieve dual control of blood pressure and LDL-C levels and maintain long-term treatment. In patients with moderate ASCVD risk and hypertension, LDL-C-lowering drug therapy should be considered if LDL-C levels are ≥ 2.6 mmol/L, alongside lifestyle modifications. For those with low ASCVD risk, lifestyle modifications and regular follow-up should be reinforced if LDL-C levels are ≥ 3.4 mmol/L.

Once the LDL-C target is reached, long-term maintenance therapy should continue. For patients who do not achieve the target LDL-C levels with moderate-intensity statin therapy, cholesterol absorption inhibitors such as ezetimibe can be added. Triglyceride-lowering therapy: Clinical findings indicate that even after aggressive control of LDL-C, residual ASCVD risk persists. Triglyceride-rich lipoproteins (TRLs) have been shown to significantly contribute to atherogenesis. Clinically, TRL levels can be assessed via triglyceride levels. Elevated triglyceride levels, even moderately, increase ASCVD risk. When triglycerides reach ≥ 5 mmol/L (443 mg/dL), the risk of pancreatitis escalates. For hypertensive patients with triglyceride levels > 2.3 mmol/L (200 mg/dL), statins should be initiated to reduce ASCVD risk. If, after achieving the LDL-C target, triglyceride levels remain between 2.3 and 5.6 mmol/L, adding fibrates or high-dose eicosapentaenoic acid ethyl ester (4 g/day) can be considered. For patients with triglyceride levels ≥ 5.7 mmol/L, if they are at low ASCVD risk, fibrates or Omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids should be immediately started. If the patient is at high ASCVD risk, fibrates or Omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids should be added to an optimized statin-based cholesterol-lowering regimen.

For hypertensive patients, enhancing long-term adherence to combined antihypertensive and lipid-lowering therapy is crucial to maximizing the benefits of ASCVD prevention and control.

5.3.3 Antiplatelet Therapy

Key Points 5J: Antiplatelet Therapy

• Recommendation for Hypertensive Patients withIschemic Cardiovascular and Cerebrovascular Diseases: antiplatelet therapy is recommended (Ⅰ, A).

• Consideration for Primary Prevention: Low-doseaspirin may be considered for primary preventionof ASCVD in patients aged 40–70 years who are athigh ASCVD risk and have at least one additionalrisk-enhancing factor, provided they do not have ahigh risk of bleeding (Ⅱb, B).

• Not Recommended: low-dose aspirin for primaryprevention of ASCVD is not recommended in patients with moderate-to-low ASCVD risk, or inthose aged <40 or >70 years (Ⅲ, C).

The role of antiplatelet therapy in secondary prevention of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases has been well-established through extensive clinical research, demonstrating a reduction in cardiovascular events by 19%–25%, including a one-third reduction in non-fatal myocardial infarction, a one-quarter reduction in non-fatal stroke, and a one-sixth reduction in fatal vascular events.[349,350]

Antiplatelet therapy should be actively considered in the following hypertensive patients: (1) hypertensive patients with ASCVD: Long-term secondary prevention with low-dose aspirin (100 mg/day) is recommended.[351,352] (2) Patients with acute thrombotic events: in cases of acute coronary syndrome, ischemic stroke, transient ischemic attack, or peripheral arterial occlusive disease, aspirin should be combined with a P2Y12 receptor inhibitor according to relevant guidelines. P2Y12 receptor inhibitors such as clopidogrel or ticagrelor can be used, typically with a loading dose during the acute phase (aspirin 300 mg, clopidogrel 300–600 mg, or ticagrelor 180 mg). Aspirin (100 mg/day) combined with clopidogrel (75 mg/day) or ticagrelor (180 mg/day) should be continued for 3–12 months, followed by long-term secondary prevention with low-dose aspirin (100 mg/day) or a P2Y12 receptor inhibitor.

Current evidence suggests that aspirin for primary prevention of ASCVD should be individualized based on a clinical benefit-risk assessment. The benefits of antiplatelet therapy in primary prevention are most pronounced in high-risk populations,[352-354] such as hypertensive patients with diabetes, chronic kidney disease (CKD), or those with a high cardiovascular risk (10-year total cardiovascular risk ≥ 10% or hypertension with three or more other risk factors) without a high risk of bleeding. For patients with moderate-to-low ASCVD risk, aspirin is not recommended for primary prevention.[353]

Currently, the recommended dose for aspirin in primary prevention is 75–100 mg/day.[125] When considering aspirin for primary prevention of ASCVD, it is not routinely recommended in patients over 70 years of age due to the greater risk of harm than benefit.[355] In patients younger than 40 years, there is insufficient evidence to assess the risk-benefit ratio. Clopidogrel can be used as an alternative in patients who cannot tolerate aspirin.

For hypertensive patients undergoing long-term aspirin therapy, the following precautions should be observed:[356,357] (1) aspirin should only be started after blood pressure is well-controlled, with levels below 150/90 mmHg. In patients whose hypertension is not adequately controlled, aspirin use may increase the risk of intracerebral hemorrhage.

(2) Enteric-coated aspirin is recommended to be taken on an empty stomach to minimize gastrointestinal side effects.

(3) If the patient has high-risk factors for gastrointestinal bleeding, such as a history of gastrointestinal diseases (e.g., peptic ulcer disease and its complications), age 65 or older, or concurrent use of corticosteroids, anticoagulants, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), preventive measures should be implemented. These include screening for and treating Helicobacter pylori infection, prophylactic use of proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), and adopting a rational strategy for combining antithrombotic agents.

(4) Aspirin should be used with caution or discontinued in patients with active peptic ulcer disease, severe liver disease, renal failure, or bleeding disorders.

(5) If severe gastrointestinal bleeding occurs during aspirin therapy, the medication should be discontinued immediately and managed according to protocols for bleeding. For milder cases, treatment with proton pump inhibitors may be sufficient.

5.3.4 Anticoagulant therapy for atrial fibrillation

Key Points 5K: Anticoagulant Therapy for Atrial Fibrillation

• Anticoagulation Indication: Hypertensive patients with atrial fibrillation and risk factors forthromboembolism should undergo anticoagulanttherapy (Ⅰ, A).

• Preferred Anticoagulants: Non-vitamin K antagonist oral anticoagulants (NOACs) are preferred forpatients with non-valvular atrial fibrillation (Ⅰ, A).

According to the latest epidemiological data, the age-adjusted prevalence of atrial fibrillation (AF) in China is 1.7% in men and 1.6% in women.[358] Hypertension is not only a significant risk factor for the development of AF[359] but also an important risk factor for non-valvular AF, stroke, and systemic embolism. Uncontrolled hypertension further increases the risk of bleeding in AF patients.

Screening and Assessment. Since AF can lead to various adverse outcomes, and many patients with AF are asymptomatic, there has been a strong emphasis on screening, especially opportunistic screening in patients over 65 years of age. This can be done using electrocardiograms, wearable devices, and blood pressure monitors with AF detection capabilities.[360] All hypertensive patients with non-valvular AF should be assessed for thromboembolism risk using the CHA2DS2-VASc score and bleeding risk using the HAS-BLED score. These assessments should be updated dynamically according to changes in the patient's condition.

Treatment Strategy. The treatment strategy focuses on three main aspects: (1) anticoagulant therapy: anticoagulant therapy should be initiated for patients at risk of stroke. (2) Rate or Rhythm Control: Implement rate or rhythm control to manage AF symptoms and improve quality of life. (3) Treatment of Underlying Conditions and Risk Factors: the treatment should include the management of underlying conditions and associated risk factors.

Structuring these treatment measures into an integrated management plan is the current trend in AF treatment. For hypertensive patients with AF and risk factors for thromboembolism, anticoagulant therapy should be administered according to the current guidelines.[360] NOACs have been shown to be non-inferior or superior to warfarin in clinical trials for non-valvular AF, with similar or lower bleeding complications and a significant reduction in intracranial hemorrhage.[361-364] Therefore, NOACs are recommended as the first-line treatment based on the patient’s indications and contraindications, with the appropriate drug type and dosage selected and regular follow-up ensured.[365] In cases where NOACs are not available or cannot be used, warfarin may be administered under the guidance of the international normalized ratio (INR), maintaining the INR within a range of 2.0 to 3.0. Special consideration and attention should be given when initiating or adjusting warfarin therapy, particularly due to the genetic characteristics of warfarin metabolism in the Chinese population, to ensure efficacy and avoid bleeding complications.

Symptomatic AF patients should be managed according to current guidelines with rate or rhythm control to improve symptoms and quality of life.[360] Recent studies suggest that for newly diagnosed AF patients (within one year) with stroke risk factors, aggressive rhythm control using radiofrequency ablation or antiarrhythmic drugs can improve outcomes.[366]

Multiple diseases and risk factors are associated with AF, and treatment should be guided by the latest guidelines. Blood pressure control should be prioritized in hypertensive patients with AF, aiming for target levels. For hypertensive patients at high risk of developing AF, such as those with left atrial enlargement, left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH), or reduced cardiac function, the use of renin-angiotensin system (RAS) inhibitors is recommended to reduce the incidence of AF.[360] However, there is no evidence currently supporting the use of these drugs for secondary prevention in reducing AF burden in patients who already have AF.

For blood pressure measurement in patients with AF, blood pressure monitors validated for accuracy in AF patients or mercury sphygmomanometers may be used. Regardless of the device used, it is recommended to take multiple readings and average the results.

5.3.5 Heart rate control

Key Points 5L: Heart Rate Control

• Resting Heart Rate Measurement: All hypertensive patients should have their resting heart ratemeasured when assessing blood pressure (Ⅰ, C).

• Definition of Increased Heart Rate: For hypertensive patients, an elevated heart rate is defined asa resting heart rate >80 beats/min in the clinic setting (Ⅱa, C).

• Investigation of Causes: When a hypertensive patient has an increased heart rate, potential triggersand underlying causes should be investigated (Ⅰ,C).

• Preferred Treatment: For hypertensive patientswith an elevated heart rate, β-blockers are the firstline treatment (Ⅰ, C).

An increased resting heart rate is likely an independent cardiovascular risk factor. Various national hypertension guidelines or consensus statements define an elevated heart rate as > 80 beats/min and recommend controlling the resting heart rate of hypertensive patients within 60-80 beats/min.[108,129,367,369] A cross-sectional survey involving 115,229 hypertensive patients from 21 cities in China showed that the average resting heart rate among Chinese hypertensive patients was 76.6 beats/min, with 38.2% of those with isolated hypertension having a heart rate ≥ 80 beats/min.[370] Multiple studies have shown that all types of elevated blood pressure are associated with sympathetic activation, which manifests as an increased heart rate, elevated plasma norepinephrine levels, and increased muscle sympathetic nerve activity.[371-373]

To date, 15 large cohort studies and randomized clinical trials have evaluated the relationship between resting heart rate and cardiovascular events or mortality in hypertensive patients, with 14 studies showing a positive correlation between increased heart rate and adverse outcomes.[374] The Kailuan study in China, which included 46,561 hypertensive patients not using β-blockers, found that all-cause mortality increased with higher baseline heart rates.[375] Compared to those with a baseline heart rate ≤68 beats/min, patients with a baseline heart rate > 82 beats/min had a 1.32 times higher risk of all-cause mortality. In the Xinan rural follow-up study in Henan, involving 5,540 hypertensive patients, the cardiovascular disease mortality risk ratios were 1.55 and 1.72 for those with heart rates of 80-89 beats/min and ≥ 90 beats/min, respectively, compared to those with heart rates of 60-69 beats/min.[376]

In studies related to heart failure and coronary heart disease, slowing the heart rate has been shown to improve outcomes,[377-381] but there is still a lack of randomized controlled trials evaluating the benefits of heart rate intervention in hypertension management. Nevertheless, heart rate management should be considered for hypertensive patients, with the following recommendations: (1) Resting Heart Rate Measurement: Resting heart rate should be measured in all hypertensive patients alongside blood pressure measurement. (2) Investigation of causes: when a hypertensive patient has an elevated heart rate, potential triggers and causes, including physiological, pathological, medication-induced, or disease-related factors, should be identified and addressed. (3) Pharmacological treatment: β-blockers are the first-line treatment for hypertensive patients with an elevated heart rate. It is recommended to preferentially choose cardioselective long-acting β1-blockers, such as bisoprolol or extended-release metoprolol. For patients with obesity, elevated blood glucose, or lipid abnormalities, the use of β-blockers with α1-blocking activity, such as carvedilol or labetalol, is recommended. For patients requiring two or more antihypertensive drugs, a β-blocker should be included. For those who cannot tolerate β-blockers, non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers (CCBs) can be used. (4) Target Heart Rate: (a) for hypertensive patients with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), the target resting heart rate should be < 70 beats/min (Ⅱa, B). (b) For hypertensive patients with atrial fibrillation, the initial ventricular rate should be < 110 beats/min, and if symptoms persist, the rate should be gradually reduced to < 100 beats/min or even < 80 beats/min (Ⅱa, B). (c) For hypertensive patients with chronic coronary heart disease, the target resting heart rate should be 55–60 beats/min (Ⅱa, C). (d) For hypertensive patients with acute aortic dissection, the target resting heart rate should be 60–80 beats/min (I, C).

5.3.6 Uric Acid-Lowering Therapy

Hypertension and hyperuricemia (HUA) often coexist, accompanied by an increased risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), heart failure, and renal insufficiency. A cross-sectional survey involving 33,785 outpatients in China indicated that the overall prevalence of HUA among hypertensive patients was 38.7%.[382] In adults, hyperuricemia is defined as serum uric acid levels ≥ 420 µmol/L in men and ≥ 360 µmol/L in women under normal dietary conditions.

For hypertensive patients with HUA, it is essential to first determine whether they are using antihypertensive medications that raise uric acid levels (e.g., thiazide diuretics) or other medications that can increase uric acid. For hypertensive patients with asymptomatic HUA, multiple serum uric acid tests should be conducted to identify the underlying cause.

Treatment goals: for hypertensive patients with hyperuricemia, the long-term control target for serum uric acid levels is < 360 µmol/L; for patients with concurrent gout attacks, the target is to maintain serum uric acid levels below 300 µmol/L; it is not recommended to reduce serum uric acid levels to below 180 µmol/L.[383-384]

Non-Pharmacological Treatment: Lifestyle interventions are crucial in managing hypertension with HUA. Specific recommendations include: (1) limiting high-purine foods, such as organ meats, seafood, nuts, and legumes; (2) restricting alcohol intake; (3) avoiding fructose-containing beverages or sugary drinks; (4) for patients with normal heart and kidney function, increasing water intake to maintain a daily urine output of 2-3 liters; (5) promoting a healthy diet with restricted daily caloric intake; (6) encouraging weight reduction; (7) engaging in regular, moderate exercise.

Pharmacological Treatment: In hypertensive patients with multiple cardiovascular risk factors, target organ damage, or high cardiovascular risk, it is recommended to combine active lifestyle interventions with uric acid-lowering drug therapy.[385] Commonly used uric acid-lowering drugs include those that inhibit uric acid synthesis, such as allopurinol and febuxostat, and those that promote uric acid excretion, such as benzbromarone.[386] Urine-alkalizing agents include citrate salts and sodium bicarbonate. Some evidence suggests that certain ARBs, ARNIs, CCBs may have beneficial effects in improving hyperuricemia.[382]

5.3.7 Comprehensive intervention for multiple risk factors

Hypertensive patients often present with multiple risk factors, making a combined intervention approach—incorporating health education, lifestyle modifications, and pharmacotherapy—the foundation for cardiovascular disease prevention. Simultaneously managing blood pressure, blood glucose, lipids, and body weight to achieve target levels is crucial in reducing cardiovascular risk. Hypertension accompanied by elevated homocysteine levels can lead to a higher incidence of stroke. A study on primary prevention of stroke in China indicated that supplementing folic acid while controlling blood pressure can reduce the incidence of stroke by 21%.[387]

5.4 Improvement and Reversal of Hypertension-Induced Target Organ Damage

Key Points 5M: Improvement and Reversal of Hypertension-Induced Target Organ Damage

• Assessment During Follow-Up: Regular evaluation of target organ damage during follow-up iscrucial for determining the effectiveness of treatment. Repeated assessments of target organ damage should be an essential part of hypertensionmanagement (Ⅰ, B).

• Impact of Antihypertensive Therapy: Antihypertensive treatment can reverse certain types oftarget organ damage or slow the progression ofsuch damage, thereby reversing the trend of increasing cardiovascular risk.

Upon diagnosing hypertension, target organ damage should be assessed to inform hypertension staging, cardiovascular risk stratification, and potentially influence the selection and decision-making regarding pharmacotherapy. Regular evaluation of target organ damage during follow-up helps in assessing the effectiveness of the treatment. Improvement in target organ damage may indicate successful treatment, while persistent or worsening damage suggests the need to review the treatment regimen and patient adherence. Therefore, repeated evaluations of target organ damage should be an integral part of follow-up care for hypertensive patients.

Early detection and timely initiation of antihypertensive treatment can reverse some types of target organ damage. Although target organ damage in long-standing hypertension may be irreversible, controlling blood pressure can still slow the progression of damage and reverse the trend of increasing cardiovascular risk.[109] A post hoc analysis of the losartan intervention for endpoint reduction in hypertension study showed that reversal of left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) by antihypertensive treatment can reduce the incidence of cardiovascular events and mortality.[388] Other studies suggest that in both diabetic and non-diabetic patients, reducing proteinuria through treatment can decrease the incidence of cardiovascular events and slow the progression of kidney disease.[389] There is also evidence that changes in estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) induced by treatment can predict cardiovascular events and the progression to end-stage renal disease (ESRD).[390] A large meta-analysis in 2020 showed that reduced progression of carotid intima-media thickness (IMT) is associated with a lower cardiovascular risk.[391]

The effects of different drugs on improving or reversing target organ damage may vary. CCBs can significantly improve arterial elasticity, reduce carotid IMT, and slow the progression of coronary or carotid atherosclerosis and peripheral vascular disease.[24,392,393] RAS inhibitors, including ACEIs, ARBs, and angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitors (ARNIs), are effective in improving LVH and reducing microalbuminuria and proteinuria.[394-396] Sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i), such as dapagliflozin and empagliflozin, can further reduce microalbuminuria on the basis of RAS inhibitors, slow the progression of kidney disease, and reduce the risk of cardiovascular and renal events.[397-399] The third-generation mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist (MRA), finerenone, has been shown to reduce microalbuminuria in patients with diabetic nephropathy and can reduce cardiovascular events in these patients.[400]

5.5 Follow-up for Hypertension Treatment
5.5.1 Purpose of follow-up

Follow-up is an essential component of hypertension management. The purposes of follow-up include evaluating the response to treatment, assessing patient adherence to medication and tolerance, analyzing whether blood pressure is stably controlled, monitoring the control of other risk factors, and checking for the development or changes in target organ damage. Additionally, follow-up helps establish a strong, trusting relationship between the healthcare provider and the patient.

5.5.2 Content of Follow-up

The main content of follow-up includes monitoring blood pressure, medication usage, and any adverse reactions. Additionally, it is crucial to pay attention to other risk factors, such as blood lipids and blood glucose, as well as to target organ damage and clinical conditions. Blood pressure should be measured in the clinic to understand the values and whether the target levels have been reached. Patients should be questioned about their adherence to medication, and adjustments to antihypertensive therapy should be made based on blood pressure fluctuations and any adverse reactions to the medication. Patients should be advised to take their medication on time, guided to improve their lifestyle, and encouraged to adhere to long-term treatment without discontinuing medication arbitrarily.

Home blood pressure monitoring (HBPM) is strongly encouraged, preferably using automated electronic blood pressure monitors that can measure, record, and store long-term, continuous HBPM data. Depending on the blood pressure phenotype, the variability between follow-up visits, and the need to assess 24-hour blood pressure control, the follow-up content may also include ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM). Evaluating target organ damage should be an important part of the follow-up process for hypertensive patients, as it can help determine the effectiveness of the treatment.

5.5.3 Follow-up Interval

During the first three months after initiating treatment, it is recommended to follow up with the patient once a month.[139] The follow-up interval can be adjusted based on the patient's overall cardiovascular risk and blood pressure levels. For patients with grade 1 hypertension classified as low or moderate risk, or those taking only one medication, follow-up can occur every 1 to 3 months. For complex cases, high-risk patients, or those with clinical symptoms, the follow-up interval may be shortened to every 2 to 4 weeks.[401] For patients whose blood pressure has reached and stabilized at target levels, follow-up should occur every 1 to 3 months. For those whose blood pressure has not reached target levels, follow-up should occur every 2 to 4 weeks. If blood pressure remains uncontrolled despite the use of at least three antihypertensive medications, the patient should be referred to a hypertension specialist for further management.
