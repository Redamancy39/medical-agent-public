New telemedicine technologies and internet-based healthcare can help improve hypertension management during follow-up and may allow for appropriately extending the follow-up intervals.

6 Hypertension with Special Phenotypes
6.1 White Coat Hypertension

Key Points 6A: White Coat Hypertension

• White coat hypertension is common in elderly individuals, those with grade 1 hypertension, and patients with resistant hypertension.

• For patients with elevated clinic blood pressurebut normal home blood pressure, standardized ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM) is recommended (Ⅰ, B).

• For patients who are not on antihypertensivetreatment, active lifestyle interventions should beimplemented to prevent and delay the onset of sustained hypertension (Ⅰ, A); for those already on antihypertensive treatment, there is no need to intensify antihypertensive therapy (Ⅲ, C).

6.1.1 Prevalence and clinical characteristics

The prevalence of white coat hypertension varies among different populations and is more common in the elderly. In the general population, the prevalence ranges from 10% to 15%, while among patients with clinic hypertension, 30% to 40% have white coat hypertension.[402] White coat hypertension can occur in patients with any grade of clinic hypertension, but it is most prevalent in those with grade 1 hypertension.[403] Among patients with resistant hypertension, 20% to 40% may exhibit white coat uncontrolled hypertension.[404]

In a medical setting, blood pressure measured by healthcare personnel may be influenced by the patient's stress and heightened alertness, leading to activation and dysregulation of the sympathetic nervous system and the renin-angiotensin system (RAS).[402] White coat hypertension is also associated with decreased arterial elasticity.[405] In the elderly, increased arterial stiffness, diminished baroreceptor function, and increased blood pressure variability (BPV) contribute to the white coat effect. Additionally, white coat hypertension is often accompanied by metabolic disorders, including elevated body mass index, blood glucose, blood lipids, and serum uric acid.[402] White coat hypertension may be a precursor to sustained hypertension, with a 2.5 to 2.8 times higher likelihood of progression to sustained hypertension compared to normotensive individuals.[406,407]

6.1.2 Definition and diagnosis

White coat hypertension is diagnosed when clinic blood pressure is elevated (≥ 140/90 mmHg) but 24-hour ambulatory blood pressure (< 130/80 mmHg), daytime blood pressure (< 135/85 mmHg), and nighttime blood pressure (< 120/70 mmHg) are normal, or when the average blood pressure over 7 days of home monitoring is normal (< 135/85 mmHg). For patients already on antihypertensive treatment who exhibit these blood pressure characteristics, the condition is referred to as white coat uncontrolled hypertension.[80,92]

Both ABPM and home blood pressure monitoring (HBPM) can be used to diagnose white coat hypertension, though discrepancies between these methods may occur. White coat hypertension with normal ABPM and HBPM has a lower cardiovascular risk than isolated normal out-of-office blood pressure.[408] Therefore, when home blood pressure is normal, further confirmation with ABPM is recommended when feasible.[409] Diagnosis using strict criteria that require normal blood pressure across 24 hours, including daytime and nighttime periods, is preferable. White coat hypertension with normal blood pressure throughout the 24-hour period does not show a significantly increased risk of adverse events compared to normotension.[410] Additionally, the reproducibility of the diagnosis based on a single clinic and out-of-office blood pressure measurement is low. Patients with a confirmed diagnosis through repeated measurements tend to have less target organ damage and a lower cardiovascular risk.[411]

6.1.3 Target organ damage and cardiovascular prognosis

Patients with white coat hypertension may exhibit damage to multiple organs, including the heart, brain, kidneys, and blood vessels, such as increased left ventricular mass index (LVMI), enlarged left atrial diameter, impaired diastolic function, increased carotid intima-media thickness (IMT), and elevated urinary albumin excretion rate. However, the extent of damage and disease risk in these patients is between that of normotensive and sustained hypertensive patients, being significantly lower than in those with sustained hypertension.[108]

The prognosis of patients with white coat hypertension varies across studies, possibly due to differences in the definition of white coat hypertension and the methods of blood pressure measurement used in the research. Patients diagnosed with white coat hypertension based on a daytime ABPM < 135/85 mmHg show a significantly higher risk of cardiovascular events compared to normotensive individuals, while those diagnosed based on a 24-h ABPM < 130/80 mmHg do not exhibit a significantly increased risk.[412] Moreover, patients with white coat hypertension who are at high cardiovascular risk, particularly those aged ≥ 60 years, have a significantly higher risk of cardiovascular disease compared to high-risk normotensive individuals.[413]

Overall, strictly defined white coat hypertension in untreated patients—where blood pressure is normal across 24 h, during the day, and at night, or where both home and ambulatory blood pressure are normal—carries a cardiovascular risk similar to that of normotensive individuals and significantly lower than that of sustained hypertensive patients. However, “partial” white coat hypertension—where only daytime or home blood pressure is normal, or where additional risk factors, target organ damage, or high cardiovascular risk are present—has a significantly higher cardiovascular risk than normotension, though still lower than that of sustained hypertension.[414]

6.1.4 Treatment principles

Due to the increased risk of progression to sustained hypertension and the frequent association of white coat hypertension with adverse metabolic factors, it is recommended that patients with untreated white coat hypertension undergo active lifestyle interventions, such as salt reduction, weight loss, and increased physical activity, to prevent and delay the onset of sustained hypertension. Additionally, these patients should be actively screened for target organ damage and cardiovascular risk, and managed according to the risk stratification guidelines recommended for white coat hypertension. Regular follow-up is essential, with out-of-office blood pressure reassessed annually, including 24-h ABPM and HBPM, to detect progression to sustained hypertension and initiate antihypertensive drug therapy promptly.

For patients with “white coat uncontrolled hypertension” already receiving antihypertensive treatment, further intensification of antihypertensive therapy is not recommended.[415-417] Future randomized controlled trials are needed to explore whether antihypertensive treatment is necessary for elderly patients with white coat hypertension who have target organ damage or are at high cardiovascular risk, as well as to conduct long-term follow-up studies to observe the outcomes and progression of white coat hypertension in younger patients.

6.2 Masked Hypertension

Key Points 6B: Masked Hypertension

• Masked hypertension is closely associated withtarget organ damage and adverse outcomes.

• Screening should be emphasized in individualswith high-normal clinic blood pressure, smokers,those with obesity, diabetes, or chronic kidney disease (CKD) (Ⅰ, B).

• Diagnosis should be confirmed based on homeblood pressure monitoring (HBPM) or 24-hour ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM) in addition toclinic blood pressure measurements(Ⅰ, B).

• Consider initiating or intensifying antihypertensive drug therapy along with lifestyle modifications(Ⅱa, B).

6.2.1 Prevalence and Clinical Characteristics

The prevalence of masked hypertension varies among different populations and based on the method of out-of-clinic blood pressure measurement used.[418] In the general population, the prevalence ranges from 10% to 20%,[419] while among patients already receiving antihypertensive treatment, it can be as high as 30% to 40%.[420] A study on outpatient blood pressure registration in China showed that the prevalence of masked hypertension diagnosed via home blood pressure was lower than that diagnosed via ambulatory blood pressure. Among untreated patients, the prevalence was 13.1% and 17.8%, respectively, and among treated patients, it was 14.5% and 18.7%, with statistically significant differences.[124]

The mechanisms underlying masked hypertension are not yet fully understood. Factors that may contribute to elevated out-of-clinic blood pressure include smoking, mental stress, and high salt intake, as well as conditions such as renal sodium retention, metabolic syndrome, and sleep apnea, which can cause elevated nighttime blood pressure. Studies in China have found that the risk of developing masked hypertension is associated with being male, increasing age, and higher body mass index (BMI).[419] Smoking in men is significantly associated with daytime ambulatory hypertension and nighttime masked hypertension detected via home monitoring.[187] In a multicenter international study on ambulatory blood pressure, the prevalence of masked daytime hypertension in prehypertensive individuals was 3.9 times higher than in those with normal blood pressure, and in diabetic patients.[421] it was 1.6 times higher compared to non-diabetic individuals.[420]

For masked uncontrolled hypertension, the condition may be related to the effectiveness of the antihypertensive treatment regimen, the half-life of the antihypertensive drugs, and the duration of blood pressure control. Patients using short- or intermediate-acting antihypertensive drugs may show well-controlled clinic blood pressure but ineffective control of nighttime and early morning blood pressure, resulting in masked uncontrolled hypertension.

6.2.2 Definition and classification

Masked hypertension is defined as having normal clinic blood pressure but elevated 24-h ambulatory blood pressure and/or home blood pressure.[80] Based on the timing of the elevated blood pressure, masked hypertension can be classified using 24-h ambulatory blood pressure and home blood pressure, as shown in Table 21. If the patient is not on antihypertensive medication and exhibits these characteristics of clinic and out-of-clinic blood pressure, the condition is referred to as masked hypertension. If the patient is already on antihypertensive medication, it is termed masked uncontrolled hypertension.[80]

Masked hypertension generally has moderate short-term reproducibility. Approximately two-thirds of patients will still have masked hypertension upon re-evaluation after four weeks, while the remaining one-third may revert to normal blood pressure or progress to sustained hypertension.[422] It is recommended to perform repeated clinic and out-of-clinic blood pressure measurements within one month to confirm the diagnosis.

6.2.3 Target organ damage and cardiovascular prognosis

A significant body of research both domestically and internationally indicates that masked hypertension is closely associated with damage to multiple organs, including the heart, brain, kidneys, and blood vessels. The prevalence of target organ damage in patients with masked hypertension is significantly higher than in those with normal blood pressure and is comparable to that of patients with sustained hypertension.[423] Masked hypertension is closely associated with adverse outcomes such as cardiovascular events and mortality. This is true for both isolated daytime hypertension and isolated nocturnal hypertension.[82] Whether diagnosed via ambulatory blood pressure monitoring or HBPM,[424] masked hypertension is associated with approximately double the risk of cardiovascular disease compared to normal blood pressure, even when clinic blood pressure is within the ideal range (< 120/80 mmHg).[6]

6.2.4 Treatment principles

For patients with masked hypertension, it is important to implement active lifestyle interventions and promptly initiate or intensify existing antihypertensive drug therapy. However, there is currently no direct randomized controlled trial evidence to confirm the benefits of antihypertensive treatment for patients with masked hypertension, so most treatment recommendations are classified as Ⅱa-Ⅱb, with C-level evidence.[108,144]

Hypertension prevention trials in individuals with high-normal blood pressure, such as those using ramipril or candesartan, may provide some insight into the potential benefits of treating masked hypertension. These studies have shown that antihypertensive treatment significantly reduced the incidence of clinic hypertension.[425,426] Additionally, in patients with clinic normotension (< 140/90 mmHg) and diabetes, antihypertensive treatment with nifedipine or enalapril significantly delayed the progression of proteinuria, diabetic nephropathy, and stroke.[427,428]

Currently, related clinical trials are being actively conducted both domestically and internationally. A single-center, randomized, placebo-controlled trial in China showed that the traditional Chinese medicine preparation Tianma Gouteng granules (5 g/dose, taken twice daily) effectively reduced daytime ambulatory blood pressure in patients with masked hypertension.[325] Preliminary analysis of a clinical trial on the protective effects of antihypertensive treatment on target organs in patients with masked hypertension, conducted by Chinese researchers and published in 2024, suggests that antihypertensive treatment based on azilsartan can significantly improve target organ damage in patients with masked hypertension and effectively lower both clinic and out-of-clinic blood pressure levels.[429]

6.3 Morning Hypertension

Key Points 6C: Morning Hypertension

• Morning hypertension is a strong predictor of target organ damage and cardiovascular risk.

• Morning hypertension is defined as morningblood pressure ≥ 135/85 mmHg, measured byhome blood pressure monitoring (HBPM) or ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM).

• The target level for morning blood pressure canbe set to < 135/85 mmHg (Ⅱa, C).

• Use of true long-acting antihypertensive drugs,taken once daily, is recommended to control 24-hour blood pressure and prevent poor control ofmorning blood pressure due to inappropriate treatment regimens (Ⅰ, B).

6.3.1 Definition and Epidemiology

Morning blood pressure refers to the blood pressure measured within 1 h after waking up, before taking medication, and before breakfast, either by home blood pressure monitoring or as recorded during the first 2 h after waking up by ambulatory blood pressure monitoring. A certain range of increase in morning blood pressure is considered physiological, but if the HBPM or ABPM morning blood pressure is ≥ 135/85 mmHg, it is classified as morning hypertension, regardless of clinic blood pressure and other time-period blood pressure levels.[430] For patients already on antihypertensive therapy, this condition can be defined as “uncontrolled morning hypertension”. Morning hypertension can be categorized into two types: “morning blood pressure surge”, characterized by a significant increase in morning blood pressure, and “sustained nighttime and morning hypertension”, where blood pressure remains elevated from night into the morning.

Morning hypertension is more common in Asian populations compared to European populations. Studies have shown that the morning blood pressure surge is significantly higher in Asian populations (40.1 mmHg) compared to European populations (23.0 mmHg).[431] A dynamic blood pressure registry study in China published in 2023 revealed that the morning blood pressure control rate among treated hypertensive patients was 34.1%.[87] Two studies conducted in Japan reported detection rates of morning hypertension in treated hypertensive patients to be 43.6% and 60.7%, respectively.[432,433]

6.3.2 Assessment methods

Morning hypertension can be assessed using HBPM and ABPM. HBPM requires measurement within 1 hour after waking up, after urination, before taking medication, and before breakfast, with two readings each morning for more than 5 days, yielding more than 10 readings in total, and using the average of all readings. ABPM involves averaging the blood pressure readings taken within the first 2 hours after waking up (one reading every 30 minutes, for a total of 4 readings). It is recommended to use validated upper-arm home blood pressure monitors and ambulatory blood pressure monitors to measure morning blood pressure.

Morning blood pressure surge can also be used to assess morning hypertension, typically represented by the difference between morning blood pressure and the lowest nighttime blood pressure, or the difference between blood pressure before and after awakening. These are referred to as sleep-trough morning surge and pre-awakening surge, respectively. However, there is no unified definition or calculation method for morning blood pressure surge, and it is not widely applicable in clinical practice due to poor reproducibility when assessed with 24-h ABPM. Compared to morning blood pressure surge, morning hypertension has a standardized diagnostic criterion, can be diagnosed using HBPM or ABPM, is easy to operate, and is widely applicable in clinical practice.

6.3.3 Target organ damage and cardiovascular prognosis

Patients with morning hypertension have an increased relative risk of carotid atherosclerosis. Morning blood pressure surge is also significantly associated with aortic pulse wave velocity (PWV), left ventricular mass index (LVMI), and left ventricular diastolic function. Among hypertensive patients, reducing home-measured morning blood pressure is more predictive of improvement in albuminuria than reducing clinic blood pressure. A cohort study with an average follow-up of 32 months showed that home-measured morning blood pressure had the highest predictive value for deterioration in renal function.

In elderly hypertensive patients, prospective studies have shown that morning blood pressure is the strongest independent predictor of stroke events. Other studies indicate that regardless of clinic blood pressure, home morning hypertension is associated with higher cardiovascular risk. Among patients whose clinic systolic blood pressure is controlled to < 130 mmHg, those with morning systolic blood pressure ≥ 145 mmHg have a 1.5 times higher risk of cardiovascular events, and those with morning systolic blood pressure ≥ 155 mmHg have a 5 times higher risk of stroke and a 5.2 times higher risk of coronary artery events, compared to those with morning systolic blood pressure < 125 mmHg.[60]

6.3.4 Treatment Principles

The treatment principles for morning hypertension include: (1) routine monitoring of morning blood pressure using HBPM and/or ABPM in all hypertensive patients to promptly detect morning hypertension; (2) setting the target level for home and ambulatory morning blood pressure to < 135/85 mmHg; (3) using true long-acting antihypertensive drugs, taken once daily, to control 24-hour blood pressure and avoid poor morning blood pressure control due to inappropriate treatment regimens; (4) for patients with isolated morning hypertension, there is currently no evidence from interventional studies, so it is recommended to adjust medication timing individually based on both nighttime and morning blood pressure.

6.4 Nocturnal Hypertension

Key Points 6D: Nocturnal Hypertension

• Definition: Nocturnal hypertension is defined asa nighttime average systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥120 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥70 mmHg, as measured by ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM).

• Evaluation Method: 24-hour ABPM is the standard method for evaluating nighttime blood pressure and diagnosing nocturnal hypertension.

• Target Levels: Ideally, nocturnal blood pressureshould be controlled to < 120/70 mmHg (Ⅱa, C).

• Treatment Recommendations: Long-acting antihypertensive medications in adequate doses orcombination therapy are recommended for controlling nocturnal hypertension (Ⅰ, B).

6.4.1 Definition and epidemiology

Nocturnal hypertension is defined by ABPM as an average nighttime SBP ≥ 120 mmHg and/or DBP ≥ 70 mmHg, regardless of whether the blood pressure pattern is dipper or non-dipper, and irrespective of daytime blood pressure levels.[84,108]

Nocturnal hypertension can be categorized into two types: “sustained day-night hypertension” and “isolated nocturnal hypertension”. The former refers to an ABPM-determined nighttime average SBP/DBP ≥ 120/70 mmHg with a daytime average SBP/DBP ≥ 135/85 mmHg. The latter refers to a nighttime average SBP/DBP ≥ 120/70 mmHg, but daytime SBP/DBP < 135/85 mmHg, making it more concealed and harder to detect. For patients already on antihypertensive medication, if the nighttime average SBP/DBP is ≥ 120/70 mmHg but the daytime SBP/DBP is < 135/85 mmHg, this condition is termed “uncontrolled nocturnal hypertension”.

Large-scale epidemiological data on the prevalence of nocturnal hypertension are currently lacking. A cohort study in China reported a detection rate of 10.9% for isolated nocturnal hypertension,[81] significantly higher than in Western populations (6.0%–7.9%).[82] Nocturnal hypertension is more common in elderly individuals, those with diabetes, chronic kidney disease (CKD), and other salt-sensitive populations.[68,434] A cohort study among CKD patients in China revealed that 73% of CKD patients had nocturnal hypertension.[435] Additionally, data from a dynamic blood pressure registry in China showed that among treated hypertensive patients, the control rate for nocturnal blood pressure was relatively low, at only 27.6%.[87]

6.4.2 Diagnosis and detection methods

24-h ABPM is the standard method for evaluating nighttime blood pressure and diagnosing nocturnal hypertension. It is recommended to define the nighttime period based on the sleep schedule recorded by the patient on the day of ABPM. If no sleep schedule is recorded, a default time period such as 23:00–05:00 can be used. Blood pressure should be measured every 30 min during the nighttime period, with at least seven valid readings considered the standard for qualifying ABPM nighttime blood pressure measurement. If conditions allow, it is recommended to repeat 24-h ABPM every 3 to 6 months or sooner to confirm the diagnosis, especially for patients who had poor sleep during the initial monitoring. In special cases, such as for hemodialysis patients, 44- to 48-h ABPM is recommended.[436]

In recent years, some new upper-arm electronic home blood pressure monitors and wrist blood pressure monitors have also been used for home nighttime blood pressure assessment. These devices are useful for screening nocturnal hypertension and conducting long-term follow-up on patients.[434]

6.4.3 Target organ damage and cardiovascular prognosis

Elevated nighttime blood pressure is closely associated with asymptomatic target organ damage, such as increased pulse wave velocity (PWV), carotid intima-media thickness (IMT), and left ventricular hypertrophy, as well as with increased risks of cardiovascular and renal events in normotensive individuals, hypertensive patients, those with diabetes, and CKD patients. Compared with those with normal nighttime blood pressure, patients with nocturnal hypertension are more likely to experience changes in cardiac and carotid artery structure.[437] Two large studies have shown that isolated nocturnal hypertension is associated with a higher risk of target organ damage and adverse outcomes.[437,438] Multiple studies and meta-analyses have confirmed that nighttime blood pressure has a stronger predictive ability for adverse outcomes, including cardiovascular events and mortality, than daytime blood pressure,[439] and even stronger than 24-h blood pressure.[58]

6.4.4 Treatment principles

Currently, there is a lack of large-scale randomized controlled trial evidence on the optimal target levels for treating nocturnal hypertension and improving prognosis. However, given that elevated nighttime blood pressure significantly increases cardiovascular risk and that antihypertensive treatment has shown consistent cardiovascular benefits across different populations, the general principles for treating hypertension also apply to nocturnal hypertension.

In principle, the average nighttime blood pressure should be controlled to < 120/70 mmHg. Adjustments may be made based on the patient's tolerance, especially for those with coronary artery disease, frailty, or elderly hypertensive patients.

Methods for improving nocturnal hypertension include: (1) actively treating primary diseases and accompanying clinical conditions such as primary aldosteronism, diabetes, CKD, and obstructive sleep apnea syndrome (OSAS); (2) lifestyle interventions, such as a low-sodium, potassium-rich diet, improving sleep, and weight reduction, can help control nighttime blood pressure; (3) it is recommended to use long-acting antihypertensive drugs in full doses or a combination of two or more drugs; (4) if conditions allow, priority should be given to medications that have been shown to effectively control nighttime blood pressure; (5) whether taking antihypertensive medication before bedtime can more effectively control nocturnal hypertension requires further research.

6.5 Isolated Systolic Hypertension (ISH)

Key Points 6E: Isolated Systolic Hypertension (ISH)

• Recommended Blood Pressure Targets for Elderly ISH: The target systolic blood pressure (SBP)for elderly patients with ISH is 140-150 mmHg (Ⅰ,A). If well-tolerated, lowering SBP to 130-140 mmHg is also reasonable (Ⅱa, B).

• Avoiding Low Diastolic Pressure: It is recommended to avoid lowering diastolic blood pressure(DBP) to < 70 mmHg (Ⅲ, C) to prevent inadequateorgan perfusion. Controlling SBP remains theprimary goal to improve the prognosis of these patients (Ⅰ, C).

• Management of ISH in Adolescents: Adolescents with ISH should undergo active lifestyle interventions and close follow-up (Ⅰ, C). If elevated outof-office blood pressure, high central arterial pressure, or other risk factors are present, medicationshould be considered (Ⅱa, C).

ISH is defined as clinic SBP > 140 mmHg with DBP < 90 mmHg. It is the most common blood pressure phenotype in patients over 50 years old and can also occur in adolescents.[440]

6.5.1 Diagnosis of ISH in the elderly

As individuals age, SBP gradually increases, while DBP peaks at 50-60 years of age and then either plateaus or decreases.[441,442] In China, the prevalence of ISH among people aged 60 years and older is 30.33%.[443]

Elderly ISH is characterized by high blood pressure variability (BPV) and often exhibits a pronounced white coat effect during clinic blood pressure measurements.[444] Therefore, multiple clinic blood pressure measurements or out-of-office blood pressure monitoring, including central arterial pressure measurement, is recommended to detect ISH.

6.5.2 Cardiovascular prognosis of elderly ISH

Extensive evidence suggests that after the age of 50, SBP has a greater impact on prognosis than DBP.[445,446] ISH is associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular events and mortality, even in patients with grade 1 ISH.[447] This risk is even more pronounced in those with orthostatic hypertension.[448] Randomized controlled trials have confirmed the benefits of antihypertensive treatment for ISH.[218,449,450] A meta-analysis of individual data from elderly ISH patients showed that active treatment reduced all-cause mortality by 13%, cardiovascular mortality by 18%, and all cardiovascular outcomes by 26%.[450] The benefits were more significant in men, patients over 70 years old, and those with previous cardiovascular complications. The study also indicated that starting treatment early after ISH detection leads to sustained cardiovascular protection, regardless of age.

6.5.3 Blood pressure targets for elderly ISH

The target SBP and DBP for elderly hypertensive patients have been a subject of debate. Based on the latest clinical research and meta-analyses, a target SBP of 140-150 mmHg is recommended. If well-tolerated, lowering SBP to 130-140 mmHg is also reasonable.

It is recommended to avoid lowering DBP to < 70 mmHg to strike a balance between SBP and DBP, thereby preventing inadequate organ perfusion. However, controlling SBP remains the primary goal to improve the prognosis in these patients. Even for patients with low DBP, the target should still be achieved if well-tolerated. In the Systolic Hypertension in the Elderly Program (SHEP) study, reducing SBP during treatment significantly reduced major cardiovascular events, despite a decrease in DBP to an average of 68 mmHg.[452]

6.5.4 Pharmacological treatment of elderly ISH

Based on pooled data, calcium channel blockers (CCBs) and thiazide diuretics are recommended as the first-line drugs for treating ISH, while ACE inhibitors (ACEIs) or angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs) are less effective. However, if there are clear indications such as heart failure, coronary artery disease, chronic kidney disease (CKD), metabolic syndrome, or diabetes, ACEIs/ARBs should still be used as initial therapy.[444,453] Since monotherapy often has a low rate of blood pressure control in ISH patients, initiating treatment with a combination of two drugs is generally recommended, especially in non-frail elderly patients.

6.5.5 ISH in adolescents

ISH in adolescents is more common in males and may also occur in children, often associated with being overweight or obese.[454] Adolescent ISH is a highly heterogeneous condition, possibly involving individuals with different genetic and pathophysiological backgrounds and clinical characteristics.

In the clinical evaluation of adolescents with ISH, it is important to consider the potential presence of a white coat effect. All adolescent ISH patients are recommended to undergo out-of-office blood pressure measurement. If adolescent ISH is diagnosed, evaluating central arterial pressure and other central arterial hemodynamic parameters and arterial elasticity may provide additional valuable information.

There is still debate over whether adolescent ISH is associated with worse outcomes and whether antihypertensive treatment is necessary. Adolescents with ISH should be advised to make lifestyle changes, particularly smoking cessation, salt restriction, and a low-calorie diet if overweight, with close follow-up.[454] For individuals with elevated out-of-office blood pressure, high central arterial pressure, and other risk factors, medication should be considered.

6.6 Isolated Diastolic Hypertension (IDH)

Key Points 6F: Isolated Diastolic Hypertension(IDH)

• Regular Blood Pressure Assessment and Lifestyle Interventions: It is recommended that all patients with IDH undergo regular blood pressureevaluations and lifestyle interventions (Ⅰ, C).

• Antihypertensive Treatment: For patients at highcardiovascular risk, antihypertensive therapyshould be initiated (Ⅰ, C). It is also reasonable toconsider antihypertensive treatment in patientsyounger than 50 years old (Ⅱb, C).

6.6.1 Definition and epidemiology

IDH is a hypertensive phenotype characterized by systolic blood pressure (SBP) < 140 mmHg and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 90 mmHg. The prevalence of IDH in the general adult population ranges from 2.5% to 7.8%.[455,456] A prospective cohort study conducted in northeastern China among residents aged 40 years and older found an overall prevalence of IDH of 3.9%,[457] with a significant decline as age increases—from 7.2% in those aged 40-49 years to 1.5% in those aged 70 years and older.

The prevalence of IDH is higher in men than in women and is more common in overweight and obese populations, particularly in those with abdominal obesity, and is associated with other components of metabolic syndrome.[455] Compared to other hypertension phenotypes, patients with IDH tend to be younger, with higher rates of male gender, alcohol consumption, smoking, and diabetes.[455,458]

6.6.2 Cardiovascular Prognosis

IDH is a common cardiovascular risk factor in young individuals. Compared to those with normal blood pressure, IDH patients are more likely to progress to systolic-diastolic hypertension.[459] Prospective cohort studies in China have shown that IDH is associated with an increased risk of hemorrhagic stroke, cardiovascular disease, and cardiovascular mortality.[457,460] Longitudinal studies indicate that unlike systolic blood pressure, the relationship between diastolic blood pressure and cardiovascular events is significantly evident only in patients younger than 50 years old.[461]

6.6.3 Antihypertensive Treatment

Currently, there is no direct evidence that antihypertensive medication provides protective effects specifically for the IDH blood pressure phenotype. Although early antihypertensive treatment trials primarily enrolled patients based on diastolic blood pressure levels, the enrolled patients also met the hypertension criteria for systolic blood pressure. Treatment led to reductions in both systolic and diastolic blood pressures, making it impossible to distinguish the effects on each.[462]

Given the existing epidemiological data and the high risk of IDH progressing to ISH or systolic-diastolic hypertension, all IDH patients should undergo regular blood pressure assessments and lifestyle interventions. Due to stronger evidence of an association between IDH and adverse cardiovascular outcomes in younger patients than in older patients, it is reasonable to consider antihypertensive treatment in patients younger than 50 years old. Antihypertensive therapy should be administered to patients at high cardiovascular risk.

7 HYPERTENSION IN SPECIAL POPULATIONS
7.1 Hypertension in the Elderly

Key Points 7A: Hypertension in the Elderly

• Initiating Treatment: In general, antihypertensive medication should be initiated for elderly individuals aged 65-79 years with blood pressure ≥140/90 mmHg (Ⅰ, A). For those aged 80 years andolder, medication may be started if systolic bloodpressure (SBP) is ≥150 mmHg (Ⅱa, B). For elderlyindividuals with frailty or other geriatric syndromes, the timing of starting antihypertensivetreatment can be more flexible (Ⅱa, C).

• Blood Pressure Targets: It is recommended toaim for a blood pressure target of < 140/90 mmHgin elderly individuals aged 65-79 years. If tolerated,blood pressure can be further reduced to < 130/80mmHg (Ⅱa, A). For those aged 80 years and older,the target should be < 150/90 mmHg (Ⅱa, B), withthe option to lower it to < 140/90 mmHg if well-tolerated.

• Individualized Targets: For elderly individualswith multiple comorbidities or geriatric syndromes,blood pressure targets should be individualized. Infrail patients, systolic blood pressure should not belowered below 130 mmHg (Ⅱa, C).

Hypertension in individuals aged 65 years and older can be defined as elderly hypertension. Isolated systolic hypertension (ISH) is the most common form of hypertension in the elderly, defined as SBP ≥ 140 mmHg with diastolic blood pressure (DBP) < 90 mmHg. According to a survey conducted across 31 provinces in China from 2012 to 2015, the prevalence of hypertension in those aged 65-74 years and ≥ 75 years was 55.7% and 60.2%, respectively. However, the control rates were only 18.4% and 17.0%, with treatment success rates at 34.9% and 32.7%, respectively.[3]

7.1.1 Clinical characteristics

As age increases, systolic blood pressure tends to rise progressively, particularly in the elderly population. ISH is the most prevalent type of hypertension among older adults, and its prevalence increases with age. In China, 66% of hypertensive patients aged 80 years and older have ISH.[443] The prominent clinical features of elderly hypertension are elevated systolic pressure and widened pulse pressure, both of which are closely associated with poor cardiovascular outcomes such as stroke, coronary artery disease, and heart failure.[446] These features also complicate blood pressure management. Due to age-related decline in baroreceptor sensitivity and the structural and functional deterioration of peripheral arteries, elderly individuals experience greater short-term blood pressure variability (BPV). Some hypertensive elderly patients may develop orthostatic blood pressure variations (such as orthostatic hypotension or supine hypertension) or postprandial hypotension.[463,464]

Abnormal circadian blood pressure rhythms are also common among elderly hypertensive patients, manifesting as nocturnal hypertension or hypotension, a pronounced morning surge, or elevated morning blood pressure. These abnormalities are related to alterations in neuroendocrine regulation, increased salt sensitivity, and changes in volume regulation mechanisms observed in elderly patients.[465,466] Elderly hypertension often coexists with various cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, endocrine and metabolic disorders, or chronic kidney disease (CKD). This population is also at higher risk for disabilities, cognitive decline, or frailty, collectively known as geriatric syndromes, which significantly complicates the management of hypertension in this demographic.[467]

7.1.2 Clinical Assessment

(1) Diagnostic assessment: for elderly patients newly diagnosed with hypertension, it is recommended to measure blood pressure in both arms and also in the standing position. It is important to rule out white coat hypertension and pseudohypertension, the latter being more common in elderly patients with significant peripheral vascular sclerosis.[468] Home Blood Pressure Monitoring (HBPM) is valuable for diagnosing and evaluating the treatment efficacy of hypertension in the elderly.[469] Ambulatory Blood Pressure Monitoring (ABPM) can help detect abnormal blood pressure fluctuations, nocturnal or morning hypertension, and assess blood pressure rhythm disorders, such as white coat hypertension and masked hypertension.

(2) Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment: For hypertensive patients with comorbidities or multiple geriatric syndromes, a Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment (CGA) and frailty assessment should be conducted to develop appropriate blood pressure management strategies. Typically, a multidisciplinary team approach using assessment scales or diagnostic equipment is employed to evaluate various aspects of the elderly patient's condition, including physical and cognitive function, comorbidities, multi-organ function, psychological and emotional state, and family and social support. The primary intervention goal is to maintain and improve the quality of life and functional capacity of the elderly patient.[470] Daily activity ability and frailty status are crucial factors influencing the benefits of antihypertensive treatment in elderly patients. The FRAIL scale (fatigue, resistance, ambulation, illness, and loss of weight index) or the Fried criteria are commonly used for frailty assessment, and these assessments serve as the basis for developing blood pressure management strategies.[471-474]

7.1.3 Antihypertensive Treatment

Elderly hypertensive patients should moderate their salt intake, maintain a healthy body weight, and avoid smoking and excessive alcohol consumption. Appropriate physical and mental activities are beneficial for maintaining and improving daily functional abilities and cognitive function.[475]

The timing of initiating drug therapy in elderly patients should be based on a comprehensive consideration of blood pressure levels, cardiovascular risk, and the results of the Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment (CGA) or frailty assessment (see Table 22). When tolerated, strict blood pressure control in elderly patients provides clear cardiovascular benefits. Regardless of age, if patients can tolerate further lowering of blood pressure after achieving the initial target, a lower blood pressure target may be set. However, attention should be paid to the risks associated with low blood pressure and related complications.[77,142] as the baseline characteristics of patients included in clinical studies may differ from those encountered in actual clinical practice.

All commonly used antihypertensive drugs can be employed for treating hypertension in the elderly. The choice of the initial antihypertensive drug type and dosage should be prioritized based on the patient's existing target organ damage, comorbidities, and drug indications. In elderly patients, large doses or monotherapy with thiazide diuretics may lead to electrolyte disturbances, such as hypokalemia. Therefore, it is typically recommended to use small doses or to combine them with RAS inhibitors. Calcium channel blockers (CCBs) may cause adverse effects like ankle edema, which is related to an increase in capillary bed hydrostatic pressure. This usually does not require special treatment; reducing the dose or combining with RAS inhibitors can mitigate or eliminate these effects. When using β-blockers in elderly patients, it is important to consider indications, and choose β-blockers that are highly selective for β1 receptors or that also have α-receptor blocking effects. Elderly patients using α-receptor blockers should be cautious about orthostatic hypotension. In older patients, especially those with comorbidities, geriatric syndromes, or polypharmacy, drug interactions and adverse reactions should be closely monitored.

The approach to initial treatment is usually determined based on the patient's blood pressure level, cardiovascular risk, and tolerance to antihypertensive therapy. In non-very old patients, standard-dose monotherapy or low-dose or standard-dose combination therapy can be used as initial treatment. For patients aged 80 years and older, or those with multiple comorbidities or frailty, it is advisable to start with low-dose monotherapy. If blood pressure targets are not met, the drug dosage should be adjusted, or combination therapy should be initiated promptly. The recommendations for optimizing combination antihypertensive therapy in elderly patients are the same as for the general hypertensive population. Long-acting formulations taken once daily should be prioritized, and when combination therapy is needed, single-pill combinations (SPC) are preferred. This approach helps control 24-h blood pressure, including nighttime and morning hypertension, reduces blood pressure variability, and enhances long-term treatment adherence.[480]

In elderly patients starting drug therapy, blood pressure should gradually reach target levels within 4 to 12 weeks. Blood pressure should be actively monitored during treatment to avoid significant fluctuations and to improve abnormal circadian rhythms, including controlling nighttime or morning hypertension. For elderly patients with postprandial or orthostatic blood pressure variability, individualized treatment should be provided based on an assessment of the safety of antihypertensive therapy.[481]

Treatment Principles for ISH with Low Diastolic Pressure: In patients with ISH and diastolic blood pressure < 60 mmHg, if severe aortic valve regurgitation or coronary artery disease is ruled out, the primary goal of antihypertensive treatment is to achieve the target systolic blood pressure. Treatment generally begins with low-dose monotherapy, gradually increasing the drug dosage or combining therapies until the systolic blood pressure target is reached. The reduction in blood pressure should be gradual, based on what the patient can tolerate, and careful monitoring of diastolic blood pressure changes and associated clinical symptoms is required.

7.1.4 Hypertension in the very elderly

Currently, individuals aged 80 years and older are defined as the very elderly. According to a nationwide stratified, multistage random sampling cross-sectional survey conducted from 2012 to 2015, the prevalence of hypertension in the very elderly was 60.27%, with the prevalence of isolated systolic hypertension (ISH) at 39.75% and isolated diastolic hypertension (IDH) at 1.14%. The awareness rate of hypertension in this population was 54.92%, the treatment rate was 50.00%, and the control rate was only 16.65%.[443]

Although the HYVET (Hypertension in the Very Elderly Trial) study indicated that frail elderly hypertensive patients could still benefit from intensive antihypertensive treatment,[482] and the SPRINT (Systolic Blood Pressure Intervention Trial) study observed consistent cardiovascular benefits from intensive blood pressure control in elderly hypertensive patients aged 75 years and older, regardless of frailty levels,[483] frailty remains considered an important factor influencing antihypertensive treatment in the very elderly.[446,484-487] For hypertensive patients in this age group, in addition to assessing known cardiovascular risk factors, target organ damage, and associated clinical conditions, Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment (CGA), particularly frailty assessment, is recommended. The FRAIL scale or gait speed measurement is commonly used for frailty assessment.

The primary treatment goal is to achieve target systolic blood pressure. The HYVET study provides evidence that antihypertensive treatment in the very elderly can reduce the risk of stroke, all-cause mortality, heart failure, and cardiovascular events.[228] However, there is limited research supporting specific treatment target values for this population. After a comprehensive assessment, the initial treatment level and target blood pressure should be individualized. For the very elderly, if systolic blood pressure is ≥ 150/90 mmHg, antihypertensive drug therapy should be initiated,[141] with the initial goal of reducing blood pressure to < 150/90 mmHg. If well-tolerated, the blood pressure target can be further reduced to < 140/90 mmHg.[228,483]

Antihypertensive treatment for elderly patients should follow the principles of starting with low doses, gradual dose escalation, combination therapy, appropriate moderation, and the use of long-acting antihypertensive drugs whenever possible. For drug therapy in very elderly patients, diuretics, calcium channel blockers (CCBs), angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs), or angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs) are recommended.[228,450,488,489] When combination therapy is needed, long-acting single-pill combinations should be the first choice.

In summary, the current evidence regarding hypertension treatment in the very elderly is limited. Therefore, it is essential to perform a comprehensive assessment based on the patient’s frailty, cognitive impairment, comorbidities, and drug tolerance, and to develop an individualized antihypertensive treatment plan accordingly.

7.2 Hypertension in Children and Adolescents

Key Points 7B: Hypertension in Children and Adolescents

• Both primary and secondary hypertension shouldbe managed to bring blood pressure below the 95thpercentile (Ⅰ, C). If kidney disease, diabetes, or target organ damage is present, the goal should be to reduce blood pressure below the 90th percentile (Ⅰ, B).

• Most children can achieve blood pressure controlthrough lifestyle interventions; however, medication should be considered if necessary (Ⅱa, C).

7.2.1 Epidemiology and influencing factors

The prevalence of primary hypertension in children (under 18 years) is primarily based on single-point blood pressure measurements from population epidemiological surveys. For example, the 2019 National Physical Fitness Survey showed that the prevalence of hypertension among school-aged children aged 6-17 years was 13.0%, with higher rates in rural areas than in urban areas, and a decreasing trend from the central to eastern and western regions​.[490] When blood pressure is measured on three separate occasions (not on the same day), the prevalence of hypertension among children drops significantly to 3%-5%.[491-492] Data on the prevalence of secondary hypertension mainly come from retrospective analyses of hospitalized cases. A statistical analysis of hospital cases from 2010 to 2020 showed that secondary hypertension accounted for 57.4% of hypertension cases among hospitalized children under 18 years old, with 91.7% of cases in preschool children (under 6 years old). The proportion of primary hypertension increases with age, reaching 51.7% among those aged 12 years and older.[493] Among the various influencing factors for childhood hypertension, obesity is the leading risk factor, contributing nearly 20% to the risk of developing hypertension.[494] Other risk factors include a family history of hypertension, low birth weight, premature birth, unhealthy lifestyle habits (e.g., excessive sugar and salt intake, insufficient vegetable consumption, lack of sleep, and physical activity), and psychological and emotional stress​.[495] The causes of secondary hypertension are relatively clear, with kidney disease being the most common cause, accounting for more than 40% of cases.[493]

7.2.2 Diagnosis and Assessment

Blood pressure measurement methods: blood pressure in children is usually measured in the right upper arm using a brachial artery cuff. For the initial blood pressure measurement, it is recommended to measure blood pressure in all four limbs to rule out coarctation of the aorta. Additionally, blood pressure should be measured in different positions (sitting, lying, and standing) to detect orthostatic hypertension. Selecting the appropriate cuff size is crucial for accurate blood pressure measurement in children. Table 23 provides a reference for cuff sizes, corresponding arm circumference, and age range. For each blood pressure measurement, at least three consecutive readings should be taken. During the first measurement, additional readings may be necessary, with a 1-min interval between readings. If the difference between consecutive readings is 10 mmHg or more, additional measurements should be taken, and the average of the last two readings should be recorded.[496]

The diagnosis of hypertension in children requires individual evaluation based on blood pressure levels measured on three separate occasions, with intervals of more than two weeks between measurements. Generally, hypertension is only diagnosed if the blood pressure is ≥ P95 on all three occasions. However, if the initial (single) blood pressure measurement shows moderate to severe hypertension—defined as systolic and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ P99+5 mmHg for children aged 3 to 15 years, or systolic and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 160/100 mmHg for those aged 16 to 17 years—hypertension can be diagnosed immediately. “White coat hypertension” and “orthostatic hypertension” are relatively common in children and can be differentiated using ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM) or a tilt-table test.

7.2.3 Comprehensive intervention

Blood pressure control goals: for both primary and secondary hypertension in children, the goal should be to reduce blood pressure to below the 95th percentile. If the child has kidney disease, diabetes, or evidence of target organ damage, the target should be to lower the blood pressure to below the 90th percentile to minimize damage to target organs and reduce the long-term risk of cardiovascular disease.

Antihypertensive treatment: For children with secondary hypertension, treatment should primarily focus on addressing the underlying cause. Lifestyle modifications should be maintained throughout, including: (1) weight management: obese children should control their weight to ensure height development while slowing the increase in body mass index and reducing body fat content. (2) Physical activity: increase aerobic and resistance exercises and reduce sedentary time. (3) Dietary adjustments: modify diet to ensure diversity, control total energy intake and fat energy ratio. Follow WHO recommendations for children to control dietary salt and sugary drink intake, and foster healthy eating habits. (4) Stress management: avoid sustained mental stress. (5) Adequate sleep: ensure sufficient sleep duration. Most children can achieve their blood pressure control targets through lifestyle interventions alone.

Indications for pharmacological treatment: medication should be considered when hypertension is accompanied by any of the following conditions, or when moderate to severe hypertension is present: (1) clinical symptoms of hypertension; (2) diabetes; (3) secondary hypertension; and (4) target organ damage.

The principle of pharmacological treatment is to start with a low dose of a single medication, with individualized adjustments based on efficacy and blood pressure changes, and to adjust the treatment plan and duration as necessary. Combination therapy may be used if required.

Choice of medications for children: the selection of medications should mainly refer to the instructions provided by the drug manufacturers. Currently, in China, the number of antihypertensive drugs approved by the National Medical Products Administration with clear recommendations for pediatric use is limited, including: (1) ACE inhibitors: captopril and fosinopril; (2) diuretics: Amiloride, chlorthalidone, hydrochlorothiazide, and furosemide; (3) adrenergic receptor blockers: propranolol, atenolol, and prazosin; (4) ARBs: losartan; and (5) dihydropyridine CCBs: amlodipine.

Numerous studies have confirmed that various antihypertensive drugs, including ACE inhibitors and ARBs, can effectively lower blood pressure in children.[500] Strict blood pressure control has been shown to provide long-term renal protection in children with chronic kidney disease (CKD),[501] reverse left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH), and improve cardiac structure and function.[502] However, there are currently no clinical trials in children that use cardiovascular events as endpoints, and the long-term safety and effects of antihypertensive drugs in children require further research.

To achieve efficacy while minimizing adverse drug reactions, it is best to use long-acting medications (with effects lasting up to 24 h). Once blood pressure is satisfactorily controlled, the dosage of antihypertensive drugs can be gradually reduced until discontinuation, but abrupt cessation should be avoided. Regular monitoring of blood pressure and evaluation of treatment efficacy are essential during the treatment process.

7.3 Hypertensive Disorders in Pregnancy (HDP)

Key Points 7C: Hypertensive Disorders inPregnancy (HDP)

• Antihypertensive treatment should be initiatedwhen office blood pressure (BP) is ≥140/90 mmHgin patients with HDP (Class I, Level B).

• Pregnant women with high-risk factors for preeclampsia should start low-dose aspirin (75-150mg/day) between 12 to 16 weeks of gestation toprevent preeclampsia, continuing until delivery(Class I, Level A).

• Cardiovascular risk assessment and screening forrisk factors should be conducted postpartum forHDP patients (Class I, Level C).

• During antihypertensive treatment, BP shouldnot be lowered below 110/70 mmHg (Class III,Level C).

The prevalence of HDP in China ranges from 5.22% to 5.57%,[503] which is lower than the 6% to 10% observed in Western countries. HDP is one of the leading causes of maternal and fetal mortality and is associated with increased risks of placental abruption, cerebral hemorrhage, disseminated intravascular coagulation, and liver and kidney failure. HDP also significantly increases the long-term risk of cardiovascular events postpartum.

7.3.1 Classification of HDP

Hypertension during pregnancy is defined as office BP ≥ 140/90 mmHg, with systolic BP between 140-159 mmHg and/or diastolic BP between 90-109 mmHg classified as non-severe hypertension, and systolic BP ≥ 160 mmHg and/or diastolic BP ≥ 110 mmHg classified as severe hypertension. HDP includes four types: gestational hypertension, preeclampsia/eclampsia, chronic hypertension complicating pregnancy, and chronic hypertension with superimposed preeclampsia. (1) Gestational hypertension: hypertension occurring after 20 weeks of gestation without significant proteinuria, with BP returning to normal within 12 weeks postpartum. (2) Chronic hypertension complicating pregnancy: hypertension that exists before pregnancy or develops before 20 weeks of gestation and persists beyond 12 weeks postpartum. (3) Preeclampsia: Defined as elevated BP occurring after 20 weeks of gestation, accompanied by clinical proteinuria (urine protein ≥ 300 mg/24 h) or, in the absence of proteinuria, accompanied by organ and system involvement (including significant changes in the heart, lungs, liver, kidneys, or abnormalities in the hematological or central nervous systems). Severe preeclampsia is characterized by persistent BP elevation above 160/110 mmHg or severe systemic involvement.[504] (4) Before 20 weeks of gestation, self-monitoring of BP or 24-h ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM) is required to exclude white-coat hypertension and masked hypertension.[505,506] The classification of HDP is illustrated in Figure 6.

7.3.2 Pre-pregnancy evaluation and intervention for patients with chronic hypertension

Among patients with chronic hypertension, 86% to 89% have primary hypertension, with the remainder being secondary hypertension.[507] The pre-pregnancy evaluation for patients with chronic hypertension should include: (1) evaluate the effectiveness of blood pressure management, and assess levels of blood glucose, blood lipids, body weight, and habits such as smoking and alcohol consumption. (2) Encourage patients to adopt healthy lifestyle changes. Pregnancy is not recommended if blood pressure is ≥ 160/100 mmHg. For overweight patients, weight management through lifestyle interventions should be emphasized before conception. (3) For patients diagnosed with hypertension before the age of 30 and/or those with poorly controlled blood pressure, a thorough evaluation of the potential risks of secondary hypertension should be conducted. If necessary, a multidisciplinary consultation may be needed to rule out secondary hypertension. (4) If antihypertensive medications are required, ACE inhibitors (ACEIs) and angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs) should be avoided.

7.3.3 Prevention of preeclampsia

For pregnant women with a history of hypertension during pregnancy, chronic kidney disease (CKD), autoimmune diseases, diabetes, chronic hypertension, or those who have one or more risk factors for preeclampsia (e.g., nulliparity, age over 40, interpregnancy interval greater than 10 years, body mass index greater than 35 kg/m², family history of preeclampsia, or multiple gestations), it is recommended to start low-dose aspirin (75-150 mg/day) as early as 12 to 16 weeks of gestation, continuing until delivery.[508]

7.3.4 Timing of antihypertensive therapy initiation and blood pressure targets

The primary goal of treating hypertensive disorders in pregnancy (HDP) is to prevent the occurrence of severe preeclampsia and eclampsia, reduce the risk of perinatal complications and mortality for both the mother and the child, and improve perinatal outcomes. Based on research on blood pressure control during pregnancy[509] and the major findings from the Chronic Hypertension and Pregnancy (CHAP) study,[510] combined with evidence from domestic observational studies,[511] it is recommended that antihypertensive treatment be initiated when office blood pressure reaches or exceeds 140/90 mmHg in HDP patients.

There is currently no consensus on the lower safe limit for blood pressure levels during antihypertensive treatment. The CHAP study indicates that, compared to a less strict control group (target blood pressure < 160/105 mmHg), the strict control group (target blood pressure < 140/90 mmHg) experienced an 18% reduction in the risk of adverse maternal and fetal composite outcomes. Notably, in the strict control group, the average blood pressure during most weeks of pregnancy was below 130/80 mmHg. Blood pressure should not be reduced below 110/70 mmHg during treatment.[512,513]

7.3.5 Antihypertensive treatment

Non-pharmacological treatment includes ensuring adequate sleep and improving sleep quality, with a preference for the lateral recumbent position; ensuring sufficient intake of protein and calories; and moderately limiting salt intake.

Different antihypertensive treatment strategies should be adopted for pregnant women with non-severe and severe hypertension.

(1) For non-severe hypertension: initiate antihypertensive treatment and adjust the medication regimen in response to blood pressure changes during pregnancy. If low-to-medium doses of a single drug fail to achieve the target, it is recommended to combine it with another low-to-medium dose drug rather than increasing the dose of a single medication.[514] Monitoring of the pregnant woman’s blood pressure, proteinuria levels, and fetal condition is necessary.[515]

(2) For Severe hypertension: immediate management is required, with the goal of minimizing the occurrence of perinatal complications and mortality for both the mother and the fetus. Under close monitoring of the maternal and fetal conditions, the duration of antihypertensive treatment, target blood pressure levels, choice of medication, and indications for termination of pregnancy should be clearly defined. If the pregnant woman develops severe preeclampsia or eclampsia, intravenous magnesium sulfate should be administered as the first-line drug for the prevention/treatment of eclampsia. When systolic blood pressure is ≥ 180 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure is ≥ 120 mmHg, appropriate emergency measures for hypertensive crises should be taken.

Common oral antihypertensive medications include labetalol, nifedipine, and methyldopa. If necessary, small doses of thiazide diuretics may be used (see Table 25 for details). Common intravenous antihypertensive drugs include labetalol, urapidil, nicardipine, phentolamine, and nitroglycerin. For detailed treatment, refer to the management of hypertensive emergencies.

7.3.6 Postpartum blood pressure management and long-term cardiovascular disease prevention

Over 50% of women with hypertensive disorders in pregnancy (HDP) experience blood pressure ≥ 150/100 mmHg during the postpartum period. Therefore, it is recommended to closely monitor blood pressure within 72 h after delivery, with at least 4 to 6 measurements per day. For those with postpartum blood pressure ≥ 140/90 mmHg, antihypertensive treatment should be continued, and the dosage can be gradually reduced based on the recovery of blood pressure until the medication is discontinued. Approximately 25% of women with HDP require ongoing antihypertensive treatment for up to two years postpartum.[516] Additionally, the risk of developing hypertension within 10 years postpartum is 2.4 times higher in women with HDP compared to those without,[517] and there is a significantly increased risk of long-term cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases.[518,519]

Preventive measures for long-term cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases in women with a history of HDP include: (1) long-term cardiovascular risk assessment: regular evaluation of cardiovascular risk factors. (2) Screening for cardiovascular risk factors: annual checks of blood lipids, blood glucose, and blood pressure post-delivery. (3) Health education: Promotion of cardiovascular health education and the cultivation of a healthy lifestyle. (4) Medical intervention: if high-risk cardiovascular factors are present and not adequately controlled by the above measures, timely medical intervention with antihypertensive, hypoglycemic, or statin medications may be necessary.

8.1 Hypertension Combined with Stroke

Key Points 8A: Hypertension Combined with Stroke:

• For patients with acute ischemic stroke andsystolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 200 mmHg and/ordiastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 110 mmHg, it maybe reasonable to reduce blood pressure by 15%within the first 24 h after stroke onset (Class IIb,Level C). For patients who are candidates forthrombolysis or endovascular therapy, blood pressure should be controlled to < 180/100 mmHg(Class IIa, Level B).

• In patients with acute intracerebral hemorrhage,when SBP is ≥ 220 mmHg, it is reasonable to actively lower blood pressure under continuous monitoring (Class IIa, Level C). If SBP is between 150-219 mmHg and there are no contraindications toacute antihypertensive therapy, lowering SBP to 140mmHg is safe (Class IIa, Level A) and may be beneficial in improving functional outcomes (Class IIa,Level B).

• For patients with acute subarachnoid hemorrhage, lowering SBP to < 160 mmHg and maintaining stability is reasonable (Class IIa, Level B).

• After a stroke, if SBP remains ≥ 140 mmHgand/or DBP ≥ 90 mmHg once the patient’s condition is stable and there are no contraindications, it isadvisable to resume pre-stroke antihypertensivemedications or initiate antihypertensive therapy.The blood pressure target should be < 140/90 mmHg (Class I, Level A); if tolerable, it may be reduced to < 130/80 mmHg (Class IIa, Level B).

Hypertension is the most significant risk factor for both the initial occurrence and recurrence of stroke and transient ischemic attack (TIA). Blood pressure control can reduce the risk of recurrent stroke. Among patients who have experienced an ischemic stroke, approximately 70% have a history of hypertension.[520] Studies conducted in China, such as the PATS study, along with participation in the PROGRESS study, have demonstrated that antihypertensive treatment is effective in secondary prevention for patients with cerebrovascular disease, significantly reducing the risk of stroke recurrence. This benefit applies to both ischemic and hemorrhagic strokes. Recent and earlier systematic reviews, which included 10 and 8 randomized controlled trials respectively, confirmed that antihypertensive therapy can reduce the risk of stroke recurrence by 22% and 19%, respectively.[226,521]

8.1.1 Acute phase blood pressure management in stroke

In patients with elevated blood pressure within the first 24 h after ischemic stroke, management should be cautious. Priority should be given to addressing factors such as anxiety, pain, nausea and vomiting, urinary retention, and elevated intracranial pressure. For patients with persistently high blood pressure (SBP ≥ 200 mmHg or DBP ≥ 110 mmHg), or those with severe heart failure, aortic dissection, or hypertensive encephalopathy, antihypertensive treatment is recommended.[522]

Intravenous medications such as labetalol or nicardipine can be selected, avoiding drugs that cause rapid blood pressure reduction. For patients with acute ischemic stroke who are candidates for thrombolysis or endovascular therapy, blood pressure should be controlled to < 180/100 mmHg, with a reduction not exceeding 15% within the first 24 h.[522] There is currently no consensus on the timing of antihypertensive therapy in the acute phase of ischemic stroke. In patients with mild to moderate ischemic stroke within 24-48 h of symptom onset and SBP between 140-220 mmHg who have not received intravenous thrombolysis, early antihypertensive therapy does not reduce the likelihood of dependency or death at 90 days.[523] In a Chinese trial involving patients with acute ischemic stroke who were randomized within 48 h of stroke onset, those in the intervention group started antihypertensive treatment within 24 h after randomization. Compared with the control group (who received no antihypertensive treatment during hospitalization), there was no difference in the primary composite outcome of death or disability (defined as a modified Rankin Scale score of ≥ 3) at 14 days or discharge.[524]

During the acute phase of intracerebral hemorrhage, blood pressure reduction treatment should begin with a comprehensive assessment of the patient's blood pressure, analysis of the causes of elevated blood pressure, and then a decision on whether to proceed with blood pressure reduction treatment based on the blood pressure status. If the systolic blood pressure is > 220 mmHg, active blood pressure reduction treatment should be carried out under continuous blood pressure monitoring. For patients with a systolic blood pressure > 150 mmHg, without contraindications to acute blood pressure reduction treatment, reducing the systolic blood pressure to 140 mmHg is safe and can improve the patient's functional prognosis.[525] Further reducing the systolic blood pressure to < 130 mmHg may affect renal function and patient prognosis.[526] During blood pressure reduction treatment, blood pressure changes should be closely monitored, with blood pressure measured every 5 to 15 min.

Hypertension is a risk factor for rebleeding in subarachnoid hemorrhage and can lead to worse outcomes. There is no clear evidence-based support for the extent of blood pressure reduction during the acute phase of subarachnoid hemorrhage, but it is reasonable to reduce the systolic blood pressure to < 160 mmHg and maintain stability, and it is generally not recommended to lower blood pressure excessively.[527]

8.1.2 Blood pressure management in stable stroke

A meta-analysis of four randomized controlled trials involving patients with stroke or TIA found that intensive blood pressure reduction (targeting SBP < 120-130 mmHg) compared to standard reduction (targeting SBP < 140-150 mmHg) could reduce the risk of stroke recurrence by 22%, although some individual studies showed negative results.[528]

Subsequent analyses of the Warfarin-Aspirin Symptomatic Intracranial Disease Trial,[529] the medical management group in the Stenting and Aggressive Medical Management for Preventing Recurrent Stroke in Intracranial Stenosis Trial,[530] and the Chinese Intracranial Atherosclerosis Registry Study indicated that in most patients with symptomatic intracranial arterial stenosis,[531] controlling SBP to < 140 mmHg is safe and associated with a lower risk of stroke recurrence. However, the potential benefits of even lower target values were not analyzed in these studies, and limited evidence suggests caution when considering more aggressive targets.[520]

For strokes or TIAs caused by low hemodynamic factors, the speed and extent of blood pressure reduction should be balanced against patient tolerance and hemodynamic effects.

The choice of antihypertensive drugs in patients with ischemic stroke or TIA is similar to that in general hypertension patients. Most stroke patients will require combination antihypertensive therapy. The selection of drug types and dosages, as well as the blood pressure target, should be individualized, taking into account the drug's characteristics, stroke subtype, and the patient's comorbidities.[520]

8.2 Hypertension and Cognitive Impairment

Key Points 8B: Hypertension and Cognitive Impairment

• Antihypertensive treatment has protective effectson cognitive function, and antihypertensive therapy should be continued in patients with hypertension combined with cognitive impairment (Class I,Level C).

• It is recommended that in general, hypertensivepatients with cognitive impairment should aim forblood pressure below 140/90 mmHg (Class I, LevelC); if tolerated, it may be reduced to below 130/80mmHg (Class IIa, Level C). For patients with severecognitive decline or dementia, a preliminary bloodpressure control target of below 150/90 mmHg maybe considered (Class IIb, Level C).

8.2.1 The relationship between hypertension and cognitive impairment

Epidemiological studies in China show that the risk of dementia in hypertensive patients is 1.86 times higher, and the risk of mild cognitive impairment is 1.62 times higher than in non-hypertensive individuals.[532]

The association between midlife hypertension and the risk of cognitive decline and dementia in old age is particularly strong.[533-535] A large meta-analysis found that midlife hypertension, elevated systolic blood pressure, and elevated diastolic blood pressure all increase the risk of dementia.[56] Most studies suggest a J-shaped relationship between hypertension and cognitive impairment.[536-537] A longitudinal study in China found that for elderly people over 80 years old, the risk of cognitive impairment was lowest when systolic blood pressure was between 130-150 mmHg.[538] Hypertension in young or adolescent individuals already negatively impacts cognitive function, and its detrimental effects may exist before midlife.[539-542] Furthermore, blood pressure variability (BPV), non-dipping nighttime blood pressure, and orthostatic hypotension are also associated with an increased risk of cognitive impairment and dementia.[543-545]

Reasonable antihypertensive treatment plays a protective role in cognitive function among hypertensive patients, and it should continue even in those with cognitive impairment.[538,546-548] Meta-analyses indicate that lowering blood pressure may have some benefits in delaying the development of dementia or cognitive impairment. Clinical trials have shown that treating hypertension can reduce the risk of dementia and Alzheimer's disease to some extent, and similar risk reductions have been observed in observational studies.[549]

8.2.2 Blood pressure targets

Although long-term cohort studies suggest that antihypertensive treatment can reduce the risk of dementia, the impact of specific blood pressure control targets on cognitive function is not yet fully understood. Therefore, blood pressure control targets cannot be solely based on the cognitive function of hypertensive patients. It is recommended that hypertensive patients with cognitive impairment aim to reduce blood pressure to below 140/90 mmHg, and if tolerated, further reduction to below 130/80 mmHg may be considered. For patients with severe cognitive decline or dementia, especially those living alone, overly strict blood pressure control may have potential adverse effects, and a more relaxed blood pressure control strategy may be appropriate, with an initial target of below 150/90 mmHg. For patients aged 80 years or older with blood pressure ≥ 150/90 mmHg, antihypertensive treatment should be initiated alongside lifestyle modifications to reduce blood pressure to below 150/90 mmHg. If well tolerated, further reduction to below 140/90 mmHg may be considered. In frail patients, individualized blood pressure control targets should be established based on specific circumstances.[550]

8.2.3 Pharmacological treatment

Common antihypertensive drugs can be used in the treatment of hypertension combined with cognitive impairment. Randomized controlled trials reporting cognitive outcomes have involved various antihypertensive drugs. The European Systolic Hypertension Study was the first randomized controlled trial to demonstrate that antihypertensive treatment with calcium channel blockers (CCBs) could significantly reduce the risk of dementia.[551] The PROGRESS study showed that perindopril could reduce vascular cognitive impairment in hypertensive patients.[552] Meta-analyses assessing the impact of different classes of antihypertensive drugs on cognitive outcomes have not found any one class to be superior to others.[549,553,554]

8.3 Hypertension Combined with Coronary Heart Disease

Key Points 8C: Hypertension Combined with Coronary Heart Disease:

• It is recommended that the blood pressure (BP)target for hypertensive patients with coronary heartdisease (CHD) be < 140/90 mmHg (Class I, LevelA). Intensive BP control may offer additional benefits, and if tolerated, blood pressure can be reducedto < 130/80 mmHg (Class IIa, Level B).

• For hypertensive patients with stable CHD, thereis robust evidence supporting the use of calciumchannel blockers (CCBs), renin-angiotensin system(RAS) inhibitors, and β-blockers (Class I, Level A).

• In hypertensive patients with myocardial infarction (MI), long-term use of β-blockers and RAS inhibitors post-MI can significantly improve longterm prognosis. These should be initiated early ifthere are no contraindications (Class I, Level A).

Hypertension as a Major Risk Factor for Coronary Heart Disease: hypertension is a critical independent risk factor for CHD. The INTERHEART study, which involved 52 countries, demonstrated that hypertension accounts for 25% to 30% of the population-attributable risk for acute myocardial infarction (AMI), second only to the apolipoprotein B/apolipoprotein A1 ratio (49.2%) and smoking (35.7%).[555] A meta-analysis of large clinical trials on antihypertensive treatment showed that lowering systolic blood pressure (SBP) by 10 mmHg reduces the risk of CHD by 17%.[135]

8.3.1 Blood pressure targets

In patients with CHD, lowering SBP by 10 mmHg reduces the risk of major cardiovascular events by 27%, CHD events by 32%, stroke by 26%, heart failure by 49%, and all-cause mortality by 18%.[135] The recommended BP target for hypertensive patients with CHD is < 140/90 mmHg. Intensive BP control may provide additional benefits, and if tolerated, BP can be reduced to < 130/80 mmHg.[135] However, in elderly patients or those with severe coronary artery stenosis, BP should not be lowered too aggressively. A gradual, stepwise reduction is recommended to prevent ischemic intolerance in vital organs.[557]

8.3.2 Antihypertensive Treatment

(1) Stable coronary heart disease: In hypertensive patients with stable CHD, CCBs, RAS inhibitors, and β-blockers have strong evidence supporting their use. CCBs can also reduce myocardial oxygen consumption and decrease the frequency of angina attacks.[244,557-558] If BP control is inadequate with these drugs, a diuretic may be added.

(2) Non-ST-Segment Elevation Acute Coronary Syndromes (NSTE-ACS): CCBs and β-blockers are preferred as first-line treatments. If BP control is suboptimal, RAS inhibitors and diuretics can be added. Additionally, when vascular spasm is suspected, large doses of β-blockers should be avoided, as they may induce coronary artery spasm.[559]

(3) ST-Segment Elevation Myocardial Infarction (STEMI): In hypertensive patients with MI, long-term use of β-blockers and RAS inhibitors post-MI significantly improves prognosis.[244,557,558] These should be initiated early if there are no contraindications. If BP control is inadequate, CCBs and diuretics can be added. Sacubitril/valsartan has been approved for use in China, with evidence supporting its use in heart failure patients.[560] Recent studies also suggest benefits in patients with STEMI,[561,562] particularly those with left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) and reduced ejection fraction, by reducing the composite endpoint of CHD death, non-fatal MI, angina-related hospitalization, or coronary revascularization, although close monitoring for hypotension is necessary

8.4 Hypertension Combined with Heart Failure

Key Points on Hypertension Combined with Heart Failure:

• For patients with hypertension combined withheart failure, the recommended blood pressure (BP)target is < 130/80 mmHg (Class I, Level C).

• In hypertensive patients with heart failure withreduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), the first-line antihypertensive treatments recommended include angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitors (ARNI) orACE inhibitors (if ACEIs are not tolerated, ARBscan be used), β-blockers, mineralocorticoid receptorantagonists (MRA), sodium-glucose co-transporter2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) (Class I, Level A), and loop diuretics (Class I, Level B).

• In hypertensive patients with heart failure withpreserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), SGLT2i (ClassI, Level A), diuretics (Class I, Level C), ARNI (ClassIIa, Level B), or ARBs (Class IIb, Level B), and MRA(Class IIa, Level B) are recommended as first-linetreatments.

8.4.1 Epidemiological characteristics

Hypertension is a major causative factor in heart failure. Studies show that 56.3% of hospitalized heart failure patients in China have concomitant hypertension, with 49.4% of HFrEF patients and 64.5% of HFpEF patients also suffering from hypertension.[563] Recent registry studies report similar findings, with 65.2% of HFpEF patients having concomitant hypertension.[564] In the Asian region, the prevalence of hypertension among heart failure patients ranges from 52% to 71%.[565]

8.4.2 Clinical Benefits of Antihypertensive Therapy

Aggressive antihypertensive treatment can prevent the development of heart failure in hypertensive patients and improve the prognosis of those with heart failure, reducing the incidence of adverse cardiovascular events. Meta-analyses of large antihypertensive trials indicate that a 10 mmHg reduction in systolic blood pressure (SBP) reduces the risk of heart failure by 28%. In patients with baseline heart failure, a 10 mmHg reduction in SBP reduces the risk of major cardiovascular events by 25% and all-cause mortality by 15%.[135,566] The SPRINT trial showed that intensive blood pressure lowering (with a target SBP of 121.5 mmHg at the end of the study) significantly reduced the incidence of heart failure by 38% compared to standard blood pressure control (target SBP of 134.6 mmHg). For HFpEF patients, the risk of heart failure hospitalization and cardiovascular death was lowest when SBP was between 120-129 mmHg.[567]

8.4.3 Blood pressure targets

For patients with HFrEF, the recommended blood pressure target is < 130/80 mmHg. For patients with HFpEF, it is recommended to reduce blood pressure to < 140/90 mmHg, and if tolerated, further to < 130/80 mmHg. However, this recommendation currently lacks support from randomized controlled trials.[568] The lower limit of blood pressure targets for HFrEF and HFpEF patients is not yet clear. Assessing the patient's age, type of heart failure, and comorbidities can help in setting individualized blood pressure targets.

For HFrEF patients, guideline-directed medical therapy has clear benefits in improving prognosis.[569-571] These treatments generally affect blood pressure, but the relationship between low blood pressure and adverse cardiovascular events is not well established. Therefore, even in the presence of low blood pressure, treatment should not be withheld unless the patient experiences intolerance or adverse events. However, in HFpEF patients, particularly those with limited preload reserve, overly aggressive blood pressure lowering should be avoided to prevent adverse outcomes.[570]

For patients with advanced or end-stage heart failure, classified as Stage D heart failure by the American Heart Association, the condition is characterized by reduced myocardial contractility and pump failure, leading to decreased cardiac output and systemic congestion. Consequently, even patients with a history of hypertension rarely present with high blood pressure at this stage. On the contrary, persistent hypotension (e.g., systolic pressure ≤ 90 mmHg) is a clinical hallmark of Stage D heart failure and often results in inadequate perfusion of vital organs, such as the brain and kidneys, leading to organ dysfunction and poor prognosis. Therefore, for Stage D heart failure patients, it is essential to maintain basic or relatively higher blood pressure levels to maximize the opportunity for guideline-directed medical therapy.[568]

8.4.4 Management of hypertension combined with chronic heart failure

For patients with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), lifestyle management should be prioritized, including controlling sodium intake, reducing body weight, and increasing physical activity. The first-line antihypertensive treatments should include evidence-based medications such as angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitors (ARNI), angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEI), angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARB), β-blockers, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRA), and sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i).[569-571] These drugs can improve the prognosis of HFrEF patients, reducing the rates of hospitalization for heart failure and cardiovascular or all-cause mortality. The treatment dosage should reach the target dose recommended by clinical studies or the maximum dose tolerated by the patient. In addition, most HFrEF patients' treatment plans also include loop diuretics, which have good antihypertensive effects.[569-571] If blood pressure remains uncontrolled after the aforementioned drug treatments, the use of dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers (CCBs) such as amlodipine or felodipine can be considered, as their impact on the prognosis of HFrEF patients is neutral.[572,573] The use of non-dihydropyridine CCBs, which have negative inotropic effects, is not recommended.

For patients with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), lifestyle management should also be emphasized. Previous heart failure guidelines mainly recommended the use of loop diuretics to alleviate symptoms and/or signs of congestion in HFpEF patients. Studies have shown that spironolactone and sacubitril/valsartan significantly reduce the risk of hospitalization for heart failure in HFpEF patients.[574,575] The addition of SGLT2i to standard heart failure treatment has also been shown to significantly reduce the composite endpoint risk of cardiovascular death or heart failure hospitalization in HFpEF patients.[576,577] Therefore, for hypertensive patients with chronic HFpEF, the aforementioned medications are preferred. If blood pressure is not adequately controlled, the combination of dihydropyridine CCBs (amlodipine or felodipine) or non-dihydropyridine CCBs may be considered; however, the use of centrally acting antihypertensive drugs is not recommended. Unless the patient has concomitant benign prostatic hyperplasia, the use of α-blockers is not recommended.

8.4.5 Management of Hypertension Combined with Acute Heart Failure

The clinical characteristics of hypertension combined with acute heart failure primarily include elevated blood pressure, predominantly left heart failure, and rapid progression. It is essential to manage heart failure while actively lowering blood pressure. The primary treatment involves intravenous administration of loop diuretics and vasodilators, including sodium nitroprusside or urapidil.

For patients with milder conditions, blood pressure can be gradually reduced over 24 to 48 h. For those with severe conditions accompanied by acute pulmonary edema, the initial reduction in mean arterial pressure should not exceed 25% of the pre-treatment level within the first hour. Blood pressure should then be lowered to 160/100 mmHg within 2 to 6 h, with a gradual reduction to normal levels over the following 24 to 48 h.

8.5 Hypertension Complicated with Peripheral Arterial Disease (PAD)

Key Points 8E: Hypertension Complicated with PAD

• For hypertensive patients with PAD, blood pressure should be controlled to < 140/90 mmHg. In patients with diabetes or chronic kidney disease(CKD), blood pressure should be controlled to < 130/80 mmHg if tolerated (Class I, Level A).

• ACE inhibitors (ACEI) or angiotensin II receptorblockers (ARB) can be used as the initial antihypertensive treatment (Class IIa, Level B). Calcium channel blockers (CCB) and diuretics can also be considered as initial combination therapy for these patients (Class IIa, Level B). Beta-blockers may also beconsidered (Class IIb, Level C).

• In patients with symptomatic extracranial carotidartery stenosis, unless in the hyperacute phase ofstroke, antihypertensive treatment may be reasonable (Class IIa, Level C).

8.5.1 Epidemiological characteristics and benefits of antihypertensive treatment

The prevalence of PAD in the general population is 3% to 10%, rising to 15% to 20% in individuals over 70 years of age.[578] Hypertension is a significant risk factor for the development and progression of PAD, with up to 55% of PAD patients also suffering from hypertension. Hypertension markedly increases the risk of cardiovascular events and mortality in PAD patients.[579]

Rational antihypertensive therapy can reduce the incidence of cardiovascular events and the rate of amputations in PAD patients. For every 10 mmHg reduction in systolic blood pressure, the risk of PAD-related amputation or death decreases by 16%.[580] In patients with lower extremity arterial disease who are indicated for revascularization, timely and appropriate vascular reconstruction can significantly improve walking ability and quality of life, contributing to better blood pressure control and reduced cardiovascular risk.

8.5.2 Blood pressure targets

There is ongoing debate regarding whether strict blood pressure control is necessary in this patient population. During antihypertensive treatment, blood flow to the affected limb may decrease, but most patients can tolerate this, with only a small number of severely ischemic patients experiencing worsening symptoms due to reduced blood flow, particularly in the case of excessive blood pressure reduction. A study of the extended-release verapamil-trandolapril trial followed over 2.7 years showed that the incidence of cardiovascular events and blood pressure control levels in PAD patients followed a J-shaped curve, with the lowest cardiovascular risk observed when blood pressure was maintained at 135-145/60-90 mmHg. For hypertensive patients with lower extremity arterial disease, blood pressure should be controlled to < 140/90 mmHg.[581] For patients with diabetes and CKD, if tolerated, blood pressure should be controlled to < 130/80 mmHg.[581,582]

8.5.3 Choice of antihypertensive drugs

Several studies have found that ACEIs can improve walking distance, making them suitable for the treatment of hypertension in patients with PAD.[583] Meta-analyses and systematic reviews have shown that beta-blockers are safe for patients with lower extremity arterial stenosis. For hypertensive patients with lower extremity arterial disease, ACEIs or ARBs can be used as the initial antihypertensive therapy, with CCBs and diuretics as options for initial combination therapy. Beta-blockers can also be considered for use in these patients.[581,582,584]

8.5.4 Hypertension complicated by carotid artery stenosis

There is still controversy regarding the target blood pressure for hypertensive patients with carotid artery stenosis. Most guidelines currently recommend controlling blood pressure to < 140/90 mmHg, and in patients with concurrent diabetes or CKD, the target should be < 130/80 mmHg. Meta-analysis has shown that when bilateral carotid artery stenosis is < 70%, increased blood pressure is directly proportional to the risk of stroke. In elderly patients with bilateral carotid artery stenosis > 70%, excessive blood pressure reduction may increase the risk of cerebral ischemia, so it is advisable to moderately relax the blood pressure target. The choice of antihypertensive drug types and doses, as well as target blood pressure values, should be individualized, considering the balance of medication effects, stroke risk, and the patient's ability to regulate blood pressure.[391] For patients with symptomatic extracranial carotid artery stenosis, unless in the hyperacute phase of stroke, antihypertensive treatment may be reasonable.

Long-term use of CCBs and ACEIs in hypertensive patients with carotid artery stenosis has been shown in multiple studies to reduce carotid intima-media thickness (IMT) and decrease stroke risk. In hypertensive patients with carotid atherosclerosis, CCBs and ACEIs should be preferentially used to slow the progression of carotid atherosclerosis.

8.6 Hypertension Complicated with Kidney Disease

Key Points 8F: Hypertension Complicated with Kidney Disease

• In patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD)without proteinuria, antihypertensive drug therapyshould be initiated when systolic blood pressure(SBP) is ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) is ≥ 90 mmHg. The target blood pressure is < 140/90 mmHg (Class I, Level A). If tolerated, blood pressure may be reduced to 130/80mmHg (Class IIa, Level B).

• In patients with CKD and proteinuria, antihypertensive therapy should be initiated when SBP is ≥130 mmHg and/or DBP is ≥ 80 mmHg. The targetblood pressure is < 130/80 mmHg (Class IIa, Level B).

• Regardless of whether diabetes is present, initialantihypertensive therapy in CKD patients with proteinuria should include an ACE inhibitor (ACEI) orangiotensin II receptor blocker (ARB) (Class I, Level B).

• For CKD patients with diabetes and non-diabetickidney disease, if the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) is ≥ 20 mL/min/1.73 m2, it is recommended to use sodium-glucose cotransporter-2inhibitors (SGLT2i) (Class I, Level A).

Hypertension and CKD are mutually aggravating conditions. Beyond neurohumoral mechanisms, volume overload and vascular stiffness also play significant roles in the pathogenesis of hypertension in CKD patients. In China, the prevalence of hypertension among CKD patients ranges from 62.0% to 71.2%, with higher rates observed as kidney function declines.[585-587] Additionally, hypertension in CKD patients is often associated with abnormal blood pressure rhythms, such as elevated nighttime blood pressure, which correlates with poor outcomes.[435,588] Therefore, monitoring blood pressure outside the clinic, such as with ambulatory blood pressure monitoring, is crucial in addition to routine office blood pressure measurements.

8.6.1 Blood pressure targets

Proteinuria is a risk factor for renal function decline and cardiovascular morbidity/mortality in CKD patients. Therefore, the timing of initiating antihypertensive medication and the target blood pressure in CKD patients differ based on the presence of proteinuria. The evaluation methods for normal/minimally increased proteinuria and moderately/severely increased proteinuria are provided in Table 26. In CKD patients with urine albumin < 30 mg/24 h (UACR < 30 mg/g), antihypertensive therapy should be initiated when SBP is ≥ 140 mmHg and/or DBP is ≥ 90 mmHg, with a target blood pressure of < 140/90 mmHg. In CKD patients with urine albumin > 30 mg/24h (UACR > 30 mg/g), antihypertensive therapy should be initiated when SBP is ≥ 130 mmHg and/or DBP is ≥ 80 mmHg, with a target blood pressure of < 130/80 mmHg. If tolerated, blood pressure may be further reduced.

The evidence supporting antihypertensive treatment in elderly CKD patients is limited. It is recommended to develop individualized antihypertensive strategies, gradually adjusting the treatment to achieve stable blood pressure control within tolerable limits. For elderly CKD patients aged 65 to 79 years, the timing for initiating antihypertensive therapy and blood pressure control targets can follow the guidelines mentioned above. For very elderly patients (aged ≥ 80 years) with CKD, antihypertensive treatment may be initiated when blood pressure is ≥ 150/90 mmHg, with a target blood pressure of < 150/90 mmHg. If tolerated, further reduction of blood pressure to < 140/90 mmHg may be considered.[108,589,590]

8.6.2 Principles of antihypertensive drug use

All commonly used antihypertensive drugs can be employed in the treatment of CKD patients. ACE inhibitors (ACEIs) and angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs) not only have antihypertensive effects but also reduce proteinuria, slow the decline of renal function, and improve the prognosis of CKD patients. For hypertensive CKD patients with proteinuria, regardless of whether diabetes is present, the first-line treatment should include an ACEI or ARB. ACEIs or ARBs can be used alone or in combination with other antihypertensive drugs; however, the combination of both drugs is not recommended. After initiating therapy, serum creatinine should be monitored at 2-4 weeks. If there is an increase of more than 30% from the baseline, the cause should be investigated (e.g., volume depletion, bilateral renal artery stenosis, concomitant use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs), and if necessary, the dose should be reduced or the drug discontinued.[591,592] Blood potassium levels should also be monitored, and potassium-lowering treatments (such as correcting acidosis, diuretics, or the use of intestinal potassium binders) should be considered if needed, to allow continued use of ACEIs or ARBs.

Dihydropyridine and non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers (CCBs) primarily exert renal protective effects through their antihypertensive action. Diuretics reduce extracellular fluid volume, and care should be taken to avoid excessive diuresis, which could lead to hypovolemia, hypotension, or a decrease in glomerular filtration rate. When the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) is ≥ per 30 mL/min per 1.73 m², thiazide diuretics may be used; when eGFR is < 30 mL/min per 1.73 m², loop diuretics should be used.

Mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs) have antihypertensive and proteinuria-lowering effects, but they can cause hyperkalemia in a small number of patients, especially those with declining renal function, so monitoring is required after administration. New non-steroidal MRAs have shown cardiorenal benefits in CKD patients with diabetes.[399] Beta-blockers counteract the excessive activation of the sympathetic nervous system and provide cardiovascular protection for CKD patients. Studies have shown that the new antihypertensive drug angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitor (ARNI) is also effective in lowering blood pressure in CKD patients and can improve proteinuria.[593] Subgroup analyses from heart failure studies involving CKD patients have shown that ARNI significantly reduces severe renal adverse events and slows the progression of renal function decline.[594] It should be noted that ARNI contains an ARB component, so monitoring of creatinine and blood potassium levels is also required during treatment.

Sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) have demonstrated cardiorenal protective effects in CKD patients, regardless of whether they have type 2 diabetes, and have shown some improvement in blood pressure in completed studies.[396] If eGFR is ≥ 20 mL/min per 1.73 m², it is recommended that CKD patients with diabetes and non-diabetic kidney disease use SGLT2i.

Most CKD patients require combination therapy to achieve target blood pressure levels. Currently, there is a lack of comparative studies on the impact of different antihypertensive combination regimens on CKD patient outcomes. It is recommended to select combination therapies based on the patient’s age, comorbidities, and other specific conditions. If necessary, alpha-blockers, centrally acting antihypertensive drugs, and other treatments may also be used in combination.

8.6.3 Antihypertensive treatment in ESRD dialysis patients

Currently, there is insufficient research evidence regarding the diagnosis and blood pressure control standards for hypertension in hemodialysis patients.[395] In clinical practice in China, a pre-dialysis systolic blood pressure of < 160 mmHg is commonly used.[596] There are numerous factors influencing blood pressure control in hemodialysis patients, with volume overload being a significant factor. In managing blood pressure in hemodialysis patients, it is crucial to focus on volume management, aiming to bring the patient as close to dry weight as possible. Additionally, the choice of antihypertensive drugs, timing of administration, and dosage should be adjusted according to the hemodynamic changes during dialysis and the impact on drug clearance.

Similarly, there is also a lack of sufficient research evidence on blood pressure control targets in peritoneal dialysis patients. According to the guidelines of the International Society for Peritoneal Dialysis referenced by China, it is recommended that the blood pressure target for peritoneal dialysis patients be < 140/90 mmHg. Volume management is equally crucial in the blood pressure management of peritoneal dialysis patients.[597-598]

8.7 Hypertension Complicated with Diabetes

Key Points 8G: Hypertension Complicated with Diabetes

• The blood pressure control target for diabetic patients is < 130/80 mmHg (Class IIa, Level B). Forelderly patients or those with severe coronaryartery disease, the target can be relaxed to 140/90mmHg (Class IIa, Level B). For pregnant womenwith diabetes, the recommended blood pressuretarget is ≤135/85 mmHg (Class IIa, Level B).

• All common antihypertensive drugs can be usedin the treatment of hypertension complicated bydiabetes; when combining antihypertensive drugs,an ACE inhibitor (ACEI) or angiotensin II receptorblocker (ARB) should be the foundation (Class I,Level A).

• It is recommended that patients with hypertension and diabetes use sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) and glucagon-like peptide-1receptor agonists (GLP-1RA) to reduce the risk ofcardiorenal events, as these also have some bloodpressure-lowering effects (Class I, Level A).

Approximately 60% of patients with type 2 diabetes in China have hypertension.[599] Hypertension complicated by diabetes significantly increases the risk of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events.[600] Antihypertensive treatment can significantly reduce the risk of macrovascular events and mortality in diabetic patients, and prevent microvascular complications such as nephropathy and retinopathy. For every 10 mmHg reduction in systolic blood pressure, the risk of death decreases by 13%, cardiovascular risk decreases by 11%, and the risk of retinopathy decreases by 13%.[601] Intensive antihypertensive treatment provides even greater benefits for patients with type 2 diabetes, reducing the risk of all-cause mortality and major cardiovascular events by 18%, the risk of myocardial infarction by 14%, and the risk of stroke by 28%.[602]

8.7.1 Blood pressure targets

The general blood pressure control target for diabetic patients is < 130/80 mmHg;[603,604] for elderly patients or those with severe coronary artery disease, a relatively relaxed blood pressure target can be set, up to 140/90 mmHg. For pregnant women with diabetes, a blood pressure control target of ≤ 135/85 mmHg is recommended to reduce the risk of accelerated hypertension progression in the mother and minimize any adverse impact on fetal growth.[336]

8.7.2 Antihypertensive treatment

Lifestyle interventions are crucial for patients with type 2 diabetes. Special emphasis should be placed on interventions targeting overweight and obesity to improve blood glucose and metabolic abnormalities.

The selection of antihypertensive drugs should consider the antihypertensive efficacy, organ protection effects, patient adherence, and the safety and metabolic impact of the drug. All commonly used antihypertensive drugs can be used in the treatment of hypertension complicated by diabetes. For diabetic patients with albuminuria or CKD, ACEIs or ARBs should be the first choice.[605] Diabetic patients typically require a combination of two or more antihypertensive drugs. When using combination therapy, ACEIs or ARBs should be the foundation, combined with calcium channel blockers (CCBs), low-dose diuretics, or selective beta-blockers. Single-pill combinations (SPC) are preferred for combination therapy, as they are superior in efficacy, adherence, and safety compared to the free combination of these drugs.[606] ACEI combined with ARB, as well as diuretics combined with selective beta-blockers, are generally not recommended for diabetic patients. Diuretics should be used with caution in diabetic patients with hyperuricemia. Beta-blockers should be used cautiously in patients who experience frequent hypoglycemia, to avoid masking hypoglycemic symptoms. If diuretics and beta-blockers need to be used, they should be used in low doses. Alpha-blockers can be used in patients with prostate hypertrophy and poorly controlled blood pressure. For diabetic patients with resistant hypertension, spironolactone can be added on the basis of triple combination therapy.

The new antidiabetic drugs SGLT2i and GLP-1RA may help improve blood pressure control in diabetic patients. A meta-analysis showed that SGLT2i and GLP-1RA can significantly reduce blood pressure in patients with type 2 diabetes, lowering systolic/diastolic blood pressure by 4.3/2.3 mmHg and 2.9/0.9 mmHg, respectively.[607] Additionally, several large clinical studies have demonstrated that SGLT2i can reduce the risk of cardiovascular events, end-stage renal disease (ESRD), and cardiovascular disease-related death in diabetic patients,[608-610] attention should be paid to volume changes when using these drugs. GLP-1RA also has certain cardioprotective and renoprotective effects , and volume changes should also be monitored during their use.[611]

8.8 Hypertension Complicated with Obesity

Key Points 8H: Hypertension Complicated with Obesity

• For hypertensive patients with obesity, it is recommended to reduce the target blood pressure to< 140/90 mmHg (ClassI, Level A). If tolerated, thetarget can be further reduced to < 130/80 mmHg(Class IIa, Level B).

• The selection of antihypertensive drugs should bebased on the principle of not worsening obesity andmetabolic parameters (Class I, Level C).

Obesity is most commonly associated with hypertension, followed by diabetes. Compared to non-obese individuals, hypertensive patients with obesity often require more medications to control blood pressure and may exhibit higher rates of resistant hypertension, masked hypertension, and isolated diastolic hypertension (IDH).[612-614] Obesity is the most significant risk factor for hypertension in children and adolescents.

8.8.1 Antihypertensive Treatment

The blood pressure targets for obese individuals do not differ from those for individuals of normal weight; the recommended target is < 140/90 mmHg, with further reduction to < 130/80 mmHg if tolerated.

Lifestyle modification combined with antihypertensive medication is recommended.[615] Weight loss through lifestyle changes helps control blood pressure. Hypertensive patients with obesity should be encouraged to lose weight through a low-calorie diet and increased physical activity.

The choice of antihypertensive drugs should be based on the principle of not worsening obesity and metabolic parameters. ACE inhibitors (ACEI), angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARB), or calcium channel blockers (CCB) are recommended as initial antihypertensive therapy for hypertensive patients with obesity. Most obese patients require combination therapy to adequately control blood pressure. When combining diuretics and beta-blockers, potential metabolic adverse effects should be weighed against the antihypertensive efficacy and indications.

Subgroup analysis from the ACCOMPLISH trial, which aimed to achieve blood pressure control and prevent cardiovascular events, showed no difference in cardiovascular outcomes between obese patients treated with a combination of ACEI and CCB or a combination of ACEI and diuretics.[616]

8.8.2 Other interventions

For hypertensive patients with obesity, although glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RA) and glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP)/GLP-1 dual receptor agonists are not recommended as initial antihypertensive treatments, they can assist in blood pressure control when used for other indications such as diabetes and obesity.[617,618] In patients with severe obesity and hypertension, metabolic surgery can help control blood pressure,[619] and reduce the number and dosage of antihypertensive medications,[620] though there is currently limited evidence on long-term outcomes.

8.9 Hypertension Complicated with Metabolic Syndrome

Key Points 8I: Hypertension Complicated with Metabolic Syndrome

• The prevalence of metabolic syndrome in Chinais high, and it significantly increases the risk ofcoronary heart disease, stroke, and chronic kidneydisease (CKD).

• The treatment of metabolic syndrome emphasizes comprehensive intervention and achievingoverall target control.

The prevalence of metabolic syndrome in China has reached 34%, with higher rates observed in women, the elderly, individuals with low educational levels, and those in economically developed regions. In rural areas, the control of metabolic syndrome components is less satisfactory. Among the Chinese population, metabolic syndrome increases the risk of coronary heart disease by 1.51 times, stroke by 1.20 times, and CKD by 1.49 times.[621,622]

8.9.1 Diagnostic Criteria and Risks

A diagnosis can be made when three or more of the following criteria are met:

Abdominal obesity: Waist circumference ≥ 90 cm in men, ≥ 85 cm in women.

Elevated blood pressure: Blood pressure ≥ 130/85 mmHg and/or a confirmed diagnosis of hypertension with treatment.

Dyslipidemia: Fasting triglycerides ≥ 1.70 mmol/L, fasting HDL-C < 1.04 mmol/L, or a confirmed diagnosis of dyslipidemia with treatment.

Abnormal glucose metabolism: Fasting blood glucose ≥ 6.1 mmol/L or 2-hour postprandial glucose ≥ 7.8 mmol/L, and/or a confirmed diagnosis of diabetes with treatment.[623,624]

In China, the most common type of metabolic syndrome is obesity combined with hypertension and dyslipidemia, accounting for 53.7% of cases, followed by obesity combined with abnormal glucose metabolism and hypertension, accounting for 30.5%.[625]

8.9.2 Interventions

(1) Treatment principles: Early comprehensive intervention to achieve overall control; simultaneous improvement of cardiovascular and metabolic abnormalities; protection of target organs; and reduction of long-term cardiovascular risk.[624]

(2) Lifestyle modifications: Healthy diet and reasonable exercise are essential and effective. Studies in domestic community populations have shown that appropriate physical activity can reduce the risk of metabolic syndrome by 10% to 20%.[626]

(3) Pharmacotherapy: Among the types of metabolic syndrome, those with abdominal obesity combined with dyslipidemia have poorer blood pressure control.[627]

In the use of antihypertensive drugs, ACE inhibitors (ACEIs) and angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) are recommended as first-line treatments, especially for patients with diabetes or obesity;[129] dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers (CCBs) can also be used. For those with heart failure and coronary heart disease, thiazide diuretics and beta-blockers can be applied.[129] The use of SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RA) can help achieve comprehensive targets;[628-629] metabolic surgery can be recommended for refractory metabolic syndrome.[630]

8.10 Antitumor Therapy and Hypertension

Key Points 8J: Antitumor Therapy and Hypertension

• The treatment of hypertension in cancer patientsdepends not only on blood pressure levels and cardiovascular risk stratification but also on the prognosis of the tumor.

• It is recommended that during cancer treatment,the blood pressure target be set at < 40/90 mmHg.For patients with concurrent diabetes or chronickidney disease (CKD), the blood pressure targetshould be reduced to 130/80 mmHg (Class I, LevelC).

• ACEIs or ARBs and dihydropyridine CCBs arerecommended as initial treatment (Class I, Level C).Non-dihydropyridine CCBs are not recommended(Class III, Level C).

Antitumor therapy can induce new-onset hypertension or worsen pre-existing hypertension. The incidence of hypertension in cancer patients receiving vascular endothelial growth factor inhibitors (VEGFi) can be as high as 80%,[631] and the increase in blood pressure can occur within hours to days after starting treatment, with blood pressure levels showing a dose-dependent increase with the drug.[632] Other antitumor drugs (such as chemotherapeutic agents like cyclophosphamide, proteasome inhibitors, poly ADP-ribose polymerase inhibitors, and Bruton’s tyrosine kinase (BTK) inhibitors), as well as adjuvant medications (glucocorticoids, erythropoietin, and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs), can also lead to the development or worsening of hypertension in cancer patients.[108]

8.10.1 Diagnosis and assessment

The diagnostic criteria for hypertension in cancer patients undergoing antitumor therapy are the same as the current diagnostic criteria for adult hypertension. An increase in diastolic blood pressure by 20 mmHg from baseline, even if the blood pressure remains < 140/90 mmHg, is also considered a criterion for diagnosing hypertension in patients receiving antitumor therapy.[633] Blood pressure should be monitored before starting VEGFi treatment, with clinic blood pressure measured weekly during the initial stage of the first treatment cycle, and then at least every 2-3 weeks thereafter. Blood pressure measurements should be accurate and consider potential confounding factors such as adjuvant treatments, anxiety, and pain, which can temporarily raise blood pressure. Therefore, out-of-office blood pressure monitoring is also important, and ambulatory blood pressure monitoring should be used if necessary to obtain accurate assessments. After blood pressure stabilizes following the first treatment cycle, home blood pressure monitoring is recommended to monitor, evaluate, and titrate antihypertensive medications.[634]

8.11 Hypertension Complicated with Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD)

Key Points 8K: Hypertension Complicated with COPD

• For hypertensive patients with a long history ofsmoking or those presenting with symptoms suchas chronic cough, sputum production, and shortness of breath during activity, pulmonary functiontests should be recommended to detect and diagnose COPD early (Class I, Level A).

• When hypertension is complicated by COPD, noadjustment to the treatment for either condition isgenerally required. If necessary, selective β1-receptor blockers can be used (Class I, Level A).

Hypertension and COPD are both common chronic diseases. Among patients with COPD, 40% to 50% also have hypertension.[640] The severity of airflow obstruction in COPD correlates with a higher risk of coexisting chronic conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, and cardiovascular diseases, as well as an increased risk of all-cause hospitalization and mortality related to these chronic conditions.[641] Due to the high prevalence of multiple chronic diseases, it is essential to include conditions like hypertension and COPD in the overall chronic disease management plan for patients.[640]

To ensure early detection and diagnosis of COPD, pulmonary function tests should be recommended for high-risk individuals, such as those with a long history of smoking and those with or without symptoms like chronic cough, sputum production, or shortness of breath during activity. Spirometry is the primary diagnostic method for COPD. A post-bronchodilator forced expiratory volume in the first second (FEV1) to forced vital capacity (FVC) ratio (FEV1/FVC) of less than 70% is the diagnostic criterion for COPD.[642]

When hypertension coexists with COPD, the treatment for both conditions generally does not require adjustment. Clinical concerns often arise due to the use of β-blockers for hypertension (or other cardiovascular diseases) and β-agonists for COPD treatment. However, studies have shown that selective β1-receptor blockers do not adversely affect the pulmonary benefits of inhaled long-acting β2-agonists in COPD patients and do not increase cardiovascular risks, exacerbations of COPD, or all-cause mortality. On the contrary, the use of selective β1-receptor blockers can reduce the risk of acute exacerbations and all-cause mortality in COPD patients.[643-645] Therefore, selective β1-receptor blockers are recommended for hypertensive patients with COPD when clinically indicated, regardless of whether the patient is using inhaled β2-agonists, but non-selective β-blockers are not recommended.

The diagnosis and treatment of COPD should follow the guidelines provided by the COPD guidelines, including standardized treatment and individualized long-term management.[642] In stable COPD patients, the use of long-acting β-agonists and/or long-acting muscarinic antagonists in inhaled formulations is generally considered safe from a cardiovascular perspective.[646,647] For stable COPD patients with heart failure, bronchodilator use may increase the risk of worsening heart failure, and careful monitoring is required during use.[647,648] The use of a triple inhaled therapy combination (“long-acting β-agonist + long-acting muscarinic antagonist + inhaled corticosteroid”) can reduce all-cause mortality in COPD patients without increasing the risk of cardiovascular disease-related death.[649,650]

Lifestyle changes, including smoking cessation and exercise, are essential for managing chronic conditions such as hypertension and COPD. Smoking cessation should be recommended for patients, with appropriate guidance provided.[642] Additionally, exposure to environmental factors such as air pollution, fuel smoke, and occupational dust should be minimized.[642]

8.12 Hypertension and immune system diseases

Key Points 8L: Hypertension and Immune System Diseases

• Actively treat underlying conditions, and comprehensively intervene in concurrent cardiovascular risk factors. Minimize the use of medicationsthat could elevate blood pressure and cause vascular damage during the treatment of the primary disease.

• Refer to general population guidelines for antihypertensive treatment and choose appropriate antihypertensive drugs.

Many immune system diseases can be associated with hypertension. The pathogenesis of some of these conditions remains unclear, often involving multiple mechanisms such as renovascular lesions, renal parenchymal disease, and drug-induced hypertension. Below is a brief overview of antihypertensive treatment strategies that differ from those used in the general hypertensive population.

8.12.1 Diffuse toxic goiter

This type of hypertension is characterized by elevated systolic blood pressure, reduced diastolic blood pressure, and increased pulse pressure.[651] The principle treatment is the aggressive management of the underlying disease. Once the symptoms of hyperthyroidism are controlled, blood pressure generally returns to normal. Antihypertensive drugs may be used as adjunctive therapy. Non-selective beta-blockers are the first-line choice for initial antihypertensive treatment, as they can block the peripheral effects of excessive thyroid hormone and renin release, thus reducing tremors and tachycardia​.[652]

8.12.2 Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE)

For newly diagnosed SLE patients without significant target organ damage, the blood pressure target is 140/90 mmHg. For those with lupus nephritis or diabetes, the target is 130/80 mmHg.[653] The treatment principles include the active control of the underlying disease, the rational adjustment of immunosuppressive drug dosages, and the comprehensive management of all associated cardiovascular risk factors, such as smoking, as well as abnormalities in blood glucose and lipids. Additionally, proper management of hypertension-mediated organ damage is crucial.[654] The first-line antihypertensive drugs recommended are ACE inhibitors (ACEIs) or angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs).[655]

8.12.3 Rheumatoid arthritis

In principle, the antihypertensive treatment for rheumatoid arthritis (RA) follows the recommendations for the general population. Lifestyle modifications should be implemented, and strict, sustained control of RA disease activity is essential. For long-term treatment, the dosage of glucocorticoids should be kept to a minimum, with attempts to taper the dose when possible.[656]

8.12.4 Systemic sclerosis

Hypertension associated with systemic sclerosis is primarily observed in scleroderma renal crisis, characterized by the development of accelerated hypertension and acute kidney injury. Approximately 50% of patients may develop microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, often accompanied by headaches, fatigue, hypertensive retinopathy, hypertensive encephalopathy, pulmonary edema, and cardiac insufficiency.

Early recognition of scleroderma renal crisis is critical, and immediate treatment with ACEI is recommended. The goal of antihypertensive therapy is to reduce systolic blood pressure by 20 mmHg and diastolic blood pressure by 10 mmHg within 24 h, with a target of normalization within 72 h. If ACEI monotherapy does not achieve the desired blood pressure reduction, combination therapy with CCB and diuretics should be considered. Beta-blockers should be avoided as they may exacerbate Raynaud's phenomenon associated with systemic sclerosis. In cases of severe microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, plasma exchange therapy may be indicated.[657]

8.12.5 Immunotherapy-induced hypertension

The treatment principle for immunotherapy-induced hypertension is to control sodium intake and minimize the dosage of the offending medication. For glucocorticoid-induced hypertension, oral diuretics, CCBs, and ACEI/ARBs can be administered. Hypertension caused by conventional disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs) such as leflunomide, cyclosporine A, and tacrolimus can be treated with CCBs (with caution as they may increase blood cyclosporine levels) in combination with multiple antihypertensive agents, including clonidine. Biologic DMARDs, such as anti-CD20 antibodies, may cause hypertension or hypotension, necessitating close monitoring of blood pressure. Hypertension induced by non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) can be managed with CCBs, centrally acting antihypertensive drugs, ACEI/ARBs, and diuretics.[638]

8.13 Management of Perioperative Hypertension

Key Points 8M: Management of Perioperative Hypertension

• Blood pressure management is required whenperioperative blood pressure increases by morethan 30% from baseline or when blood pressure is ≥140/90 mmHg; the target blood pressure should be< 140/90 mmHg (Grade I, Level C).

• Elective surgery should be postponed if preoperative blood pressure is ≥180/110 mmHg (Grade I,Level C). Intraoperative systolic blood pressureshould not fall below 100 mmHg, and mean arterialpressure should not drop below 60 mmHg (GradeIII, Level C).

• Beta-blockers and CCBs used preoperatively canbe continued. However, ACEIs, ARBs, and ARNIsshould be discontinued at least 24 h beforesurgery(Grade I, Level C).

8.13.1 Definition and causes

(1) Definition: Perioperative hypertension refers to an increase in blood pressure (systolic, diastolic, or mean arterial pressure) by more than 30% from baseline, or a systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg during the period from the decision to undergo surgery until the completion of surgery-related treatment. A perioperative hypertensive crisis is defined as a sudden increase in blood pressure during the perioperative period, exceeding 180/110 mmHg.[659-662]

(2) Causes: Primary hypertension is the most significant high-risk factor for elevated perioperative blood pressure, accounting for approximately 90% of cases. Other contributing factors include a history of secondary hypertension with poor preoperative control, improper anesthesia or analgesia, and strong patient anxiety, fear, or poor sleep related to anesthesia or surgery, which can lead to fluctuations in perioperative blood pressure.[660]

8.13.2 Principles and Goals of Blood Pressure Control

The management of perioperative hypertension should focus on prevention. Preoperative risk assessment is essential for hypertensive patients. It is important to ensure adequate perfusion of vital organs, reduce cardiac afterload, and maintain cardiac function.

For patients with Grade 1 or 2 hypertension (blood pressure < 180/110 mmHg), surgery can proceed. However, for those with blood pressure ≥ 180/110 mmHg in the operating room or preoperatively, elective surgery should be delayed. For patients with hypertension combined with other serious conditions, such as acute renal failure or severe hypokalemia (< 2.9 mmol/L), measures should be taken in a short period to improve target organ function and correct internal environment disorders before proceeding with surgery.

The goal for blood pressure control is < 140/90 mmHg. Intraoperative blood pressure fluctuations should not exceed 30% of baseline.[662] For hypertensive emergencies during surgery, the target for intravenous antihypertensive therapy is to reduce diastolic blood pressure to 110 mmHg within 30-60 min, with a reduction of no more than 25% within this time frame. Blood pressure should be gradually reduced to 160/100 mmHg within 2-6 h. In non-cardiac surgery, systolic blood pressure < 100 mmHg and mean arterial pressure < 60 mmHg are detrimental.[663] Therefore, it is recommended that intraoperative mean arterial pressure be maintained above 65 mmHg, with slightly higher targets postoperatively​.[664]

8.13.3 Pharmacological Treatment

The therapeutic doses of CCBs can typically enhance the effects of intravenous anesthetics, inhalation anesthetics, muscle relaxants, and analgesics, and thus can be continued. For patients on ACEIs, ARBs, or ARNIs preoperatively, these should be discontinued 1 day before surgery due to the risk of intraoperative hypotension.[662] Sudden discontinuation of beta-blockers before surgery should be avoided to prevent rebound tachycardia; they should be continued until the morning of surgery.[660] Diuretics should be discontinued 2-3 days before surgery​.

8.14 Psychological Disorders and Hypertension
8.14.1 Treatment with anxiolytics and antidepressants

Anxiety and depression are closely associated with the incidence of hypertension, poor blood pressure control, and low treatment adherence.[665,666] Studies have shown that in hypertensive patients with coexisting anxiety and depression, the use of antidepressants and anxiolytics can help reduce the number and dosage of antihypertensive medications needed and improve the rate of blood pressure control.[204]

According to the “Diagnostic and Therapeutic Guidelines for Mental Disorders (2020 Edition)”, the following treatments are recommended: (1) antidepressants: Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) are the most commonly used antidepressants. (2) Anxiolytics: Common anxiolytics include benzodiazepines and partial agonists of the 5-hydroxytryptamine 1A (5-HT1A) receptor. Benzodiazepines act quickly and are often used in the early stages of anxiety attacks, while 5-HT1A receptor partial agonists usually have a slower onset of action. If the above medications do not effectively alleviate symptoms of anxiety and depression, it is recommended that patients be referred to a mental health specialist. It is important to monitor the increased risk of bleeding with SSRI use and the potential for elevated blood pressure with SNRI use. Both classes of drugs are metabolized by cytochrome P450, so drug interactions with antihypertensive medications should be considered, and antidepressants should be started at the lowest effective dose.[667,668]

It is also important to note that antihypertensive drugs can have an impact on mood.[669] Research, including animal models, epidemiological studies, and genome-wide studies, suggests that the pathways and molecular targets of commonly used antihypertensive drugs may play a role in the pathogenesis or progression of mood disorders.[670] A study involving 144,066 hypertensive patients found that the use of ACEIs or ARBs may be associated with a reduced risk of mood disorders.[671] It is recommended to prioritize the use of RAS inhibitors and diuretics, as these two classes of drugs have fewer interactions with antidepressants. When antidepressants (such as SSRIs) are used in combination with CCBs and alpha-1 receptor blockers, there is a potential risk of orthostatic hypotension, and caution is advised.

8.14.2 Hypertension with Insomnia Disorder

Insomnia, which includes both short-term and chronic (lasting more than 3 months) forms, can have acute or chronic adverse effects on blood pressure. Hypertension combined with insomnia should be treated actively.[672,673] The basic principles of insomnia treatment include addressing the underlying cause, cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), and sleep hygiene education, along with the judicious use of hypnotics when necessary.[674]

CBT-I primarily aims to correct behaviors and beliefs that contribute to insomnia and is the first-line treatment for insomnia disorders. The main components of CBT-I include sleep restriction, stimulus control, cognitive therapy, relaxation training, and sleep hygiene education. A typical course of CBT-I lasts 6-8 weeks, with effects that can persist for 6-12 months. Research shows that CBT-I combined with pharmacotherapy is more effective than pharmacotherapy alone in patients with chronic insomnia. Research indicates that for patients with chronic insomnia, the short-term efficacy of CBT-I is comparable to that of pharmacotherapy. However, in the long term, CBT-I proves to be more effective than medication.

According to the “Diagnostic and Therapeutic Guidelines for Mental Disorders (2020 Edition)”, the pharmacological treatment of insomnia should follow principles of individualized therapy, on-demand use, intermittent dosing, and adequate dosing. Continuous medication use generally should not exceed four weeks; if extended use is necessary, regular monthly evaluations are required. The pharmacological treatments include: (1) Benzodiazepines: these medications should be used with caution, particularly in elderly patients (65 years and older), those with impaired liver function, and individuals with respiratory diseases, where the dosage should be halved. It is also important to monitor for adverse effects such as muscle relaxation and orthostatic hypotension. Patients should be advised to move slowly when getting up to avoid falls. (2) Non-benzodiazepine medications: Similar precautions apply as with benzodiazepines, particularly regarding dosage adjustments in vulnerable populations. (3) Sedative antidepressants: although most of these medications do not have formal approval for treating insomnia, they are commonly used in clinical practice, especially in patients with insomnia who also experience anxiety or depressive symptoms. Physical therapies for insomnia mainly include light therapy, repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS), transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS), and biofeedback therapy. Traditional Chinese medicine has also shown effectiveness in treating insomnia, including the use of sedative and tranquilizing herbs and herbal formulas, as well as acupuncture and massage therapy.

9 RESISTANT HYPERTENSION

Key Points on Resistant Hypertension

• The diagnosis of resistant hypertension requiresmeeting both in-office and out-of-office blood pressure criteria.

• It is essential to rigorously exclude and investigate the causes of pseudo-resistant hypertension.

• Screening for potential secondary causes of hypertension, particularly primary aldosteronism andobstructive sleep apnea, is recommended.

• It is advocated to implement lifestyle improvements, appropriately use antihypertensive medications, and administer drugs at their maximum tolerated doses. If necessary, renal denervation (RDN)treatment may be considered.

From 2009 to 2013, a blood pressure registry study conducted across 59 hospitals in 18 provinces and cities in China showed that the prevalence of resistant hypertension was 8.3%, with the highest prevalence among those aged 60 to 80 and the lowest among those under 40.[675] The study also indicated that patients with resistant hypertension tend to develop hypertension earlier, have a longer disease course, and often have comorbidities such as obesity, smoking, alcohol consumption, and disrupted circadian blood pressure rhythms.

9.1 Definition

Resistant hypertension is defined as a condition where, despite intensive lifestyle interventions and the concurrent use of three different types of antihypertensive medications (including a thiazide diuretic) for at least four weeks, the patient's office blood pressure remains ≥ 140/90 mmHg, the 24-hour ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM) average is ≥ 130/80 mmHg, or the home blood pressure average is ≥ 135/85 mmHg. Alternatively, if blood pressure control requires the use of four or more antihypertensive drugs, it can also be diagnosed as resistant hypertension. Patients using five or more antihypertensive drugs (including a thiazide diuretic and an aldosterone receptor antagonist) who still fail to achieve target blood pressure are diagnosed with refractory hypertension.[676]

9.2 Excluding Pseudo-Resistant Hypertension

It is necessary to routinely exclude common causes of pseudo-resistant hypertension, which include:[677,678]

Measurement errors due to not following recommended blood pressure measurement guidelines.

The white-coat effect causing elevated office blood pressure.

Poor medication adherence.

Inadequate drug combinations or suboptimal dosing.

The use of medications that may raise blood pressure, such as corticosteroids, cyclosporine, erythropoietin, contraceptives, NSAIDs, antidepressants, and licorice-containing traditional Chinese medicines.

Unhealthy lifestyle factors such as excessive salt intake, excessive alcohol consumption (30–50 g/day of alcohol), chronic sleep deprivation, or lack of physical activity.

Poor control of comorbid conditions such as obesity and diabetes.

9.3 Screening for Secondary Causes

It is recommended to focus on screening for the following potential causes: (1) primary aldosteronism: The prevalence of this condition in patients with resistant hypertension can be as high as 20%; (2) chronic kidney disease (CKD): CKD is both a cause of and a common complication in resistant hypertension; (3) renal artery stenosis; (4) thyroid disorders; (5) sleep disorders: this primarily includes conditions characterized by short sleep duration and poor sleep quality; (6) obstructive sleep apnea (OSA): Prevalent in 70%–90% of patients with resistant hypertension. Hospitals lacking the necessary facilities to diagnose these conditions should consider referring patients to higher-level medical centers for screening.

9.4 Comprehensive Assessment

Resistant hypertension requires a thorough assessment that includes a review of medical history, physical examination, both office and out-of-office blood pressure measurements, and various tests such as blood plasma aldosterone/renin ratio, 24-h urinary sodium excretion, thyroid function, kidney function, electrolyte levels, fundoscopic examination, electrocardiography, vascular function tests (including pulse wave velocity and central arterial pressure), renal ultrasound, renal artery ultrasound, and CT scans, as well as echocardiography to evaluate for target organ damage.[678]

9.5 Management Principles

The management of resistant hypertension includes the following principles: (1) patients should be referred to a hospital with specialists experienced in the management of resistant hypertension if such expertise is not available locally.

(2) Use home or ambulatory blood pressure monitoring to assess out-of-office blood pressure.

(3) Evaluate and address medication adherence issues, such as missed doses or discontinuation of therapy, by patiently discussing the reasons with the patient and encouraging consistent medication use.

(4) Assess for adverse effects related to antihypertensive medications and make appropriate adjustments.

(5) Advise on improving unhealthy lifestyle habits, including reducing salt intake, limiting alcohol consumption, engaging in regular physical activity, losing weight, avoiding late nights, and improving sleep quality.

(6) Adjustment of Antihypertensive Therapy: the common medications include long-acting calcium channel blockers (CCBs), ACE inhibitors (ACEIs), angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs), angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitors (ARNIs), and thiazide diuretics. For patients with stage 4 CKD or higher, loop diuretics such as furosemide may be used.

(7) If blood pressure remains uncontrolled, consider adding a fourth antihypertensive drug, preferably an aldosterone receptor antagonist such as spironolactone or eplerenone. Other options include beta-blockers, alpha-blockers, potassium-sparing diuretic combinations, and newer antihypertensive agents (such as endothelin receptor antagonists). Additionally, actively investigate other secondary causes of hypertension.

(8) For patients with resistant hypertension and sleep apnea, continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) therapy can improve blood pressure control.

(9) Evaluate the presence of sleep disorders and excessive psychological stress, and refer the patient to the appropriate specialist for treatment.

(10) For some patients with resistant hypertension, device-based therapies such as renal denervation (RDN) may be considered after comprehensive evaluation by a hypertension specialist. Further details on these therapies can be found in the relevant section of this guideline.

10 SECONDARY HYPERTENSION

Secondary hypertension accounts for approximately 10% of all hypertension cases, though studies in specific populations suggest that this proportion may be higher. Secondary hypertension is caused by other diseases or pathophysiological conditions involving multiple systems such as the kidneys and renal vasculature, endocrine system, sleep respiration, autoimmune conditions, nervous system, hematological disorders, as well as by drugs, mechanical blood flow obstructions, and monogenic mutations. In addition to the cardiovascular risks associated with elevated blood pressure, secondary hypertension is also linked to conditions such as hypokalemia, activation of the renin-angiotensin system (RAS), hyperaldosteronism, hypercortisolism, elevated catecholamine levels, and hypoxemia, which can independently cause cardiovascular damage.

10.1 Renal Parenchymal Hypertension

Renal parenchymal hypertension is one of the most common causes of secondary hypertension. Renal parenchymal diseases can be categorized based on the primary affected area into glomerular diseases, tubulointerstitial diseases, or vascular diseases, and by etiology into primary, secondary, and congenital/hereditary renal parenchymal diseases. Any type of renal parenchymal damage can affect the kidney’s regulation of blood pressure, leading to the development and persistence of hypertension. The mechanism of renal parenchymal hypertension is believed to involve multiple factors, including impaired sodium and water excretion by the kidneys leading to fluid retention, increased sympathetic nervous system activity, excessive activation of RAS, and increased vascular stiffness, which collectively contribute to the condition.

Clinically, patients with renal parenchymal hypertension often exhibit abnormal urine tests, including varying degrees of proteinuria, hematuria, and changes in renal function, such as decreased glomerular filtration rate. Imaging may reveal abnormalities in kidney size and shape, and renal biopsy can assist in confirming the pathological diagnosis. In most cases, renal damage precedes or coincides with the onset of hypertension. Hypertension-induced renal parenchymal damage is usually found in patients with a long-standing history of uncontrolled hypertension and is typically identified during routine evaluations for hypertension-related target organ damage or through investigations for other conditions. The clinical manifestations of these two conditions may overlap, and kidney biopsy may be necessary to clarify the diagnosis. Hypertensive kidney damage is primarily characterized by arteriosclerosis and hyaline degeneration of renal arterioles, accompanied by varying degrees of glomerular ischemia/sclerosis, tubular atrophy, and interstitial fibrosis/inflammation.[679,680]

Renal cysts are a common imaging finding in patients with kidney disease and can result from both genetic and non-genetic causes. Autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD) is a common hereditary cystic kidney disease, primarily caused by mutations in the polycystin-1 (PKD1) or polycystin-2 (PKD2) genes. In ADPKD patients, increased RAS activity and extracellular volume expansion often occur before the decline in glomerular filtration rate (GFR), leading to early-onset hypertension in the disease course. Intensive antihypertensive treatment can reduce the rate of kidney enlargement and slow the decline in GFR in high-risk patients.[592] In the absence of contraindications, RAS inhibitors are the first-line treatment. Tolvaptan, a vasopressin V2 receptor antagonist, is recommended for ADPKD patients with an estimated GFR (eGFR) ≥ 25 mL/min per 1.73 m² and at high risk for kidney disease progression, to slow the rate of eGFR decline.[681]

Non-hereditary renal cysts can be classified as simple or complex, and the Bosniak classification system can assist in their diagnosis and management. Simple renal cysts are usually asymptomatic and benign, often requiring no treatment; however, complex cysts may necessitate imaging follow-up, biopsy, or surgical excision for diagnosis. Hypertension in patients with simple renal cysts is generally attributed to factors unrelated to the cysts, such as primary hypertension. In rare cases, renal cysts may cause angiotensin-dependent hypertension by compressing the surrounding renal parenchyma.

In the treatment of renal parenchymal hypertension, addressing the underlying disease is crucial, which may involve the use of corticosteroids and immunosuppressants. Antihypertensive therapy plays an important role in delaying kidney disease progression and reducing cardiovascular events. The timing and target blood pressure for initiating antihypertensive treatment in renal parenchymal hypertension depend on whether proteinuria is present (for more details, see “8.6 Hypertension with Kidney Disease”). Common antihypertensive drugs can be used in treating renal parenchymal hypertension, with RAS inhibitors often being preferred due to their additional non-blood pressure-dependent effects, such as reducing proteinuria and protecting target organs like the kidneys.[592]

Non-pharmacological treatment emphasizes dietary salt restriction, which can directly lower blood pressure in patients with renal parenchymal hypertension and enhance the blood pressure and proteinuria-lowering effects of RAS inhibitors. It is currently recommended that patients with renal parenchymal hypertension limit their dietary salt (sodium chloride) intake to less than 6 g/day (sodium < 2.3 g/day or < 100 mmol/day).[681] During the initial phase of strict salt restriction, patients with impaired renal function may experience sodium-negative balance due to the kidneys' inability to rapidly reduce sodium excretion, leading to a significant decrease in extracellular volume and potentially causing prerenal acute kidney injury. Monitoring is essential during this period.

10.2 Renovascular Hypertension and Hypertension Caused by Other Vascular Diseases
10.2.1 Renovascular hypertension

Renovascular hypertension is one of the common types of secondary hypertension, resulting from increased systemic blood pressure due to insufficient renal blood flow. The primary cause is renal artery stenosis, which accounts for 3% to 5% of hypertension cases. Other less common causes of renovascular hypertension include renal artery embolism, renal artery aneurysm, arteriovenous fistula, involvement of the abdominal aortic dissection, compression from renal (or extra-renal) tumors, and congenital abnormalities of renal artery development. In China, atherosclerosis is the leading cause of renal artery stenosis, accounting for approximately 82% of cases, followed by Takayasu arteritis (about 12%), and fibromuscular dysplasia (FMD) and other causes accounting for around 6%. The role of renal vein compression, such as in Nutcracker syndrome, in causing renovascular hypertension is still debated. Early stages are generally characterized by hematuria and proteinuria, and later stages may impact renal function, leading to increased blood pressure.

10.2.1.1 Diagnostic evaluation

Clinical clues: (1) onset of hypertension before the age of 30 or after the age of 55; resistant hypertension; malignant hypertension; hypertension with hypokalemia. (2) Unexplained renal failure; worsening renal function after treatment with ACE inhibitors or ARBs; one kidney shrinking or a significant size discrepancy (> 1.5 cm) between the two kidneys. (3) Unexplained heart failure or transient pulmonary edema; previously controlled hypertension that suddenly becomes difficult to manage without changes in antihypertensive medication.

Diagnostic Methods for Renal Artery Stenosis: (1) renal artery ultrasound is the preferred initial imaging screening method. (2) Renal artery CT angiography (CTA) or magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) is important for confirmation. (3) Percutaneous selective renal artery angiography is considered the “gold standard” for diagnosing renal artery stenosis.

Diagnostic evaluation of renovascular hypertension: the assessment should clarify the cause, location, and extent of the lesion, the pathophysiological diagnosis, and whether revascularization will be beneficial.

10.2.1.2 Treatment

All patients with renal artery stenosis require optimized medical therapy, including antihypertensive and antiplatelet therapy. For patients with atherosclerosis, it is also essential to emphasize the use of statins.

ACE inhibitors (ACEIs) or angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) can serve as the foundational medication for patients with renovascular hypertension. However, ACEIs and ARBs are contraindicated in patients with bilateral renal artery stenosis or stenosis of a solitary kidney. Most patients with renovascular hypertension usually require a combination of multiple antihypertensive drugs, including beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers (CCBs), diuretics, and alpha-blockers.

For patients with non-atherosclerotic renal artery stenosis, such as fibromuscular dysplasia (FMD) and Takayasu arteritis, renal artery revascularization treatment is recommended, with renal artery balloon angioplasty as the preferred method. For patients with non-atherosclerotic renal artery stenosis, including those with fibromuscular dysplasia (FMD) or Takayasu arteritis, renal artery revascularization is recommended, with renal artery balloon angioplasty being the preferred method. For patients with atherosclerotic renal artery stenosis, medical therapy is the first-line treatment.[144,580] However, renal artery revascularization is advised in the following situations:[682] (1) patients with renal artery stenosis who also have transient pulmonary edema, heart failure, angina pectoris, or acute coronary syndrome; (2) patients whose blood pressure remains difficult to control and whose renal function continues to deteriorate despite appropriate medical therapy; (3) patients with resistant hypertension combined with bilateral renal artery stenosis or stenosis of a solitary kidney artery; (4) patients with a systolic pressure gradient across the renal artery stenosis ≥ 20 mmHg or a mean pressure gradient ≥ 10 mmHg.

10.2.2 Aortic stenosis

Aortic stenosis can be congenital or acquired. Congenital aortic stenosis is known as coarctation of the aorta, typically occurring near the site of the ductus arteriosus in the aortic isthmus, characterized by localized narrowing or occlusion of the aorta. Acquired aortic stenosis mainly includes conditions caused by Takayasu arteritis, atherosclerosis, and aortic dissection. The fundamental pathophysiological change in this disease is the redistribution of blood flow due to the stenosis, leading to elevated blood pressure proximal to the stenosis and increased cardiovascular load, which can result in left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH), heart failure, or stroke. Distal to the stenosis, reduced blood pressure and renal ischemia lead to sodium and water retention and activation of the renin-angiotensin system (RAS).

The main clinical manifestations of aortic stenosis are hypertension in the upper extremities and hypotension in the lower extremities (ankle-brachial index, ABI < 0.9), with a noticeable vascular murmur upon auscultation in the precordial area and surrounding the back. ABI can be used as a screening tool, and aortic CT angiography (CTA) or magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) are important for definitive diagnosis. Depending on the specific condition, endovascular therapy or open surgery may be chosen. During the active phase of Takayasu arteritis, treatment with corticosteroids and immunosuppressants is necessary.

10.3 Obstructive Sleep Apnea Syndrome (OSAS)

OSAS is a sleep disorder characterized by recurrent upper airway collapse during sleep, leading to intermittent nocturnal hypoxia and hypercapnia. The typical manifestations include snoring with episodes of apnea during sleep and excessive daytime sleepiness. It is currently estimated that about 176 million adults aged 30-69 years in China have OSAS.[683] Among OSAS patients, 50% to 80% also have hypertension, and among hypertensive patients, 30% to 50% have OSAS.[684] The prevalence of OSAS in patients with resistant hypertension can reach 70% to 85%.[685]

OSAS can cause hypertension and, when combined with obesity and other metabolic disorders, increases cardiovascular risk.[686-690] The mechanisms by which OSAS induces hypertension and heightens cardiovascular risk include excessive activation of the sympathetic nervous system and renin-angiotensin system (RAS), endothelial dysfunction, increased oxidative stress, metabolic disturbances, endocrine imbalances, and enhanced systemic inflammatory responses.[691-694]

10.3.1 Screening and Diagnosis

High-risk populations: resistant or masked hypertension; current or previous arrhythmias; unexplained pulmonary hypertension; type 2 diabetes mellitus; recurrent nocturnal angina difficult to control; refractory congestive heart failure; stroke; secondary hypertension patients with conditions such as primary aldosteronism, hypothyroidism, or Cushing’s syndrome

Signs and symptoms: obesity; abnormalities in the nasopharyngeal and maxillofacial anatomical structures; snoring during sleep, witnessed apnea, night sweats, gasping for breath, and sudden awakenings at night; daytime sleepiness; morning headaches and dry mouth; reduced libido; decreased concentration and memory during work; poor sleep quality.

Common physical examination findings: upper airway abnormalities (Mallampati score ≥ 3); neck circumference and waist circumference exceeding the standard (neck circumference > 43 cm in men, > 40 cm in women); body mass index (BMI) ≥ 30 kg/m².

Laboratory and instrumental examinations: thyroid function tests; electrocardiogram (ECG) and echocardiography; chest X-ray, cephalometric X-ray, sinus CT, and lung CT; ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM), which often reveals morning hypertension or a “non-dipper” or “reverse dipper” blood pressure pattern.

Screening tools: widely used screening tools include the STOP-BANG questionnaire (≥ 3 points), the Berlin Questionnaire (≥ 1 point in two categories), the NoSAS score (≥ 8 points), the No-Apnea score (≥ 3 points), and the Epworth Sleepiness Scale (≥ 10 points).

Diagnosis: polysomnography (PSG) is the gold standard for diagnosing OSAS. Portable sleep monitoring devices can also be used for diagnosis. An apnea-hypopnea index (AHI) ≥ 5 events per hour can diagnose OSAS, with 5-14, 15-29, and ≥ 30 events per hour indicating mild, moderate, and severe OSAS, respectively. PSG is also used to diagnose central sleep apnea syndrome.

10.3.2 Treatment

(1) Lifestyle modification and sleep health education: quit smoking and limit alcohol consumption; sleep in a lateral position; engage in regular physical exercise and weight loss; comprehensive control of other cardiovascular risk factors;[695,696] avoid naps at irregular times; discontinue benzodiazepines; cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) for sleep may assist OSAS patients with insomnia.

(2) Antihypertensive medications: research on the effects of antihypertensive drugs on OSAS is limited. Mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs), such as spironolactone, are thought to improve the severity of OSAS and reduce blood pressure.[697] Common antihypertensive drugs and related SPCs (Summary of Product Characteristics) can be used, but central antihypertensive drugs should be used with caution.

(3) Targeted OSAS treatment: (a) positional therapy: suitable for all OSAS patients, especially those with positional OSAS. (b) oral appliances: suitable for patients with simple snoring and mild to moderate OSAS, or severe OSAS patients who are intolerant of CPAP; can be combined with surgery or non-invasive positive airway pressure therapy for severe OSAS. (c) Inspiratory muscle strength training: suitable for patients with simple snoring, mild to moderate OSAS, or severe OSAS intolerant of oral appliances/CPAP; can be used in combination with other therapies. (d) Upper airway surgery: suitable for non-severely obese patients with moderate or mild OSAS, and for severe OSAS patients with grade II or above tonsil hypertrophy. (e) Positive airway pressure (PAP) therapy: including CPAP, automatic PAP (APAP), and bi-level PAP (BiPAP) therapy;[688] particularly effective for moderate to severe OSAS, especially those with excessive daytime sleepiness, decreased sleep-related quality of life, and/or comorbid hypertension; mild OSAS patients with significant symptoms or comorbid cardiovascular disease or diabetes. The blood pressure-lowering effect is relatively stable (2-4 mmHg), but its effect on cardiovascular disease is uncertain and depends largely on patient adherence. (f) Hypoglossal nerve stimulation: A promising treatment for upper airway management in OSAS,[698] though its impact on blood pressure is still unclear.

For patients with resistant hypertension, it is advisable to avoid medications that can elevate blood pressure.

10.5 Primary Aldosteronism
10.5.1 Epidemiology and clinical characteristics

Primary aldosteronism is a clinical syndrome characterized by the autonomous secretion of excessive aldosterone from the zona glomerulosa of the adrenal cortex, which is not suppressed by high sodium intake. The main clinical features include hypertension, elevated aldosterone levels, and suppressed renin activity. Primary aldosteronism accounts for 5% to 15% of hypertension cases,[704] making it one of the most common forms of secondary hypertension. Studies indicate that the prevalence of primary aldosteronism among newly diagnosed hypertensive patients is about 4.1%,[705] and this prevalence increases with higher blood pressure levels, reaching approximately 20% in patients with resistant hypertension.[704]

The typical clinical manifestations of primary aldosteronism include hypertension, high aldosterone levels, and renin suppression. Potassium levels may be normal in the early stages, but as the disease progresses, potassium loss increases, potentially leading to hypokalemia. Research shows that the incidence of hypokalemia in primary aldosteronism is only about 37%.[706]

The common subtypes of primary aldosteronism include aldosterone-producing adenoma (35%), idiopathic hyperaldosteronism (IHA, 60%), and other less common types such as adrenal carcinoma producing aldosterone, familial hyperaldosteronism, and ectopic aldosterone-secreting tumors.

Excess aldosterone continuously activates mineralocorticoid receptors, leading to adverse pathophysiological effects, including vascular inflammation, endothelial cell damage, and interstitial fibrosis in various target organs, including the heart.[707] Compared with patients with primary hypertension, those with primary aldosteronism have a significantly higher risk of cardiovascular diseases such as coronary artery disease, atrial fibrillation, heart failure, and stroke.[708] Additionally, the rates of kidney damage, abnormal glucose metabolism, and bone metabolism disorders are higher in primary aldosteronism patients compared to those with primary hypertension,[709,710] and their quality of life is also lower.[711] Therefore, early screening and treatment of primary aldosteronism are particularly important.

10.5.2 Diagnostic process

The clinical diagnostic process involves three main steps: screening, confirmation, and subtype classification.

Screening targets and main indicators: screening for primary aldosteronism should be considered in the following cases: blood pressure exceeding 150/100 mmHg, resistant hypertension, hypertension combined with spontaneous or diuretic-induced hypokalemia, adrenal incidentaloma, a first-degree relative with primary aldosteronism, sleep apnea syndrome, exclusion of atrial fibrillation caused by structural heart disease or hyperthyroidism, early-onset hypertension,[712] or a family history of cardiovascular events before the age of 40. Newly diagnosed hypertensive patients should be considered for primary aldosteronism screening. Hypokalemia is no longer a necessary condition for screening.[713]

The aldosterone-to-renin ratio (ARR) is used as a screening indicator.[714] There are currently two methods for measuring renin: plasma renin activity and direct renin concentration. Before screening, antihypertensive medications that have minimal impact on the renin-angiotensin system (RAS) should be adjusted, and drugs with minimal effect on ARR (such as non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers and alpha-blockers) should be used. Mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRA), diuretics, and licorice-containing products should be discontinued 4 weeks before screening, and RAS inhibitors, dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers, and beta-blockers should be discontinued 2 weeks prior. If renin activity is below the detection limit while taking the aforementioned renin-stimulating drugs, and the ARR is elevated, the original medication regimen may be maintained. Hypokalemia should be corrected, and normal sodium intake should be ensured. If the patient's ARR exceeds the cutoff value, specifically ARR > 30 when calculated with renin activity [aldosterone units in ng/dL (1 ng/dL: 27.7 pmol/L), renin activity units in μg/(L·h)]; or ARR > 3.7 when calculated with renin concentration (aldosterone units in ng/dL, renin concentration units in mU/L), and plasma aldosterone concentration is > 15 ng/dL, the diagnostic phase should be initiated.

Confirmation tests: the main confirmation tests include the intravenous saline infusion test, captopril challenge test, fludrocortisone suppression test,[713] and high-sodium diet test. The fludrocortisone suppression test is rarely conducted in China due to the lack of availability of the drug. It is recommended to use at least one or more confirmation methods in combination. Patients with a plasma aldosterone concentration > 20 ng/dL, renin levels below detectable limits, and spontaneous hypokalemia can be directly diagnosed with primary aldosteronism.

Subtype diagnosis: the purpose of subtype diagnosis is to guide the choice of treatment. Methods include adrenal imaging studies and adrenal venous sampling (AVS).

It is recommended that all patients diagnosed with primary aldosteronism undergo thin-slice contrast-enhanced adrenal CT. CT has a better spatial resolution than MRI, but it cannot effectively distinguish smaller aldosterone-producing adenomas or identify functional differences in aldosterone secretion between the adrenal glands. Therefore, adrenal CT is primarily used to detect larger aldosterone-secreting adrenal cortical carcinomas (> 4 cm) and to provide adrenal vein distribution and anatomical localization information to radiologists performing AVS or urologists performing adrenalectomy. MRI has lower spatial resolution than adrenal CT and may be considered in patients with impaired renal function who cannot undergo contrast-enhanced adrenal CT.

For patients with a confirmed diagnosis of primary aldosteronism who are considering surgery and meet the surgical criteria, AVS is necessary. AVS can differentiate between unilateral adrenal disease (including aldosterone-producing adenoma and unilateral adrenal hyperplasia) and bilateral adrenal disease (idiopathic hyperaldosteronism). For patients under 35 years old with typical manifestations of primary aldosteronism (high aldosterone, suppressed PRA, hypokalemia, unilateral adrenal mass > 10 mm in diameter), and no abnormalities in the contralateral adrenal gland, AVS may be unnecessary.[706] AVS is an invasive procedure, and the primary adverse reactions include bleeding at the puncture site, adrenal vein rupture, or adrenal hematoma.

Genetic testing is recommended for young patients with primary aldosteronism or those with a family history of primary aldosteronism and early-onset stroke. Currently, four types of familial hyperaldosteronism (FH) are known: FH-I, FH-II, FH-III, and FH-IV. FH-I, also known as glucocorticoid-remediable aldosteronism (GRA), is caused by an unequal genetic recombination between CYP11B1 (11β-hydroxylase) and CYP11B2 (aldosterone synthase), resulting in a CYP11B chimeric gene regulated by adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) and possessing aldosterone synthase activity.[715] FH-II, FH-III, and FH-IV are caused by germline mutations in the chloride voltage-gated channel 2 (CLCN2), potassium inwardly rectifying channel subfamily J member 5 (KCNJ5), and calcium voltage-gated channel subunit α1H (CACNA1H) genes, respectively, leading to increased aldosterone synthase activity.[716-718]

Monitoring of electrolytes and renal function is necessary during treatment. For patients with glucocorticoid-remediable aldosteronism (GRA), low-dose dexamethasone can be used, while other types of familial hyperaldosteronism are treated with MRAs.[722,723]

Recent clinical trials in China and abroad have shown that percutaneous adrenal ablation is a safe and effective treatment for primary aldosteronism, with few adverse effects, making it an option for patients who are unsuitable for surgery or do not respond well to medical interventions.

10.6 Pheochromocytoma and Paraganglioma
10.6.1 Definition, prevalence, and genetic mechanisms

Pheochromocytoma (PCC) originates from the adrenal medulla, while paraganglioma (PGL) arises from the sympathetic ganglia along the spinal column in the thoracic, abdominal, and pelvic regions. Both are classified as neuroendocrine tumors and are collectively referred to as pheochromocytoma and paraganglioma (PPGL). These tumors can secrete large amounts of catecholamines, including norepinephrine, epinephrine, and dopamine. However, head and neck paragangliomas (HNPGL),[724,725] which are located along the neck and base of the skull and originate from the glossopharyngeal and vagus nerves, do not produce catecholamines. If metastases occur in non-chromaffin tissues or other soft tissues, the condition is no longer considered malignant but is instead termed metastatic PPGL. The tumors are staged from I to IV according to their location, the primary tumor, and the presence of metastases.[726]

Prevalence of PPGL: The prevalence of PPGL in general hypertensive outpatient clinics is about 0.2% to 0.6%, 1.7% in children with hypertension, and 5% in patients with adrenal incidentalomas. Among PPGL cases, 80% to 85% are pheochromocytomas (PCC), and 15% to 20% are paragangliomas (PGL). Approximately 35% to 40% of PPGL cases are hereditary, and 10% to 17% are metastatic, with PCC accounting for 5% to 20% and sympathetic PGL for 15% to 35%. The peak incidence occurs between 30 and 50 years of age, with no significant gender difference.

About 50% of PPGL patients have pathogenic gene mutations, with 35% to 40% being germline mutations and 15% to 25% being somatic mutations. Among children with PPGL, 50% to 70% have germline mutations. In adults, 43% to 71% of metastatic PPGL cases are associated with mutations in the gene encoding succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) subunit B. Depending on the SDH gene subtype involved, PGL is categorized into types PGL 1 to 5.[724]

10.6.2 Clinical manifestations

(1) Hypertension: hypertension associated with pheochromocytoma and paraganglioma (PPGL) can be paroxysmal, sustained, or a combination of sustained and paroxysmal. Approximately 70% of patients may experience orthostatic hypotension. Most cases are characterized by resistant hypertension, though a small percentage of patients may have normal blood pressure. About 10% of patients may experience hypertensive crises due to PPGL, which can be life-threatening.

(2) Classic triad: The classic triad of symptoms includes headaches, palpitations, and excessive sweating.

(3) Multi-system dysfunction: PPGL can cause a wide range of symptoms, signs, and severe complications across multiple systems, including cardiovascular, gastrointestinal, urinary, neuropsychiatric, hematological, and endocrine-metabolic disorders.

(4) Palpable abdominal tumor: in about 15% of patients, an abdominal mass may be palpable during physical examination, and the tumor can cause increased blood pressure due to compression.

(5) Familial paraganglioma syndromes: patients with familial paraganglioma (PGL) syndromes may also develop gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GISTs), renal cell carcinoma, pituitary adenomas, and other hereditary syndromes such as multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2 (MEN2), von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) syndrome, and neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1).

(6) Pheochromocytoma and paraganglioma: patients with hereditary pheochromocytoma and paraganglioma (PPGL) tend to present with an earlier onset and multiple lesions.

10.6.3 Screening

The following populations are recommended for screening:[724,725] (1) patients exhibiting symptoms and signs of PPGL, such as paroxysmal hypertension accompanied by the classic triad of headaches, palpitations, and excessive sweating, as well as those with orthostatic hypotension.

(2) Individuals who experience PPGL attacks triggered by dopamine receptor antagonists, sympathomimetics, opioids, norepinephrine, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), or monoamine oxidase inhibitors.

(3) Patients with adrenal incidentalomas.

(4) Individuals with a family history of related genetic syndromes.

(5) Patients with a personal history of PPGL.

10.6.4 Qualitative and Localization Examinations and Genetic Testing

It is recommended to use liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS), liquid chromatography-electrochemical detection (LC-ECD), or high-performance liquid chromatography-electrochemical detection (HPLC-ECD) methods to measure catecholamine metabolites, including plasma free or 24-hour urinary metanephrines, normetanephrines, and 3-methoxytyramine. Additionally, measurements of norepinephrine, epinephrine, dopamine, and their terminal metabolites (homovanillic acid and vanillylmandelic acid) in blood or urine are recommended.[724,727]

Individualized localization examinations should be selected based on the patient's condition. CT is recommended as the first choice for tumor localization imaging.[724,728,729] MRI is used for paragangliomas at the base of the skull and neck, in patients without retained metallic foreign bodies, in those allergic to CT contrast agents, and in children or pregnant women where radiation exposure should be minimized. When necessary, positron emission tomography (PET)-CT can be performed.[730] For patients with metastatic or inoperable PPGL, ^131I-metaiodobenzylguanidine (^131I-MIBG) scintigraphy should be performed first to evaluate the potential for treatment.[731]

All patients with PPGL should undergo genetic testing in a qualified laboratory, with the selection of appropriate pathogenic gene testing based on tumor localization, nature, catecholamine biochemical phenotype, and family history.

10.6.5 Treatment

Pheochromocytoma and paraganglioma (PPGL) are complex endocrine diseases that require a multidisciplinary approach for phased and coordinated diagnosis and treatment. Except for head and neck paragangliomas (HNPGL) and tumors secreting dopamine, other patients should be pre-treated with α-receptor blockers for 2-4 weeks. If tachycardia is present, β-receptor blockers may be added. Most pheochromocytomas (PCC) are treated with laparoscopic minimally invasive surgery, while most paragangliomas (PGL), tumors larger than 6 cm, and invasive PPGLs are treated with open surgery. For bilateral PCC, partial cortical-sparing adrenalectomy should be considered.[732]

Criteria for adequate preoperative preparation: (1) blood pressure ≤ 140/90 mmHg in patients with persistent hypertension, with reduced frequency and severity of paroxysmal hypertension episodes; (2) Restoration of blood volume without significant orthostatic hypotension; (3) improvement in hypermetabolic symptoms and abnormal glucose metabolism; (4) The duration of preoperative medication preparation varies individually, typically lasting 2-4 weeks. For patients with severe complications, the preparation time should be extended, and a multidisciplinary consultation is recommended.

10.6.6 Prognosis and follow-up

Patients with PPGL should receive individualized management. Blood pressure and heart rate should be closely monitored for 24-48 h postoperatively. Lifelong follow-up is generally required, with at least one follow-up visit per year. For children, adolescents, patients with a family history of PPGL, genetic mutations, or metastatic PPGL, follow-up every 3-6 months is recommended. Regular testing for catecholamines, metanephrines, and normetanephrines, as well as imaging studies, should be conducted. Direct relatives of these patients should undergo genetic testing and regular check-ups.

10.7 Other Endocrine Hypertension
10.7.1 Cushing's syndrome

Cushing's syndrome, also known as hypercortisolism, is a clinical syndrome characterized by excessive secretion of glucocorticoids from the adrenal cortex. It presents with central obesity, a moon face, a plethoric appearance, purple striae, hypertension, hyperglycemia, and osteoporosis. The causes include pituitary ACTH tumors, cortisol-secreting adrenal adenomas, and ectopic ACTH-secreting neuroendocrine tumors (such as small cell lung cancer). Excessive use of exogenous steroids can also cause Cushing's syndrome. Hypertension is very common in Cushing's syndrome, with approximately 80% of patients affected, and about 20% of those with exogenous hypercortisolism also developing hypertension.[733] The mechanisms by which hypercortisolism induces hypertension are complex and include overactivation of mineralocorticoid receptors (due to saturation of 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 2), increased sympathetic nervous system activity, an imbalance between vasoconstrictor and vasodilator factors, and vascular remodeling. Patients with hypertension combined with adrenal incidentalomas, hypokalemia, and disorders of glucose and lipid metabolism should be screened for cortisol rhythm to detect subclinical Cushing's syndrome.

Currently, the recommended diagnostic tests for Cushing's syndrome are: 1 mg overnight dexamethasone suppression test, 24-h urinary free cortisol, and midnight salivary cortisol measurement.[733] The localization diagnosis of Cushing's syndrome primarily relies on ACTH measurement, pituitary MRI, adrenal CT, and bilateral inferior petrosal sinus sampling. The hypertension associated with Cushing's syndrome is related to the excess secretion of cortisol; thus, restoring normal cortisol levels through surgery is the first-line treatment for hypertension in Cushing's syndrome patients. For those who are undergoing preoperative preparation, have failed surgery, or are unable to undergo surgery, the first choice of antihypertensive medication is RAS inhibitors (ACEI or ARB), followed by CCBs. If hypokalemia is present, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRA) such as spironolactone or eplerenone are preferred. If further blood pressure control is needed, alpha-blockers can be considered.[733] Beta-blockers should be used cautiously as they may exacerbate metabolic disturbances in Cushing's syndrome.

10.7.2 Thyroid disorders

Both hyperthyroidism and hypothyroidism can cause hypertension. Hyperthyroidism is a syndrome characterized by an increased synthesis and secretion of thyroid hormones, leading to a state of hypermetabolism. Clinical manifestations include palpitations, sweating, increased appetite, frequent bowel movements, and weight loss.

In patients with hyperthyroidism, hypertension is primarily characterized by an increase in systolic blood pressure and widened pulse pressure. The elevated levels of thyroid hormones can directly enhance myocardial contractility and increase the sensitivity of the myocardium to catecholamines, indirectly increasing cardiac output and leading to higher systolic pressure. In contrast, patients with hypothyroidism often present with elevated diastolic blood pressure, which is related to increased peripheral vascular resistance due to the lack of thyroid hormones. The treatment of hypertension in both hyperthyroid and hypothyroid patients should focus on addressing the underlying thyroid disorder. For hyperthyroidism patients, beta-blockers are often the first choice for blood pressure control.

10.7.3 Congenital adrenal hyperplasia

Congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH) is a group of autosomal recessive disorders caused by mutations in genes encoding enzymes involved in the biosynthesis of steroid hormones in the adrenal cortex. Deficiencies in 17α-hydroxylase and 11β-hydroxylase can lead to CAH, which may cause hypertension. The characteristics of 17α-hydroxylase deficiency include hypertension, hypokalemia, and sexual infantilism. Hypertension and hypokalemia are due to the accumulation of mineralocorticoid-active metabolites, such as deoxycorticosterone, while sexual infantilism results from impaired synthesis of androgens and estrogens. In contrast, 11β-hydroxylase deficiency is characterized by hypertension, hypokalemia, and virilization or precocious puberty in females (due to excess androgen). This condition is primarily associated with the adrenal production of excessive amounts of deoxycorticosterone and androgen precursors, such as dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate (DHEA-S).[734]

For children presenting with hypertension and hypokalemia along with low renin and low aldosterone, screening for CAH is recommended. Diagnostic evaluations should include cortisol, ACTH, secondary sexual characteristics, and sex hormones. Genetic testing is necessary for confirmation. The management of hypertension in CAH primarily relies on effective glucocorticoid replacement therapy, which suppresses ACTH production and reduces the overproduction of mineralocorticoids. Antihypertensive treatment typically includes spironolactone and calcium channel blockers (CCBs).

10.7.4 Rare endocrine hypertension

Other rare forms of endocrine hypertension include acromegaly, renin-secreting tumors, and hyperparathyroidism.

Acromegaly is a condition characterized by excessive production of growth hormone, leading to excessive growth of soft tissues, bones, and cartilage. It causes changes in facial features, enlargement of extremities such as hands and feet, thickening of the skin, enlargement of internal organs, and bone and joint disorders. Approximately 30% of patients with acromegaly also have hypertension.[735] The hypertension associated with acromegaly is related to factors such as increased extracellular fluid volume, increased peripheral vascular resistance, and sleep apnea.

Renin-secreting tumors are rare benign renal tumors that typically present with resistant hypertension, high renin levels, hyperaldosteronism, and hypokalemia. Surgery is the primary treatment for these tumors.

Hyperparathyroidism is a disease caused by excessive secretion of parathyroid hormone, leading to abnormalities in calcium and phosphorus metabolism and bone disease. Some patients may also present with elevated blood pressure, although the exact mechanism is not entirely understood.[736]

The treatment of these rare forms of endocrine hypertension should follow general hypertension management guidelines, while also addressing the underlying endocrine disorders.

10.8 Monogenic Hypertension

Monogenic hypertension refers to a type of hypertension caused by mutations in a single gene, following Mendelian inheritance patterns.[737] These conditions often manifest at an early age and are usually characterized by resistant hypertension, with severe damage to target organs such as the heart, brain, and kidneys. Currently, about 20 types of monogenic hypertension have been identified, involving over 40 pathogenic genes.

Based on the functions of the related genes, monogenic hypertension can be classified into three main categories: (1) Mutations Directly Affecting the Sodium Transport System in the Distal Nephron: These mutations lead to increased sodium reabsorption, resulting in hypertension. Examples include Liddle syndrome, Gordon syndrome, and apparent mineralocorticoid excess (AME). (2) Mutations Leading to Abnormal Steroid Synthesis in the Adrenal Gland: These mutations result in abnormal activation of mineralocorticoid receptors in the distal nephron, contributing to hypertension. This category includes familial hyperaldosteronism (as discussed in section “10.5 Primary Aldosteronism”) and congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH) caused by deficiencies in 17α-hydroxylase or 11β-hydroxylase. (3) Mutations in Genes Associated with Neuroendocrine Tumors: These mutations are related to hypertension observed in tumor syndromes, including multiple endocrine neoplasia, hereditary pheochromocytoma, and paraganglioma.[738]

Among these, Liddle syndrome is relatively common, while the other types are rare, often reported as individual cases.[739] The diagnosis of monogenic hypertension relies on genetic testing. Once diagnosed, most patients can receive effective targeted treatment. However, missing or delaying the diagnosis can lead to serious target organ damage and poor prognosis. Additionally, once a pathogenic gene mutation is identified in the proband, it is recommended that direct relatives in the family undergo Sanger sequencing to check for the same gene mutation. Routine genetic testing for monogenic hypertension in all hypertensive patients is not recommended.

10.9 Connective Tissue Disease (CTD) and Hypertension

Connective tissue diseases (CTDs) are a group of autoimmune, chronic inflammatory disorders. Conditions such as systemic lupus erythematosus, rheumatoid arthritis, systemic sclerosis, and Sjögren's syndrome often lead to atherosclerosis, hypertension, and an increased risk of early cardiovascular events.[740]

Mechanisms Leading to Hypertension in CTD: in addition to traditional risk factors such as genetics, abnormalities in glucose and lipid metabolism, and obesity, CTDs have unique mechanisms that contribute to hypertension. These include renal damage, the effects of antirheumatic drugs, chronic vascular inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and arteriosclerosis mediated by immune regulatory molecules and inflammatory factors.[740,741]

Hypertension is rarely the initial symptom of CTD. However, hypertension is more likely to occur during the course of immune-related kidney disease, or during treatment with steroids and antirheumatic drugs. CTD is often associated with early-onset atherosclerosis and cardiovascular disease that cannot be explained by conventional risk factors. Early diagnosis and effective treatment of CTD can improve prognosis. It is important to conduct preliminary screening for CTD in hypertensive patients who are at risk. If CTD is highly suspected, referral to a rheumatology and immunology specialist for diagnosis and treatment is recommended.

Treatment: specialized treatment for CTD includes the use of corticosteroids, immunosuppressants, and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) targeting the primary disease and symptomatic treatment. For the treatment of CTD-associated hypertension, common antihypertensive drugs can be used either as monotherapy or in combination. Studies have shown that RAS inhibitors (ACEIs, ARBs) may have potential benefits in improving endothelial function and reducing inflammation markers.[742] When necessary, statins and antiplatelet therapy should be actively administered. Research indicates that statin therapy in CTD patients can lower cholesterol, reduce cardiovascular disease, and decrease all-cause mortality, providing significant clinical benefits.[742]

10.10 Hematological Diseases and Hypertension

Common hematological diseases leading to hypertension include polycythemia vera (PV) and multiple myeloma (MM).

Polycythemia Vera (PV): PV is a myeloproliferative neoplasm typically occurring in middle to older age, often associated with mutations in the Janus kinase 2 (JAK2) gene.[743] Clinical features commonly include headache, dizziness, a ruddy complexion, thrombotic and hemorrhagic events, hyperuricemia, splenomegaly, and hypertension, which is often related to increased blood volume. Hypertension is also a risk factor for thrombus formation in PV patients. The diagnostic criteria for PV include a hemoglobin concentration > 16.5 g/dL and hematocrit > 49% in males, or hemoglobin > 16.0 g/dL and hematocrit > 48% in females, along with trilineage hyperplasia in the bone marrow and JAK2 gene mutation as major diagnostic criteria. A reduced erythropoietin level is a minor criterion.[744] PV patients require treatment by a hematologist, including phlebotomy and bone marrow suppression therapy with hydroxyurea as the first-line treatment, along with antiplatelet and symptomatic treatments. For hypertension control, RAS inhibitors, calcium channel blockers (CCBs), and other antihypertensive drugs are commonly used, though treatment must be carefully managed given the patient's condition.

Multiple Myeloma (MM): MM is a clonal plasma cell malignancy characterized by the proliferation of malignant plasma cells in the bone marrow and the production of monoclonal immunoglobulin. Hypertension in MM patients may be related to the use of certain medications, such as steroids, or due to kidney damage caused by light chain deposition.[745,746] Effective control of MM, along with management of any renal impairment and careful monitoring of blood pressure, is crucial for these patients. Treatment strategies for hypertension in MM typically involve the use of antihypertensive medications in line with standard hypertension management practices, tailored to the individual patient's condition and comorbidities.

11 HYPERTENSIVE EMERGENCIES AND URGENCIES

Hypertensive emergencies and urgencies were previously collectively referred to as hypertensive crises. These conditions involve a significant elevation in blood pressure, which may or may not be accompanied by progressive damage to target organs.[747]

11.1 Definition and Assessment of Hypertensive Emergencies and Urgencies
11.1.1 Hypertensive emergencies

Hypertensive emergencies refer to a clinical syndrome characterized by a sudden and significant increase in blood pressure (typically systolic > 180 mmHg and/or diastolic > 120 mmHg) in patients with primary or secondary hypertension. This rise in blood pressure is associated with acute and ongoing damage to target organs, or a worsening of pre-existing organ dysfunction. Hypertensive emergencies include conditions such as hypertensive encephalopathy, intracranial hemorrhage (cerebral hemorrhage and subarachnoid hemorrhage), cerebral infarction, acute heart failure, pulmonary edema, acute coronary syndromes (unstable angina, non-ST-elevation and ST-elevation myocardial infarction), aortic dissection, pheochromocytoma crisis, drug-induced hypertension (e.g., from amphetamines, cocaine, hallucinogens), perioperative hypertension, and eclampsia.[108,144]

It is important to note that the severity of acute target organ damage does not always correlate with the absolute level of blood pressure. Some hypertensive emergencies may occur without particularly high blood pressure levels. For example, in cases associated with pregnancy or certain forms of acute glomerulonephritis, failure to promptly control blood pressure within a reasonable range can cause severe organ damage or even be life-threatening. Cases with acute pulmonary edema, aortic dissection, or myocardial infarction should be treated as hypertensive emergencies even if the blood pressure is only moderately elevated.

11.1.2 Hypertensive urgencies

Hypertensive urgencies are characterized by a significant elevation in blood pressure without acute target organ damage. Patients may experience symptoms such as severe headache, chest tightness, nosebleeds, or agitation due to the marked increase in blood pressure. Many patients with hypertensive urgencies may have poor medication adherence or inadequate treatment.[108,144]

11.1.3 Distinguishing between hypertensive emergencies and urgencies

The degree of blood pressure elevation is not the criterion for differentiating between hypertensive emergencies and urgencies. The key distinction lies in the presence or absence of new, acute, and progressive target organ damage. The treatment and prognosis differ between the two conditions.

11.1.4 Assessment of target organ damage

Acute, progressive hypertension-mediated organ damage is the hallmark of hypertensive emergencies and a critical factor in determining patient prognosis. Therefore, it is crucial to assess whether there is progressive worsening of target organ function as quickly as possible to identify hypertensive emergencies early and provide timely treatment to improve outcomes. Comprehensive history-taking, physical examination, and laboratory tests should be performed to evaluate target organ involvement. The specific steps for evaluation are outlined in Table 32. 11.2 Treatment of Hypertensive Emergencies and Urgencies
11.2.1 Treatment of hypertensive emergencies

Treatment principles: hypertensive emergencies are characterized by acute, progressive target organ damage, with rapid changes in clinical status. Patients with such critical conditions should receive intensive monitoring and treatment, with continuous observation of changes in their condition. It is important to remove or correct the triggers and underlying causes of elevated blood pressure; sedatives may be used as needed to alleviate the patient's anxiety.

Elevated blood pressure is a direct cause of target organ damage and may further exacerbate organ dysfunction. Therefore, early, rapid, and safe blood pressure control is crucial for patients with hypertensive emergencies. However, it is important to note that lowering blood pressure too quickly or excessively can result in insufficient organ perfusion and further organ damage. Thus, the extent and speed of blood pressure reduction should be individualized based on the patient's clinical characteristics and the type of hypertensive emergency to ensure adequate organ perfusion.[108,144,747-750]

Medication selection: medication should be selected based on the function of the affected target organs and the status of liver and kidney function. It is necessary to consider the drug's impact on cardiac output, organ perfusion, hemodynamics, liver and kidney function, and potential adverse effects. The ideal medication should provide predictable control over the intensity and speed of blood pressure reduction, protect target organ function, and allow for easy adjustment. Once blood pressure is stabilized with initial intravenous medication and target organ function improves, oral medications can be introduced, and intravenous drugs can be gradually tapered off.

Extent and speed of blood pressure reduction: When lowering blood pressure, it is essential to consider the patient's age, disease course, the degree and speed of blood pressure elevation, target organ damage, and any coexisting clinical conditions, to develop an individualized treatment plan. Controlled blood pressure reduction should be carried out in a manner that does not compromise organ perfusion, gradually adjusting blood pressure to an appropriate level. The faster the blood pressure rise, the quicker the reduction should be; conversely, if the hypertension has a longer course, the reduction should be slower. The goal is to minimize or prevent damage to the heart, brain, kidneys, and other target organs, while avoiding uncontrolled rapid blood pressure reductions that could lead to complications.[108,144]

In general, the initial phase (within minutes to 1 h) aims to reduce mean arterial pressure (MAP) by no more than 25% of the pre-treatment level. Over the following 2-6 h, blood pressure should be lowered to a safer level, generally around 160/100 mmHg. If this blood pressure level is tolerable and the clinical situation stabilizes, blood pressure should be gradually reduced to normal levels over the next 24-48 h.

The extent and speed of blood pressure reduction may vary depending on the specific target organ damage present in hypertensive emergencies, and the blood pressure targets should also consider the specific requirements of organ treatment.

For hypertensive emergencies combined with acute coronary syndromes or acute left heart failure, blood pressure should be quickly reduced to levels that improve cardiac blood supply, reduce myocardial oxygen consumption, decrease resistance load, and enhance cardiac function.

In cases of hypertensive emergencies with aortic dissection, where the condition can progress rapidly and mortality rates can increase within hours, it is generally recommended that the speed and extent of blood pressure reduction be more aggressive. Blood pressure should be quickly reduced to the lowest level necessary to maintain basic organ perfusion, with an initial target systolic pressure of 100-120 mmHg within the first hour. Combined antihypertensive therapy is often required, and adequate use of β-blockers is emphasized. If β-blockers are contraindicated, non-dihydropyridine CCBs can be considered to control blood pressure, while also managing heart rate and suppressing myocardial contractility.

For patients with acute ischemic stroke and elevated blood pressure within 24 h, blood pressure management should be handled cautiously, first addressing conditions such as anxiety, pain, nausea, vomiting, and elevated intracranial pressure. For patients with acute ischemic stroke who are not undergoing intravenous thrombolysis or endovascular treatment, it is recommended to initiate antihypertensive therapy when systolic blood pressure is ≥ 200 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure is ≥ 110 mmHg.[522] For those who are candidates for intravenous thrombolysis or endovascular treatment, blood pressure should be controlled to ≤ 180/100 mmHg before treatment, using intravenous medications such as urapidil, labetalol, or nicardipine.[522]

For patients with acute hemorrhagic stroke and hypertension, a comprehensive assessment of the patient's blood pressure should be conducted, analyzing the causes of the elevation before deciding on antihypertensive therapy. It is recommended to control systolic blood pressure within the range of 130-140 mmHg during the acute phase. Early, aggressive blood pressure lowering is considered safe, though its effectiveness in improving outcomes requires further validation. Close monitoring of blood pressure changes is essential during antihypertensive treatment.

In cases of severe preeclampsia or eclampsia, or catecholamine crisis, the target for blood pressure control during the first hour should be a systolic blood pressure of < 140 mmHg.

Precautions: blood pressure control in hypertensive emergencies should aim for rapid reduction while ensuring adequate perfusion of vital organs. Close monitoring of target organ function, such as changes in neurological symptoms and signs, or worsening chest pain, is crucial. In patients with existing target organ damage, excessively rapid or aggressive blood pressure reduction can lead to decreased tissue perfusion pressure and may trigger ischemic events.

11.2.2 Treatment of hypertensive urgencies

In patients with hypertensive urgencies, blood pressure can be gradually reduced to 160/100 mmHg over 24-48 h. There is no evidence to suggest that urgent blood pressure reduction in this situation improves prognosis. Many patients with hypertensive urgencies can be managed with oral antihypertensive medications such as CCBs, ACEIs, ARBs, alpha-blockers, beta-blockers, or loop diuretics as needed. Initial treatment can take place in an outpatient clinic or emergency room, with observation for 5-6 h after medication administration. Doses may be adjusted at a follow-up visit 2-3 days later, and long-acting medications can be used to achieve the final target blood pressure.

Patients with hypertensive urgencies who visit the emergency room should receive recommendations for adjusting their oral medication therapy once initial blood pressure control is achieved, and be advised to attend regular outpatient follow-ups. Many patients, unaware of the need for medication adjustments, continue with suboptimal treatment regimens after an emergency visit, leading to recurrent hypertensive urgencies and potentially severe consequences. High-risk patients with hypertensive urgencies, such as those with cardiovascular diseases, may also require hospitalization.

12 COMMUNITY STRATEGIES FOR HYPERTENSION PREVENTION AND STANDARDIZED MANAGEMENT

Key Points 12: Community Hypertension Prevention and Management

• Early Detection: Early detection of hypertensionis the first step in prevention and treatment. Establish a “first-visit blood pressure measurement” system in community and primary healthcare institutions and provide opportunities for opportunisticblood pressure measurement.

• Integration into General Practice: Integrate hypertension management into the routine work ofgeneral practitioners by establishing a tiered diagnosis and treatment system focused on generalpractitioners to ensure smooth two-way referrals.Gradually develop a networked information management system.

• Self-Care Awareness: Enhance the awareness ofhypertension patients regarding disease prevention,self-care, and self-management. Promote and popularize the use of home blood pressure monitoringtechniques.

12.1 Community Strategies for Hypertension Prevention

Community hypertension prevention strategies should encompass comprehensive measures targeting the general population, high-risk groups for hypertension, patients with hypertension, and those at high risk of cardiovascular diseases. These strategies should integrate primary, secondary, and tertiary prevention interventions.

12.1.1 General population strategy

The general population strategy primarily uses health promotion theories, focusing on the following aspects: (1) promotion of healthy lifestyles: emphasize the reduction of salt intake and weight control. (2) Early detection and screening: promote the early detection and proactive screening of patients with hypertension, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia. (3) Health education: seek support and cooperation from local governments and public health committees within the community to carry out various forms of hypertension prevention and “three highs (hypertension, hyperglycemia, hyperlipidemia)” management education and publicity. (4) Strengthening health management systems: collaborate across multiple sectors to promote grid-based, contract-based, and intelligent health management for residents, and mobilize the whole society to participate in hypertension prevention. (5) Venue-based interventions: health promotion activities should be tailored to different venues, which include administrative regions, hospitals, residential communities, workplaces, and schools. Intervention plans should be formulated and implemented according to the characteristics of these locations. (6) Health promotion methods: utilize new media, live broadcasts, public service advertisements, television, apps, and websites for health education and promotion.

12.1.2 Strategies for hypertension-prone populations

The intervention strategy for hypertension-prone populations within the community primarily focuses on the early identification of factors that may lead to hypertension and implementing effective interventions to prevent the onset of the condition.

(1) Screening of hypertension-prone populations: the key risk factors for hypertension include normal-high blood pressure, overweight and obesity, excessive alcohol consumption, a family history of hypertension, high salt intake, and age over 55 years or being a postmenopausal woman.

(2) Blood pressure screening for hypertension-prone populations: it is recommended to measure blood pressure annually during health check-ups and to promote home blood pressure monitoring. Opportunities for blood pressure screening include routine medical visits, health check-ups, and annual exams. Screening locations may include clinical settings, health stations, public areas equipped with blood pressure monitoring devices, and homes. Remote monitoring using wearable devices and telemedicine can also be utilized.

(3) Blood pressure management strategy for hypertension-prone populations: (a) health check-ups: health check-ups should include general inquiries, measurements of height, weight, and blood pressure, urinalysis, and assessments of blood glucose, blood lipids, renal function, and electrocardiograms. (b) Risk factor control: similar to the strategies for the general population, individuals identified as prone to hypertension during health check-ups should undergo risk factor management and follow-up, with guidance on adopting a healthy lifestyle.

12.1.3 Strategies for high-risk cardiovascular disease populations

Cardiovascular disease has become the leading health risk affecting life expectancy in China. By conducting early screening and comprehensive interventions for high-risk cardiovascular populations, effective strategies for preventing and controlling cardiovascular disease can be developed, thereby reducing the incidence and mortality of cardiovascular events.

(1) Screening of high-risk cardiovascular disease populations: Individuals at high and very high risk are identified based on risk stratification.

(2) Early screening for high-risk cardiovascular disease populations: every six months, health check-ups should include general inquiries, measurements of height, weight, waist circumference, blood pressure, and heart rate, as well as tests for urinary protein/creatinine ratio, blood glucose, blood lipids, uric acid, renal function, and electrocardiograms.

(3) Controlling risk factors: different strategies for risk factor intervention should be formulated based on the characteristics of various populations. If necessary, antiplatelet therapy, anticoagulation therapy, or statin treatment should be administered to achieve specific target goals.

(4) Follow-up management and lifestyle guidance: high-risk individuals identified through screening should be managed with follow-up and provided with lifestyle guidance to reduce the incidence of cardiovascular events.

(5) Referral for changing conditions: if the patient's condition changes, they should be referred to a higher-level hospital for further evaluation and treatment.

12.2.1 Screening and registration of hypertensive patients

Hypertensive patients should be screened using the following methods, and health records should be established for newly diagnosed hypertensive patients, incorporating them into standardized management:

(1) Population screening: for permanent residents aged 35 years and older within the community, offer free blood pressure measurements once a year (measure blood pressure three times on different days if elevated).

(2) Outpatient screening: implement a “first-visit blood pressure measurement” system in primary healthcare institutions. For those with systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg who have not been definitively diagnosed with hypertension, measure blood pressure three times on different days or use ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM) after ruling out factors that may elevate blood pressure.

(3) Opportunistic screening: in primary healthcare institutions, take advantage of general outpatient services, health check-ups for the elderly, health record establishment, health education, and health consultation services to measure blood pressure.

(4) Screening for high-risk hypertension groups: it is recommended to measure blood pressure at least once every six months.

12.2.2 Evaluation and Follow-up of Newly Diagnosed Hypertensive Patients
